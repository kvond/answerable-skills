# vt-deck-authoring

*Public-facing. This skill ships with two companion documents: the rationale, and
the case file. Read the case file first. It will not take long and the rest of
this makes a different kind of sense afterward.*

---

## 0. What this skill will and will not do

**It will** carry the structure: the order of the questions, what has to be held
still while something else varies, the format, and the checks.

**It will not** decide what is worth teaching in your content, whether a student's
question should be followed, or when to abandon the plan. Those are yours. The
structure exists so there is room left for them.

[If you find yourself wanting the skill to make one of those calls, that is worth
noticing. It usually means the content decision underneath has not been made yet.]

---

## 1. Before any slide exists

Answer these three, in writing, before building.

**1. What is the critical aspect?** The one feature of this phenomenon a student
must be able to discern in order to understand it. Not the topic. Not the
standard. The thing that, if she cannot see it varying, leaves her unable to
reason about the rest.

**2. What varies, and what is held invariant?** A student discerns an aspect by
meeting cases that differ in that aspect and in nothing else. If your examples
differ in three ways, she cannot tell which difference matters, and she will
usually pick the wrong one.

**3. What does she already have to have discerned?** Cycles depend on each other.
Write down the dependency.

---

## 2. The 5 core questions

In this order, once for each critical aspect.

1. **Critical Aspect question.** Opens the aspect.
2. **Contrast Set.** Case A against Case B. Everything the same except one thing.
3. **Build a Rule.** The student completes a sentence stating a general rule.
4. **Pattern Break.** The case that strains the rule she just made.
5. **3-Tier Question.** Getting Started, Working On It, Mastery. With a word
   bank, offered as "use any, modify any, or use none."

The word "beat" is not used for these, anywhere.

**The rule on question 1, and it is where most cycles fail in the room.** An
aspect *is* a difference. A question that names a thing without naming a
difference cannot be answered, and the class goes quiet. "How are polar bears
adapted?" produces silence. "Which polar bear is more adapted, one with long fur
or short fur?" produces an answer, and *then* "why did you choose?" opens it.

Put the variation inside the question. Do not name the aspect on slide 1 and wait
for the Contrast Set on slide 2 to supply the difference.

Narrow first, then widen. This inverts the common failure, where a teacher asks
open, meets silence, and collapses into gap-fill — at which point the chance to
explore is gone.

---

## 3. The conditional slide types

Use only where the content calls for them. Forcing one on every cycle is the
commonest way this design gets flattened.

- **Continuity question.** A question with no answer to look up.
- **Stock-and-flow model.** Only where the aspect turns on a quantity that fills
  and drains.

---

## 4. The fusion decision

Separating aspects one at a time is not the end of the work. A student can
discern each aspect cleanly and still fail to coordinate them, which shows up on
a transfer task and gets misread as low effort.

Ask: **can two of my critical aspects operate at the same time in a real case?**

If yes, pick the structure that fits:

- **Stock-and-flow** — the aspects are a quantity and the rates that change it.
- **Compensatory pair** — two aspects vary inversely while the outcome stays the
  same, so neither alone predicts it. *Sea turtle and elephant: a hundred eggs
  with almost no survival, one calf with high survival, both populations steady.*
- **Conflict case** — one aspect points one way, another points the other, and
  the case cannot be resolved from either alone. *Legless snakes.*

If no, say so and record why. Some content has no coordination to do, and an
invented device wastes a slide and teaches a false relationship.

**The requirement, whichever you choose:** the case must be unanswerable from one
aspect. If a student can reach a defensible answer using only one, the slide has
not done fusion work.

---

## 5. The Concept Bank

Terms met across separate weeks, brought onto one page so relations between them
become visible. Nothing new is introduced. What changes is that a sequence becomes
a structure.

[The design point: co-presence is the precondition, not the achievement. A page
that lists the terms has achieved co-presence and left the relating to chance. A
page that asks the student to state how two of them connect does the work
outright. Same absence of testing pressure, different demand.]

---

## 6. The teacher note

One slide. Not speaker notes. Deletable in one action if you decide against it.

- The critical aspect, in plain words.
- What is held invariant, and the statement that the examples differ in one
  dimension on purpose.
- **What breaks if an example is substituted.**
- What this cycle assumes, and what depends on it.
- Which slide types appear, and which conditional ones were left out.

[Why this is a slide and not a manual entry: a manual is read after compliance is
already the frame, if it is read at all. A teacher adapting your deck in good
faith, not knowing that three examples differ in exactly one dimension by design,
will substitute a better example and destroy the contrast. The note protects the
design against helpful improvement.]

---

## 6b. Enactment — how the question runs in the room

Design is half of it. This is the other half, and it is the part a slide cannot
carry on its own.

1. Establish the difference.
2. Ask a multiple-choice question about it. Narrow the possibilities on purpose.
3. Pair talk.
4. Sequential hands-up vote, so you can see who votes for what.
5. Choose the next move from the vote pattern:
   - Split → two students who voted differently justify, then re-vote.
   - Low confidence → more pair talk, vote again on the original question.
   - Confident and wrong → several students justify, to surface what they saw.
6. Widen: why did you choose?

**On the vote, and this matters if your students are not there yet.** A hands-up
vote asks for two things at once: a commitment, and public exposure. In a room
where a wrong first answer is not yet safe, the exposure cost dominates and the
rational move is to look at nobody. Voting is a capability you build toward, not
a technique that works on demand.

Visibility runs on its own scale, from lowest to highest: written and private, to
a partner, read aloud by the teacher without a name, owned aloud, voted.

Complexity runs on a separate scale: one aspect, a rule, a strained rule, two
aspects coordinated, a counterfactual.

**Do not lock them together.** A high-complexity question is usually easier in a
low-visibility form, because the student needs thinking time more than she needs
an audience. A vote is low complexity at high visibility, which is why it lands
badly early. Raise visibility across the year; reset it low inside each cycle.

**Visibility belongs to the move, not to the lesson.** Each move tolerates a
different amount of exposure, and the sequence already ramps it — so visibility
resets low at the start of every cycle while the ceiling rises across the year.

| Move | Most exposure it will bear |
|---|---|
| Critical Aspect question, Contrast Set | Read aloud unattributed, or a vote |
| Build a Rule | A partner |
| Pattern Break, 3-Tier | Written and private |
| Continuity question | A partner, or aloud |
| Stock-and-flow, compensatory pair, conflict case | Group |
| What-if | Written and individual — it is your evidence |

Move 4 is the lowest for a reason: the student's own rule breaks there. Do not
make that happen in front of thirty people. If you cold call, this table is what
tells you where it is safe.

**Group work is a visibility reducer, not a rung.** It lets you run high
complexity at low exposure, which is why the fusion moves belong there. But a
group product tells you nothing about who did the coordinating, so it is a good
occasion for fusion and no evidence of it. A class can also enjoy group work
indefinitely without ever moving up, because a group is a place to be quiet in.

The written first answer with a rewrite is the low-visibility form of commitment.
It gets you the student's real thinking in rooms where a vote would produce
silence.

---

## 7. Format

4:3. Arial. Defined palette. White background. Clone slide types from a reference
build rather than styling by hand — a cloned slide keeps its sizes, colors, and
positions, so a deck built this way cannot drift.

---

## 8. Before you teach it

- Every example in a contrast set differs in exactly one dimension.
- The Pattern Break actually strains the rule the Build a Rule slide produces.
- The fusion case cannot be answered from one aspect.
- The teacher note names what breaks on substitution.
- Links resolve.

---

## 9. What this is for

Two aspects at once is the demand on a student. Content, this student, this
class, and the design at once is the demand on you, in about two seconds, several
times a period. The structure here is meant to be settled in advance so your
attention is free for the part no skill can specify.
