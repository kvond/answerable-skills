# Biology Education Research — What to Read, and Why

Positioned against the Answerable Teaching approach. Read in the order given.
Every entry says what it does for your argument, not just what it is.

---

## The journal

**CBE—Life Sciences Education** — https://www.lifescied.org/

Open access, quarterly, published by the American Society for Cell Biology with
HHMI funding. Peer-reviewed work on life science education at K-12,
undergraduate, and graduate levels. It publishes evaluation studies, basic and
applied research on biology teaching and learning, and essays on assessment,
student engagement, and curriculum innovation.

It is the flagship for biology education research and the one place a reviewer
will assume you have read. The archive is free, so the whole thing is available
without institutional access.

Frequent authors worth knowing by name: **Kimberly Tanner** (metacognition and
teaching structures), **Sara Brownell**, **Erin Dolan**.

[The scope caveat: LSE skews undergraduate. Your setting is high school. That is
a real gap in fit and it cuts both ways — it means less of the literature speaks
directly to your context, and it means a well-grounded high school argument has
somewhere to go.]

**Secondary journals**, in descending relevance to you:

- *Journal of Research in Science Teaching* (JRST) — the methodological standard
  for the field; K-12 heavy
- *Science Education* — theory-forward, more likely to engage your sociocultural
  commitments
- *Evolution: Education and Outreach* — open access, and directly relevant to
  the teleology problem
- *Journal of Biological Education* — practitioner-facing, international
- *International Journal of Science Education*

## 1. Read first — the conceptual obstacle you are already teaching against

**Ross Nehm**, on evolutionary reasoning. Search LSE and JRST for Nehm and for
the ACORNS assessment instrument. His work documents that students reason about
evolution with **cognitive biases that persist through instruction** — teleology
(traits appear because they are needed), essentialism (species have fixed
natures), and inconsistent reasoning across surface contexts, where the same
student explains gain of a trait correctly and loss of a trait incorrectly.

**Why this matters more than anything else on this list for you:** gain-versus-
loss inconsistency is precisely the Lezir case. Legless snakes is a loss item.
Nehm's finding is that students who handle gain items well fail loss items, which
means your conflict case is aimed at a documented and specific failure, not a
general one. That converts your design intuition into a claim with evidence
behind it.

[This also gives you an assessment instrument with published validity work, which
matters if you ever want to say the fusion slides did something. Whether ACORNS
suits your setting is a separate question — check whether the scoring burden is
realistic for one teacher with 150 students.]

**Also**: Gregory, "Understanding Natural Selection: Essential Concepts and Common
Misconceptions" (*Evolution: Education and Outreach*, 2009). Open access, and the
cleanest single inventory of what students get wrong.

## 2. The active learning evidence base — what your approach has to be more than

**Freeman et al. (2014), PNAS**, the meta-analysis of 225 studies showing active
learning outperforms lecture in STEM. This is the paper everyone cites.

**Read it to know its limits.** "Active learning" in that literature is a
heterogeneous category — clickers, group work, worksheets, anything not lecture.
It tells you that doing something beats doing nothing. It does not tell you which
variation makes an aspect discernible.

[The positioning line, and it is yours to make: Variation Theory specifies *what
has to vary and what has to be held still*, which the active learning literature
does not. A curriculum can be fully active and still show students three examples
differing in three ways, which teaches nothing about which difference matters.
That is a real contribution and this literature is what makes it visible as one.]

**Kimberly Tanner**, "Structure Matters: Twenty-One Teaching Strategies to Promote
Student Engagement and Cultivate Classroom Equity" (LSE, 2013). Directly relevant
to a Title I setting, and short.

## 3. Threshold concepts and conceptual change

**Meyer and Land** on threshold concepts — ideas that are troublesome,
irreversible, and integrative, and that gate everything after them. The
**integrative** criterion is the one to notice: a threshold concept is one that
makes previously separate things cohere.

[That is diachronic simultaneity under a different name, arrived at from a
different tradition. Worth citing precisely because the convergence strengthens
the claim — two literatures reaching the same structure independently.]

**Ross and colleagues** applied threshold concepts to biology specifically;
search LSE and *Journal of Biological Education*.

**Posner, Strike, Hewson, Gertzog (1982)** on conceptual change — the classic,
physics-derived. Read it to see why it does not transfer cleanly to biology: it
assumes a coherent prior theory to be dissatisfied with, and biology
misconceptions are often fragmentary rather than theoretical.

## 4. The biology-specific reform record

**Vision and Change in Undergraduate Biology Education** (AAAS, 2011). The
consensus reform document. Core concepts and core competencies.

**John Jungck** on problem-based biology and the "three P's" — problem posing,
problem solving, persuasion. This is the closest existing candidate for the
artifact you suspect biology never got, and you need to know it before claiming
the absence.

**HHMI BioInteractive** — https://www.biointeractive.org/ — free case-based
materials, data-driven activities. Practitioner resource rather than research,
but it is what your adopting teachers will already have.

[Check these three before writing "biology never got its reform artifact" as a
gap. A reviewer working in biology education will know this literature, and the
argument reads better engaging it than describing an absence. My own picture here
is not current enough to ground the claim for you.]

## 5. Variation Theory in science, not mathematics

Most Learning Study work is mathematics. Search for **Learning Study biology**,
and for **Ference Marton with Ming Fai Pang** on the economics and science
applications.

[This is where your originality probably concentrates. Variation Theory is
well-established in mathematics education and thin in biology, which is the
mirror of the gap you named. If the search comes back sparse, that sparseness is
itself citable.]

## 6. Dialogic, for the teacher-judgment half

- **Nystrand**, *Opening Dialogue* (1997) — authentic questions and uptake,
  measured at classroom level
- **Matusov** and the *Dialogic Pedagogy* journal — open access,
  https://dpj.pitt.edu/
- **Lampert** (2001) — mathematics, and still the nearest model for what you are
  doing with Lezir
- **Chin** on teacher questioning in science specifically; search JRST and
  *International Journal of Science Education*

## How to use this

You do not need to read all of it. Section 1 is the one that changes what you can
claim, because Nehm's gain-versus-loss finding gives your snake case an evidence
base it does not currently have.

Section 2 is what you read to know what you are arguing against.

Everything else can wait until a chapter needs it.
