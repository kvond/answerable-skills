# Question Types — Full Spec

This file covers each question type with purpose, format, examples, and anti-patterns. Read it whenever you're drafting the actual question text for an insert slide.

---

## 1. Critical Aspect Question

**Purpose:** direct student attention to the single most important feature of the concept. One aspect, one question.

**Format:** narrow, specific, concrete. Not "what do you see" — "which one." Not "tell me about X" — "what is the one thing that differs."

**Strong examples:**
- "Which parent passes this trait to the offspring?"
- "What part of the cell holds the genetic information?"
- "What is the one feature that is different here?"
- "Which molecule carries the code for the protein?"

**Anti-patterns:**
- Reveals the answer in the prompt ("The nucleus holds DNA. Where is it?")
- Asks for more than one feature at once ("What are the parts of the cell and what do they do?")
- Vague observation ("What do you notice about this cell?")

**Key rule:** do not reveal the critical aspect answer in the prompt. The student is supposed to form the idea themselves.

---

## 2. Contrast Set Question

**Purpose:** force comparison between two examples that differ only along the critical aspect. Everything else held fixed.

**Format:** side-by-side organisms (preferred) or cases. The question asks what differs or how they differ — not what each one is.

**Required:** both images visible simultaneously on the slide, or on the immediately previous content slide with a clear reference. Students should not have to hold both in their heads.

**Strong examples:**
- "What is different between these two parents?"
- "How is trait A different from trait B?"
- "Which image shows the chromosomes more clearly?"
- "What do the two cells share? What's different?"

**Anti-patterns:**
- Describing each organism separately ("Tell me about cell A. Now tell me about cell B.") — that's description, not contrast
- Comparing things that vary along more than one dimension (confounds the critical aspect)
- Using abstract diagrams when whole organisms would work — organisms bring the concept back to a whole, and students engage more

**Follow with a Build a Rule slide** (see section 3 below). The Build a Rule slide is the dedicated bridge from Contrast Set to Pattern Break — it asks the student to articulate the rule the contrast just made visible.

---

## 3. Build a Rule Question

**Purpose:** push reasoning from contrast toward the critical aspect. The student articulates the rule they've just seen demonstrated. This is the dedicated Generative Question (it used to live as an informal follow-on to the Contrast Set; it's now a slide of its own).

**Format:** an incomplete sentence with one structural blank. The student writes the completion. The sentence frame restates the critical aspect.

**Strong examples:**
- `"A grouping is useful when _______________________________________."` (after a contrast of two grouping schemes)
- `"DNA gives us better groupings than appearance because _______________________________________."` (after a contrast of appearance vs. DNA)
- `"Traits pass to offspring when _______________________________________."` (after a contrast of two crosses)
- `"Cells divide differently when _______________________________________."` (after a contrast of mitosis vs. meiosis)

**Anti-patterns:**
- More than one blank — the student should fill one rule, not several
- A blank so short it dictates the answer ("when ___" with three letters of underscore)
- A frame that doesn't restate the critical aspect — e.g., asking for a generic "rule about cells"
- A rule the contrast didn't actually show — the rule must come from what's on the previous slide

**Key rule:** the sentence frame should make the critical aspect explicit *as a structural blank*. The student is articulating what the contrast made visible — not generating a new idea from scratch.

---

## 4. Pattern Break Question

**Purpose:** once students form the easy version of the rule from the Contrast Set, hand them a case that breaks it. Forces them to rethink.

**The Pattern Break organism IS the contrast case that doesn't fit the simple rule.** It should emerge from the same concept space — not a disconnected counterexample. If the lesson doesn't have a natural pattern-break organism, flag it rather than forcing one.

**Format:** "If [simple rule], why [case that breaks it]?" The structure forces the student to hold the rule and the break simultaneously.

**Strong examples:**
- "If offspring get traits from parents, why aren't they exactly the same as either parent?"
- "If cells all have DNA, why can't we always see chromosomes?"
- "If a trait appears in the offspring, does that mean both parents showed it?"
- "If DNA codes for proteins, why do different cells in your body make different proteins?"

**Anti-patterns:**
- A case that doesn't actually break the rule (just extends it)
- A break from a rule the students haven't formed yet — the Pattern Break has to come AFTER the Contrast Set
- An answerable one-word question ("Is this offspring the same as the parent? Yes/No") — the question should open a real rethink

## 5. 3-Tier Concept Question

**Purpose:** closes the reasoning arc. The student has worked through Contrast Set → Build a Rule → Pattern Break and now articulates the critical aspect with the depth they've achieved. Three tiered prompts let students self-select where they are.

**This is the load-bearing slide for the downstream pipeline.** Workflow A detects it by pattern-matching on the three tier labels: `Getting Started`, `Working On It`, `Mastery`. Keep them exact.

**Required structure on the slide:**
- Main question (the critical aspect restated as a direct question)
- Three tier blocks: tier label + tier prompt
- Word bank (4–6 single-word terms in brackets, with 1–2 optional hint phrases)
- Collaboration reminder

**Writing the three tier prompts:**

| Tier | Cognitive depth | What the prompt asks |
|---|---|---|
| **Getting Started** | Concrete recall / example identification | Name an example, identify a case, point to a thing from the lesson. Answerable from memory of what they saw. |
| **Working On It** | Apply the rule, push back on the simple version | Why isn't the simple version of the rule enough? Why can two things look alike but be different? |
| **Mastery** | Explain the mechanism, causal reasoning required | What specifically makes the rule work? What does the grouping have to be based on? Connect cause to effect. |

**Strong examples (all three tiers on the same critical aspect):**

*Critical aspect: Why grouping by relationship is better than grouping by appearance*
- Getting Started: "Name two animals that look alike but DON'T belong in the same group. Why don't they?"
- Working On It: "Why isn't 'looks the same' enough to put two organisms in the same group?"
- Mastery: "If a grouping has to predict things about an organism (like how it raises its young), what specifically does that grouping have to be based on?"

*Critical aspect: Why DNA gives better classification than appearance*
- Getting Started: "Name an example where two animals look alike but aren't actually closely related."
- Working On It: "Why can two unrelated animals end up looking the same?"
- Mastery: "Appearance can lie because of convergent evolution. So what specifically about DNA makes it more reliable than what an organism looks like?"

**Anti-patterns:**
- Three different questions on three different topics — they must address the *same* critical aspect at different depths
- A tier hierarchy that doesn't actually escalate — "Working On It" can't be easier than "Getting Started"
- Telling the student which tier to attempt — let them choose
- A "Mastery" tier that asks for recall — Mastery must require explanation with mechanism
- Skipping the word bank — recent decks include it on every 3-Tier slide, even when the open-ended prompts could stand alone, because the bank is a scaffold for students who freeze

**Word bank format:**
- 4–6 single-word terms in square brackets with surrounding spaces, separated by triple spaces: `[ classify ]   [ trait ]   [ ancestor ]   [ predict ]   [ related ]`
- 1–2 optional hint phrases, lowercase, in their own brackets on a separate line: `[ shared ancestors give shared traits — appearance does not ]`
- Word bank label: `Word bank (use any, modify any, or use none):`

The "use any, modify any, or use none" framing is intentional — it tells students the bank is a scaffold, not a constraint.

## What-If Question

**Purpose:** a counterfactual that requires a committed position. Can't be factually wrong because the premise is hypothetical. Forces engagement even from resistant students.

**Three flavors:**

- **Organism what-if** — trace internal consequences. "What if a cell had no mitochondria?"
- **Ecology what-if** — trace outward consequences. "What if every plant in a forest made the same fruit?"
- **Evolution what-if** — trade-offs and variation. Highest value, especially priming the evolution unit. "What if DNA never made a copying mistake?"

**Format:** one scenario, one question. Student commits to a position and explains briefly. An unanswered what-if can still do productive work by staying open.

**Placement:** one per lesson where it fits. Outside the main four-step sequence — typically near the end of the lesson or on its own slide.

**Strong examples:**
- "What if DNA never made a copying mistake?" (evolution — stability vs. variation trade-off)
- "What if every offspring was identical to one parent?" (evolution/inheritance)
- "What if the nucleus disappeared from a cell?" (organism — trace consequences)

## Generative reasoning — how the arc bridges

The five-slide arc has two scaffolded generative moments where the student articulates the critical aspect, not just identifies it:

1. **Build a Rule** (after Contrast Set) — student articulates the rule the contrast just made visible. Sentence-frame format with one structural blank. See section 3 above.

2. **3-Tier Concept Question** (after Pattern Break, closing the arc) — student articulates the rule again, this time after seeing it break and reform. Three tiers let them choose the depth they can sustain. See section 5 above.

The two should be clearly related — the Build a Rule frame and the 3-Tier's main question should pull in the same direction so the student feels the through-line. Build a Rule asks them to *state* the rule; 3-Tier asks them to *explain why* it's the right rule.

## Strong vs. weak — the general test

A strong question:
- Forces confrontation with exactly where the simple rule breaks down
- Uses backward reasoning (function → structure, outcome → mechanism)
- Is answerable in 1–2 minutes at 7th-grade reading level
- Has a concrete scenario attached, not abstract terminology

A weak question:
- Asks for recall or vocabulary restatement
- Is vague ("What do you notice?", "Circle something important", "What stands out?")
- Uses fill-in-the-blank for high-rigor prompts (OK for the 3-Tier word bank and the Build a Rule sentence frame; not OK for Critical Aspect or Pattern Break)
- Could be answered without understanding the concept

When in doubt, draft the question, then ask: "Could a student answer this correctly by skimming vocabulary, without understanding?" If yes, rewrite it.
