Welcome

This is the teacher packet for Answerable Biology, a full year of high school biology built around a single question: what makes a student answerable for their own thinking? Everything here follows from that. The year is organized as twenty cycles rather than as chapters. In each cycle, students commit to a first answer before they are ready, and the work of the cycle is what changes that answer.

You do not need to read this packet front to back. Start where your question is. If you have never seen the system, begin with the next section. If you want to know what actually happens in a classroom on a given day, go to Teaching One Cycle, Day by Day — the shortest section here, and the one teachers reread most. If students arrive Monday, go straight to Setting Up Before You Teach and The First Week.

A few words appear throughout and are worth meeting now. A cycle is one core idea run all the way through, usually three class blocks rather than a week. A fiddle is a short hands-on task students can pick up at the start of class without being told how. A CORE deck is the slide deck that carries a cycle on its own; four cycles also offer an EXTEND deck, a second deck for classes with room to go further. The DRAFT slide is the one slide in each deck where a student's first and revised answers sit side by side; its speaker notes hold the AI coaching prompt students use on Day 3. Each of these is defined again, more fully, where it first matters.

The curriculum rests on variation theory: a student learns what a thing is by experiencing what changes about it against what stays the same. That idea shapes how every deck is sequenced, and the next section explains it properly rather than assuming it.

Two companions travel with this packet. The free guide Ten Questions That Make a Lesson Answerable is the one-page version of the thinking behind all of this, and it comes with every Answerable Biology product. The interactive tank-and-flow models the decks link to run in any browser, with nothing to install, at answerable-biology-models.netlify.app.

One promise about the tone of what follows. This packet is written to be usable at seven in the morning by a teacher who has four preps and a meeting. Where something is optional, it says so. Where you must supply your own material or link, it says that plainly rather than pretending the gap is not there.

What Answerable Biology Is Built Around

Answerable Biology is a full year of high school biology aligned to the Next Generation Science Standards, written as 20 flexible cycles running from Cycle 01, Classifying Organisms, through Cycle 20, the Human Impact Capstone. A cycle is one topic taught across roughly three class blocks — the unit you plan from.

Questions and critical aspects, not coverage

This is not a day-by-day pacing guide organized around coverage. It is organized around questions and around critical aspects, the term used throughout for the small number of distinctions a topic actually turns on. A lesson carries two or three of them, not eight facts — distinctions that, if a student fails to make them, leave everything downstream incoherent.

I find them where students reliably go wrong: biology compresses a great deal of reasoning into short statements, and misconceptions hide inside that compression. Cycle 02, Ecosystems and Feeding Relationships, has two: energy flow through trophic levels, meaning the feeding stages an ecosystem is organized into, and how two species affect each other.

The working discipline is two things per class. One lesson built around a critical aspect or two, where students must actually think rather than copy or guess from context, and one activity focused on that same aspect rather than on completing a procedure. It sounds like reduced ambition and is the opposite: two things done well take more teaching work than six done quickly, and students find it doable — the condition for genuine thought.

The arc every core idea runs through

Students repeatedly put their developing understanding into their own words, in writing, with no answer to copy and no blank to fill. Every core idea runs the same five-move arc; here it is as Cycle 02 runs it.

First answer. Before any teaching, students face a question they cannot look up: "The energy in your dinner started as sunlight. Grass catches sunlight, a cow eats the grass, you eat the cow. At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy, or something else? Why?" Students write their best current thinking on a response slide, a slide in their copy of the deck reserved for their writing. You are watching for whether a student has a working model, not for accuracy. Every thinking slide carries the same instruction: think first, discuss with a partner, then write.

Contrast. Two cases that differ only in the one thing you want noticed. Case A is grass, thousands of plants catching sunlight directly, roughly 10,000 calories stored across the field. Case B is red-tailed hawks, 10 to 15 of them sharing that same meadow. Then: "If the grass holds 10,000 calories, why can't the meadow feed 10,000 hawks?" This is the Montessori pink tower's logic — cubes identical except in size — carried into content a student cannot hold: vary only the critical aspect, hold everything else still, and it becomes perceptible as the only thing changing.

Rule. Students commit to a statement: "Finish this sentence as a rule: each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ______." The rule they build will be too simple — that is the point of asking.

Pattern-break. A pattern-break is a case the class's too-simple rule cannot account for, put to them directly: "Your rule said energy passes upward. But decomposers eat dead things at every level, dead grass, dead rabbits, dead hawks. Where does a decomposer fit, 10 percent from one level, or energy from all levels at once? Does your rule still hold?" Pattern-breaks interrupt the over-generalization a contrast reliably produces — not to catch students out; articulating a defense matters more than the answer.

Revised answer. On Day 3 students return to their first answer and revise it, right on the slide, underneath the original, with both left showing. Seeing your own thinking change is the point of keeping both.

"Keep going" questions run alongside the arc, stretching whoever is ready for more without moving them to a different task: "If energy is lost at every step and never comes back, why hasn't life run out of energy? There's no answer to look up." And the concept questions come in three tiers, labelled Getting Started, Working On It, and Mastery, with a word bank students may use, modify, or ignore — so every student answers the same question at the depth they can reach.

What each part of a lesson is for

Every element of a lesson has to answer the same test: does it make the critical aspect perceivable to these students, in this room, on this day?

Teacher explanation. I bring the conceptual material and decide before class which aspect the lesson will make perceivable. The slides carry enough text to support absent or reviewing students, but I scan each one and talk freely rather than reading it, highlighting with my marker and drawing where drawing helps. The questions are hard because the thinking is hard, not because the wording is.

Examples and contrasts. The move the design rests on. Grass beside hawks. A bee on a flower beside a mosquito on your arm, with the question "Both are two species interacting, so what is actually different?" A bat beside a bird in Cycle 01. Vary only the critical aspect, and students cannot help noticing it.

Demonstrations. Same test, done with your hands. A demonstration earns its slot when watching the thing happen is the fastest way to make the aspect perceivable, as with the fish and bromothymol blue demonstration in Cycle 06, or potato and peroxide in Cycle 10. If it is just the photogenic part of biology, it is theater.

Simulations. What makes a simulation work is that the variable students manipulate is the critical aspect itself, not that it is interactive or digital. In the Energy Tank Model, students drag the transfer efficiency slider and watch the upper tanks starve — not memorizing the 10 percent rule but handling an object that lets them feel it.

Matching and card activities. These establish a shared floor — the vocabulary, classifications, and structures everyone needs before the real work starts. The Cycle 01 cup sort, where students order the seven Linnaean levels of classification and then call the teacher over, and the Pacific Northwest species cards in the food web build are both this kind of work. It is completion work, honestly graded as completion; nothing downstream pretends it proves deep understanding.

Open activities. Most activities are for noticing, not output: students handle something, notice something, and record it. A worksheet fits meaning to a form the student did not make; a noticing activity gives the student something to make meaning of. The two-day food web build is the model case — students construct the web themselves, then do the 10 percent rule math of the energy pyramid step on the web they built. The Day 2 bobtail squid investigation is another: twenty minutes with a Hawaiian squid that houses glowing bacteria in a light organ, asking what the squid gets and what the bacteria get. Some activities deliberately allow productive messing around — poking at a thing until you have a feel for it is a legitimate phase of learning, not off-task time.

Labs. Labs are here, but done soberly. They cost time, setup, and class periods, so I run one when physically doing the thing matters for what students need to discern, not because the curriculum prescribed a lab this week. The directions are deliberately scant — I used to write labs as though they were being prepared for FDA approval; now, if someone does it wrong, that is something to think about rather than a failure to remediate. Complicated directions pull attention to the procedure instead of the aspect; complexity belongs in the lab's design, not its directions.

Revision and feedback

The finished student deck, first answer beside revised answer with both still showing, is the single artifact of the cycle — no exit tickets, no separate packet, no stack of papers to grade.

Revision runs from the deck's DRAFT slide, the one slide where the student assembles their answers; its speaker notes hold a three-step coaching prompt written for an AI assistant. On Day 3 the student opens that slide, copies the prompt, and runs it against their own first answer. The AI will not give the answer: it names one thing they got right, quoting their own words back, asks two questions about an idea they might have wrong or left out, names one distinction worth thinking about, and stops. The student then writes the revised answer under the first on the response slide, both staying visible.

For you, one batch step over the finished decks produces, for each student, a one-page Conceptual Growth Report — a printed summary of how their thinking changed, handed to them on paper — plus a class summary, which tells you who can trace energy through a web, who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed, and what the class is ready for next.

A person reads all of it before it reaches a student. I read every piece of feedback and adjust it as needed — not a small caveat but the whole architecture. Left to its defaults, an AI judges — right or wrong, corrected version, moving on — and calls a partial answer deficient. Your reading is where the human judgment lives, not quality control bolted on at the end. AI makes the work doable; it is not the doer.

Grading keeps two things apart. Completion means participation in thinking, and the students' standing rule, printed on every closing checklist, is "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." Proficiency means demonstrated understanding, read from the growth artifacts rather than the completion column. That separation makes first answers safe to write; students must expose incomplete thinking for this to work, and grading developing answers for correctness would stop them risking one. I remind them constantly that "because" is not an answer and "I don't know" is not an answer, but anything that shows me their thinking earns credit. Copying quietly loses its payoff: a first answer, a pattern-break defense, and a revision cannot be copied into existence, and the thought questions have no answers to look up.

Why lessons have breathing room

Pace alone often destroys depth: students asked to touch ideas briefly and move on before conceptual relationships stabilize are surviving procedurally, not thinking. So the three-block rhythm carries deliberate slack. Day 1 is the most teaching plus a shorter activity. Day 2 is shorter teaching plus the longer activity or the lab. Day 3 is finishing, returning to earlier answers, feedback, and revision.

Real classes lose minutes to attendance, late arrivals, passes, office calls, tech problems, drills, and the individual help that is the actual job. These lessons are designed for that classroom. The pacing keeps.

Why finishing at different times is planned for

Students do not finish at the same time; the design assumes that rather than fighting it.

Within a lesson, the three tiers and the "Keep going" questions let a fast thinker go deeper on the same question the room is answering. There is no separate track telegraphing who has been judged less capable. The question I ask is not who needs easier work, but what this student needs to be respected as a knower right now: for one student the Getting Started tier is the way in, not less work but the version that lets them be a knower; for the capable student who has turned off, the Mastery tier and the challenge slides say I see what they are capable of. Much of teaching is making the harder thing available without forcing it.

Across the year, every cycle ships a CORE deck, a complete path through the topic on its own, and four cycles add a second EXTEND deck for classes moving faster: Cycle 07 pairs Population Ecology with Populations and Succession, Cycle 12 pairs Reproduction and Meiosis with The Process of Meiosis, Cycle 15 pairs Genes and Chromosomes with Protein Synthesis, and Cycle 16 pairs Stem Cell Differentiation with Mutations and Gene Expression. Use one deck or both depending on pace: faster classes get relevant extension so slower classes can have the time they need.

Cycles 08 and 16 ask you to supply the CORE activity yourself, and the CORE activities in Cycles 07, 13, and 15 call for some materials you provide. NGSS performance expectation HS-LS2-6 currently rests on the Cycle 07 EXTEND deck rather than on a CORE deck.

Why Fiddles and challenges exist

A Fiddle is a simulation already open on screen when students walk in: the Energy Tank Model sliders, the BioMan food web game where you remove a species and watch what happens, or OneZoom's tree of life with a prompt like "find a pair that look almost the same but are far apart on the tree." It covers attendance, transitions, waiting, and early finisher time with something worth poking at — a real alternative to phones — and it is usually pointed at the aspect the day will teach, so the fiddling is a head start, not busywork.

Challenges are the standing invitation upward. "Break the web" asks students to remove one species, trace the arrows, and predict a year out, then asks whether any single species' removal would barely change the web. The "Keep going" stretches do the same job inside a question, as do the What-if slides, such as "What if decomposers suddenly disappeared from every ecosystem on Earth?" There is always something in the room that has met your strongest student — available, never forced.

Teaching One Cycle, Day by Day

This is what to do before, during, and after one cycle — one core idea run all the way through, from first answer to revised answer. Every cycle is built on variation theory, the idea that a student learns to see something by watching it vary while everything around it stays still, so the moves below are all versions of holding most things steady and changing one. Read this once, then keep it beside you while you teach your first cycle.

How long a cycle takes

Plan roughly a week to a week and a half; a faster class can finish in a week. The extra half week is not a recovery buffer — it is designed in, for classes that need time to formulate an idea, test it, and revise it.

Four cycles — 7, 12, 15, and 16 — ship with a second deck. Treat it as an option for pace and depth, not a required sequence: start with the CORE deck, which carries the cycle on its own, and add the second when this class has time and is ready for more.

Before the cycle

Read Slide 1 first — the teacher reference slide, carrying the cycle number, unit, block expectation, essential claim, standards, and objectives.

Then find the conceptual spine: what must students be able to tell apart by the end that they are likely to fuse together at the beginning? That distinction is what you teach toward.

Scan the deck for the pieces you will run: the first-answer slide, the deliberate contrasts, the rule-building question, the pattern-break (the case a class's rule cannot account for), the tiered concept questions, the final response slide, and the DRAFT slide — the one slide where the student assembles their answers — whose speaker notes carry the three-step coaching prompt for Day 3.

Choose CORE — the material every class does — before anything else. Add EXTEND or EXPLORE and CHALLENGE material only when it serves this class, not because there is time to fill.

Day 1: establish the whole and create a first answer

Start with the whole idea or phenomenon. Students need somewhere for details to land; without a whole to attach to, details arrive as clutter.

Ask for a first answer early, before students can copy the finished explanation back. A first answer — the student's best current thinking, written before any teaching — is allowed to be wrong; its job is to reveal the starting model.

Teach only the key terms students need to see the relationship under study. Vocabulary should support noticing, not replace it.

Use the first contrast to make one critical aspect stand out. In a feeding relationship cycle, two cases might hold the ecosystem stable while varying who eats whom, or where matter and energy enter. The goal is not memorized cases but a difference visible enough to reason with.

Ask students to finish a sentence as a rule; written down and owned by the class, a provisional rule gives everyone something to test later.

Begin the shorter activity or simulation if the class is ready. If not, stop there — the breathing room is part of the design, not a sign you have fallen behind.

Day 2: manipulate the idea

Teach a shorter segment today, then move students into the activity, lab, model, data set, comparison, or simulation where they vary something themselves and observe what changes.

Open activities and matching activities do different jobs. Matching establishes names, categories, and shared reference points. Open work lets students manipulate a relationship and notice the consequences — where the conceptual movement happens.

When the class has a simple rule in hand, give them the pattern-break: the case their rule cannot account for. It is not a trick; it makes the limits of the current rule impossible to ignore. Then ask the four questions that turn it into thinking: What changed? What stayed the same? Which part of our rule survives? What needs revision?

Use the Getting Started, Working On It, and Mastery versions of the concept questions to keep the destination shared while varying the entry point. Everyone answers the same question; the tiers only change where a student starts.

Keep a Fiddle — a short hands-on task a student can pick up without being told how — or a relevant challenge ready for dead space. Do not make faster students wait by handing everybody more of the same work.

Day 3: return, feedback, revise

Finish the activity if it still needs finishing; do not rush the conceptual return because the calendar says Day 3. Then send students back to their first answers, and have them read what they wrote before changing anything.

Now run the AI feedback step. Each student opens the deck's DRAFT slide, copies the three-step coaching prompt from its speaker notes, and runs it against their own first answer. The feedback should identify one thing that is right in the student's own words, ask two questions, and offer one distinction. It must not write the revised answer.

Give students time to write the revised answer under the first on the response slide, so both stay visible; held together, the two answers are the evidence of conceptual movement.

Teacher moves when students are stuck

  • Almost nothing on the page: lower the threshold. Ask for a difference, a choice between two cases, or the Getting Started version of the question.
  • A rule that is partly right: do not replace it. Give a contrasting case that tests the part that is too broad, and let the rule fail in front of them.
  • Right words, weak reasoning: ask for mechanism. What causes that? What changes next? What would you predict if one part changed?
  • Already strong: give a transfer question or a pattern-break, not more repetition of finished work.

What not to do

  • Do not turn the cycle into a minute-by-minute script. The design depends on your judgment about when this class is ready to move.
  • Do not grade every developing answer for correctness. The cycle works only if students expose incomplete thinking, and grading the exposure teaches them to stop.
  • Do not mistake completion for proficiency. Sorting cards correctly shows the sorting got done, nothing more.
  • Do not add directions simply because a guide can hold them. Complexity belongs in the design of the lesson, not in instructions you decipher while teaching.

The First Week

This section covers the classroom conditions that make Answerable Biology work — names, seating, visible group responsibilities, new-student placement, and classroom jobs — and how to establish them during the first week, while Cycle 01 (a cycle is the curriculum's multi-day lesson arc) is already running.

What the first week is for

Answerable Biology asks students to write down what they actually think before they know the answer, defend a rule they built themselves, and let everyone see their thinking change. None of that happens in a room where students feel anonymous, watched, or interchangeable. In Katherine's words, the goal is a room where "students feel less like objects of my teaching and more like workmates in a shared process. That is not a technique. It is a stance."

The first week builds the conditions for that stance. It is not a week of rules, syllabus review, or icebreakers: the biology starts immediately, with Cycle 01, Classifying Organisms, while the five structures below get established underneath it.

All of it serves one payoff, due at the sixth class meeting — early in week two on a daily schedule, later on the calendar on an alternating block. That is when Cycle 02, Ecosystems and Feeding Relationships, puts up its first response slide (the slide where each student writes a genuine first answer before instruction), asking "At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy, or something else? Why?" Students only expose incomplete thinking to a teacher who knows who they are, at a table where they feel safe, in a room they partly run.

What follows is the conditions. The lesson content is Cycle 01 in the Year Arc and the Ecology Unit Guide; day-to-day operation lives in the Master Agenda.

Meeting students at the door

Stand at the door every day of the first week with a printed roster on a clipboard — one per block, large font, space to write beside each name. Greet each student, ask what they want to be called (often not what the roster says), and mark the roster: preferred name, a pronunciation note, anything that attaches the face to the name. Keep using the roster until you no longer need it. That is the whole system. No name tents, no name games.

Doing it daily matters because this curriculum runs on students being known. Katherine crouches beside students, hunts a student down when a graded answer shows a problem, and invites individuals to the counter by name. The moment she calls the "got me" look, "the look of someone who has just found out that what they think actually matters to the person they are thinking with," cannot happen with a student whose name the teacher is still reaching for. A student still anonymous in week three has concluded their thinking is anonymous too, and will write first answers accordingly.

Make the recognition repeated and low pressure. Take attendance by looking at faces rather than calling names, checking the roster only when you miss; the Fiddle — the interactive simulation students open on arrival — covers this time, so attendance can be slow without the room idling. Use names while you circulate from day one; reading the name off the roster first and then using it is not a failure of the system, it is the system. On days three through five, greet students by name at the door before they speak, and let them correct you without ceremony, because being wrong cheaply and often is how names stick.

Never put a student on the spot to test your own recall, and never make name learning a class activity. The pressure belongs on you, not on them. A realistic target is every name without the roster by the end of week two; until then, the clipboard goes where you go.

Seating, and why friends may sit together

On day one, let students sit with their friends, in table clusters of three to five. Say so plainly: "Sit where you want. Sit with people you know." This is a deliberate design decision, not a soft opening you will walk back, for three reasons.

First, the curriculum's opening demand is intellectual risk. Every thinking slide says to think first, discuss with a partner, then write. A student seated next to a stranger has a partner in name only; a student next to a friend has someone they will actually say a half-formed idea to. Existing peer relationships are immediate social safety, the kind that otherwise takes six weeks of assigned groupings to rebuild.

Second, the off-task worry assumes the room has nothing for them to do. This room does: from the first day there is a Fiddle open on arrival, a first answer due, and a Cycle 01 cup sort the table completes together and then calls the teacher over to check. Friend groups go off task when they are under-tasked — a workload problem, not a seating problem. The one incident in Katherine's own account points the same way: the student joking with friends was a strong student the work had not yet met, and her response was harder work, not a new seat.

Third, purposeful structure turns a friend group into a working group, and the next two sections, task index cards and classroom jobs, are that structure. Without them, friend seating is just proximity. With them, the friendship helps the work along instead of competing with it.

You still own the map: clusters of three to five, arranged so you can circulate to every table, with students choosing within that structure. Students who know no one get placed by you, warmly and by name, into a cluster you judge can absorb them — the same move described below for new students. A cluster that shows over several weeks it cannot work gets rearranged as a working decision, stated as one: "This group isn't getting the build done. I'm moving two of you." When the tasks are real, that stays rare.

Task index cards

At each table cluster, keep a small set of index cards, one per responsibility a group task needs: each card names a job in one line, with two or three specifics under it. When a group task starts, the cards get dealt at the table, and whoever holds a card owns that job for the task. Laminate the cards or stand them in a card holder, leave them at the table permanently, and rotate them by dealing — no negotiation phase, no teacher assigning roles table by table.

A working set for the ecology arc's group activities: Materials gets the card set, cups, or sheets from the supply station and returns them sorted and complete, with the Master Supply System defining complete for each activity. Recorder holds the group's written answers and checks that everyone's noticing gets in, not just the fastest talker's. Checker confirms the group actually agrees before it calls the teacher over; in the Cycle 01 cup sort, the deck's own instruction, "When you think you have the correct order, call your teacher over," is this card's moment. Spokesperson speaks first for the group when the teacher arrives or the class debriefs.

Deal only as many cards as the task needs. For the two-day food web build in Cycle 02, the same four cards cover it: Materials owns the Pacific Northwest species cards, Recorder owns the web sheet, Checker owns whether the arrows point the right way before the group does the pyramid math, and Spokesperson answers when you stop at the table.

The cards make responsibility visible. Without them, every group task quietly assigns itself: the conscientious student does everything, the others watch, and friend seating takes the blame. With them, the workload distributes by card rather than by habit, and you can address the role instead of fishing for a volunteer: "Who's Checker here? Are you ready for me?" A card also gives a quieter student a legitimate, bounded way to matter — holding Materials is participation with no public performance, the same low-stakes entry that annotation offers in the lessons.

Teach the cards once, during the first group task of Cycle 01, in under two minutes: this is what the cards are, deal them, go. They explain themselves in use; do not open with a lecture about roles.

New students

Students who arrive after the first week — and in many schools they arrive all year — do not get pointed at an empty chair. Placement is a teaching decision you make with what you now know about every cluster. The move has three parts, all small.

  1. Choose the cluster on purpose. You are matching a person you barely know to groups you know well, so pick by the group: which cluster has room socially, works reliably, and contains at least one student likely to pull a newcomer in? Avoid both the struggling cluster that needs no new variable and the tight foursome with no room. Adjust later as you learn who the new student is.
  2. Introduce them to the cluster, not to the class. A whole-class introduction is high pressure and carries no information. At the table it is cheap and real: "This is Marisol. She's joining your group. You're two days into the food web build. Recorder, catch her up on what your web says so far, and deal her a card next task." The task cards do half the welcome — a newcomer holding the Materials card has a role and a reason to talk within ten minutes of sitting down.
  3. Close the content gap with the deck, not with a packet. The slides deliberately carry enough text to support absent students, and the same feature covers students who were never here. The new student reads the cycle's slides, writes first answers where the class already has them, and enters the revision loop at the next Day 3, the day each cycle turns to revising first answers. Their Conceptual Growth Report — the printed report you generate and hand to each student, tracking growth across cycles — starts from their first cycle, and because growth is measured from a student's own baseline, joining in November carries no built-in penalty.

Within their first week, run the classroom job resume with them individually, five minutes or as homework, and slot them into the jobs system. A new student with a job stops being new faster than anything an icebreaker ever accomplished.

Classroom jobs

The charter for the whole jobs system is Katherine's, written about students already working above the class: "I sometimes offer what looks like a task that helps the classroom, organizing or making materials, but is really a way of meeting the level they are working at. I tell them: you can say no. You can stop when you want. The agency stays theirs."

So: jobs are real work the room actually needs, offered as a way of respecting what a student can do, and every job may be refused or quit. A job a student cannot decline is a chore with a nicer name, and it produces compliance rather than ownership.

Jobs matter because this curriculum's endgame is a class that owns its knowledge. As Katherine puts it, "By the middle of the year the class has its own corpus, cases, questions, and small disagreements no other classroom will have. The knowledge is theirs, partly, because they made it." A room cannot belong to students intellectually while belonging to the teacher operationally. Jobs are how the room becomes partly theirs: materials come out because a student got them, simulations are up because a student opened them, decks went in because a student swept for missing submissions. Students take care of what is theirs, and they think harder in a room they take care of.

Here are jobs the room actually needs; draw from these and add your own.

  • Materials manager. Pulls and returns activity materials — card sets, cups, lab trays — against the Master Supply System lists, and flags anything missing or broken.
  • Tech lead. Gets the day's Fiddle link up on the arrival devices, and is the first responder for "my Chromebook won't" before it becomes the teacher's problem.
  • Lab steward. Resets stations after labs and stations days, and owns the "clean up your station" line of the lab safety rules.
  • Submission checker. At the end of the block, sweeps for classmates who have not submitted their deck. Both answers present is the whole check — completion, not judging anyone's answers.
  • Print and prep. Makes copies, cuts card sets, laminates. This is the organizing and making materials work from Katherine's own example, and the natural offer for a student working above the class.
  • Greeter and catch-up. Meets a returning absent student or a new student, and points them to the slides and to where the class is.
  • Board and agenda. Posts the day's agenda line from the Master Agenda and updates the cycle number.

Use jobs instead of icebreakers. On day two or three, hand out the job resume described in the next section; the following day, cluster students by the jobs they applied for and let them talk. Icebreaker talk is content-free performance, the kind of forced public sharing the rest of the curriculum is careful never to demand. Job cluster talk is purposeful — students who chose the same kind of work already have something in common, and the conversation has an object (what the tech lead actually does when the cart wifi dies) instead of a script. Students meet each other over shared work, the way adults do, and the room gets staffed in the process.

Run the job interviews in clusters, as a 15 to 20 minute block late in week one.

  1. Cluster students by job interest from the resumes — everyone who listed tech lead in one corner, materials in another. Students who listed several pick the one they care most about.
  2. Give each cluster that job's card (the one-line description and its specifics) plus one question: "What would make someone good at this, and which of you wants it when?"
  3. Circulate and interview briefly: who has done something like this, who wants it first, who would rather be backup. This is the best ten minutes of reading your students you will get all week — who volunteers, who defers, who organizes the group without being asked.
  4. Post the roster the next day: job, holder, backup. Most jobs rotate, but a student who loves a job and does it well can keep it. The point is ownership, not fairness in turn taking.

Say the house rules to the class once: every job is real, nothing invented to keep hands busy; every job is refusable — you can say no, and you can stop when you want; no job is a reward or a punishment; jobs are never graded. The agency stays theirs.

The classroom job resume

The resume is a one-page application, ten minutes, done in class during the first week. Its stated purpose is staffing the jobs. Its real yield is larger: it is the first piece of writing in which the student tells you who they are — who fixes phones, who has a job after school, who writes three words, who fills the page. Read every one. Use it when you place jobs, and when you crouch beside a student in week two already knowing something true about them.

Keep it exactly as low stakes as the curriculum's first answers: completion credit only, never graded for writing, and say so on the form. Sentence fragments are fine. A student may apply for nothing, and the form still asks what they are interested in — the same rule the response slides run on: anything showing real thinking counts.

Here is a form you can copy. It fits on one page; adjust the job list to match your own roster of jobs.

Setting Up Before You Teach

The practical onboarding: what to open, decide, configure, and test, in the order you will do it, so you reach Day 1 without rebuilding the curriculum first.

How the course is organized before you touch anything

The Master Agenda is the navigation for the whole year: one entry per cycle, where a cycle is a three-block unit of teaching built around a single central question. Each entry names the cycle's slide deck, links its one authoritative copy on the shared cloud drive, and lists the materials, the supplies, and the fiddle — the short hands-on or on-screen task students do as they arrive, before the lesson begins.

The Unit Agendas do the same job for one unit at a time, at teaching depth rather than at a glance. Everything else — simulations, activity guides, card set PDFs, videos — is linked from the deck slides themselves, so the deck you project already carries its own links to whatever you need next.

Do not download the whole drive and reorganize it into your own folder system. The curriculum is already organized and the agendas are the index into it. Duplicating everything breaks the links, cuts you off from later corrections, and costs days you do not have. You copy only two things: the student deck, because students must type into their own copy, and whatever your school requires you to hold inside your learning management system. Everything else opens from a link.

During class you have two things open: the deck, and the Master Agenda or Unit Agenda for the cycle you are teaching.

If you have only the free unit — Cycle 02 on ecosystems and feeding relationships, plus the Free Unit Guide, the Year Arc, and Start Here — this setup still works. Do steps 1, 3, 5 through 7, and 11 through 12 for that single cycle — an honest trial of the whole system. The Free Unit Guide stands in for the Unit Agenda in step 4 and carries the Cycle 02 supply list from step 10. What has to be done before Day 1, and what can wait

Step 1. Read Start Here

Read Start Here once, front to back. It is the ten-minute version of how this curriculum thinks: what critical aspects are; how a cycle moves from a student's first answer through a contrast, a rule, a pattern-break (a case that breaks the rule students just built), and finally a revision; why completion and proficiency are kept separate; and how the AI loop stays under your control. Do not skip past it to get at the files.

Step 2. Choose how the cycles fit your bell schedule

Open Schedule Options and find your bell schedule among the three worked examples: alternating A and B blocks (the rhythm the decks were built on), daily ninety-minute blocks, or forty-five-minute periods. Memorize the rule now: cut at a day divider or immediately after a response slide, and keep the rule-building and its pattern-break together in the same sitting.

This is not a pacing commitment; the cycles are flexible by design, with slack built into the year on purpose.

Step 3. Skim the Year Arc

Skim the whole Year Arc once to see the four arcs the year is built from — Ecology in Cycles 01 through 07, Cells in 08 through 11, Genetics in 12 through 16, and Evolution and Human Impact in 17 through 20 — plus the year-at-a-glance table. Read only Cycles 01 and 02 closely, since those come first.

Four cycles ship with two decks instead of one: Cycle 07 (Population Ecology plus Populations & Succession), Cycle 12 (Reproduction & Meiosis plus The Process of Meiosis), Cycle 15 (Genes & Chromosomes plus Protein Synthesis), and Cycle 16 (Stem Cell Differentiation plus Mutations & Gene Expression). Step 4. Open the Unit Agenda for your first unit

If an agenda for your unit does not exist yet, copy the blank Unit Agenda Template from the Unit Guides folder and fill it in from the deck's teacher reference slide and the matching Year Arc entry — about twenty minutes. For Cycles 01 through 07, the Ecology Unit Guide carries the full teaching depth behind the agenda: read its Cycle 01 and 02 sections before Day 1 if you have time, and the rest as those cycles arrive.

Step 5. Decide whether to teach from links or copy materials into your LMS

Decide before you teach; the copying itself can happen later. The default, and the recommendation, is to teach from the linked files on the drive and copy into your LMS only when forced.

Some things stay linked and are never copied: the agendas, the simulations and tank models, the activity guides and card set PDFs (print straight from the original file), the videos, and the deck you project directly from the drive.

One thing must be copied no matter what: the student deck. Each student types a first answer and later a revised answer into the response slides, and runs the coaching prompt from the speaker notes of the deck's DRAFT slide — the one slide where they assemble their answers — so every student needs their own editable copy of the cycle's deck — one copy per student, not one per teacher.

Copy more only if your school requires all student-facing material inside the LMS. If so, copy the deck and printed handouts for the current cycle only, cycle by cycle, never the whole drive at once. Either way, the Master Agenda stays your index.

Step 6. Set up student access

Do this before Cycle 1; it repeats at the start of each cycle (step 12). Each student needs three things.

  1. Their own editable copy of the current deck, with speaker notes visible. The decks are PowerPoint files. If your school runs Google, upload the deck to Drive and open it with Google Slides; the speaker notes — where the DRAFT slide's coaching prompt lives — survive the conversion, though spot-check the DRAFT slide's notes afterward. If your school runs Office 365, distribute the PowerPoint file directly.
  2. A place to turn the finished deck in. Katherine's own pattern is a class folder, and the student checklist inside the decks reads "I turned the deck in to the folder." A Schoology assignment folder, a Google Classroom assignment, or a shared Drive folder all do the job.
  3. Device access to the linked simulations: BioMan, the tank models at answerable-biology-models.netlify.app, OneZoom, and PhET. Nothing to install — all run in a browser. Whether your school's content filter allows them is exactly what step 11 checks.

Step 7. Create the cycle's assignments

Do this for Cycle 1 before you teach, and for later cycles as you go. Katherine's working pattern, visible on the decks' own teacher slides, is two assignments per cycle.

  1. The deck submission, one per cycle, due at the end of Day 3. This is the completion artifact: the first answer beside the revised answer, both still showing.
  2. The graded activity, where the cycle has one: Cycle 02 the food web build, Cycle 03 the station checksheet, Cycle 05 the lab sheet. Each is linked from the deck's teacher slide and the Unit Agenda.

For Cycle 02, create the deck submission assignment and, if you intend to grade the food web build, an assignment for that as well. Then paste both links into the link lines of your Unit Agenda before Day 1 — the one part of setup no document can do on your behalf.

While you are here, set one standing policy: notes are due before students leave the room. Katherine's reason is printed to students in the decks themselves. "When you wait until later: you remember less, you're more likely to copy, you miss the learning that the notes are designed to create." Set due dates to the class period, not midnight.

Step 8. Set up your gradebook categories

Have this done before your first Day 3. The system's default is a single gradebook category: completion. As the decks tell students, "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." Proficiency is read from the Growth Reports and the class summary, not entered as a column of its own.

Keep the grades heavy enough to matter. In Katherine's words, "I am not going to pretend ninth-graders will engage purely for intrinsic reasons, and they need the grades to navigate the system."

If your school requires a graded proficiency entry, the document Assessment: Completion, Copying, and Proficiency in the Assessment folder lays out both ways: Option A is completion only, as designed; Option B adds proficiency for schools that require it. Set your category weights before entering the first Cycle 1 completion marks, so you are not reweighting the gradebook mid-quarter.

Step 9. Settle the AI feedback workflow

Settle this before your first Day 3 as well; only one of its two sides needs work from you.

The student side is already built. The three-step coaching prompt lives in the speaker notes of the deck's DRAFT slide, the one slide where the student assembles their answers. On Day 3 a student opens the DRAFT slide, copies the prompt from its notes, runs it against their own first answer, then writes the revised answer under the first on the response slide, so both stay visible. Every time, the AI quotes one thing back to the student, asks two questions, names one distinction, and never gives the answer. What you must settle is which AI tool students will paste it into, under your school's AI policy — no tool is named here, deliberately. As Katherine puts it, "I am not describing a product... The teacher side is the batch step. Once the decks come in, one batch step produces a one-page Conceptual Growth Report for each student — a printed report you hand to the student on paper — and a class summary for you. The full workflow, prompts, and feedback register live in the AI Feedback folder, in a document called AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth. Read it before your first Day 3, not after.

Whichever tools you use, one thing does not bend: you read every report before it goes to a student. "That is not a small caveat. It is the whole architecture."

Putting the Course in Your LMS

This section wires Answerable Biology into whatever learning management system your school uses, so the submission pattern stays intact, every link points at one authoritative copy, your teaching documents stay out of student view, and you never have to guess which version of a deck a student opened.

It assumes you have read Start Here and have the Master Agenda open. Read the first two parts, which apply everywhere, then only the part for your platform. Katherine teaches on Schoology, so that section comes from her own practice; the Google Classroom, Canvas, and Moodle sections from what those platforms can do.

The one pattern your platform has to preserve

One workflow cannot bend, because everything downstream depends on it: each student works in their own copy of the cycle's slide deck (a cycle is the course's repeating three-block lesson unit), and each student submits that deck.

Notes are due before class ends; the standing policy slide tells students directly: "The purpose of the notes is to help your brain process today's lesson while it is still fresh... For that reason, students who are present complete and submit their notes before leaving class." On Day 3 the student opens the deck's DRAFT slide — the one slide where they assemble their answers — copies the three-step coaching prompt from its speaker notes, runs it against their own first answer, and writes the revised answer under the first one on the response slide, leaving both showing. That annotated deck is the single artifact the whole system turns on: the evidence of completion ("Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade.") and the input your batch run reads to produce each student's Conceptual Growth Report — the printed report you generate and hand back on paper — and your class summary. Even the Cycle 20 capstone proposal travels this way: "turn it in with your deck to the class folder — no separate upload."

Your platform has exactly four jobs each cycle.

  1. Get an editable copy of the deck to every student, speaker notes intact, since the DRAFT slide's notes carry the revision prompt.
  2. Give students one obvious place to submit that same file, and collect it before the bell on each block.
  3. Hand you back the finished decks in a single folder for the Day 3 batch run.
  4. Post completion — both answer areas filled — and nothing about the quality of the answers.

Anything beyond those four jobs is convenience. Anything that breaks one breaks the system, and three things commonly do: a submission flow that flattens the deck into a PDF, a copy step that strips the speaker notes, and a folder structure that scatters the decks so you cannot collect them.

One hard rule follows: never distribute or collect decks as PDFs. A PDF has no speaker notes and therefore no revision prompt, no editable answer boxes, and no way to be revised on Day 3. The deck must travel as a live slide file — PowerPoint or Google Slides — from one end of the cycle to the other.

Rules that hold on every platform

Keep one authoritative copy of every file. Each deck and each printable — the Pacific Northwest species cards, the station checksheets, the activity guides — lives in exactly one place: your cloud drive. Your platform holds links to that place, or copies the platform itself generated for students, never a second uploaded original. The Master Agenda names the authoritative file for each cycle, and the deck filename is the name of record. Once the same deck exists as an upload inside three assignments, you have three things to keep updated and no way to know which one a student opened.

Let the links live on the deck slides. The decks already carry the links to the simulations, games, and readings. Do not rebuild that link list inside your platform; you would then maintain it in two places that drift apart. Each cycle your platform needs only the place students get the deck and submit it, and at most the address of the fiddle — the warm-up task in the tab students open on arrival.

Keep teacher materials out of the student view entirely. The Master Agenda, the Unit Agendas, the Ecology Unit Guide, the Master Supply System, Standards and Alignment, your batch prompts, and your class summaries are your operating documents: keep them in your drive and bookmark them. The Master Agenda is meant to be open during class, and a pinned tab serves that better than any page inside a course shell. Conceptual Growth Reports are private to each student — printed and handed back, or delivered one at a time, never posted anywhere shared.

One deliberate exception: Slide 1 of each deck is the teacher reference — cycle, essential claim, NGSS performance expectations, objectives — and it travels with every student copy by design. It is reference, not secret, and it supports absent students and administrators who ask. You simply never project it.

Organize by cycle, in arc order. Mirror the Master Agenda's four arcs: Ecology in Cycles 01–07, Cells in 08–11, Genetics in 12–16, Evolution and Human Impact in 17–20. Make one folder, topic, module, or section per cycle, named exactly as the Master Agenda names it — for instance "Cycle 02 — Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships" — so your platform, drive, and agendas all use the same names. Do not organize by week or date; the cycles are flexible, your calendar will move, and a date-built structure must be rebuilt every time it does.

Name student copies so the batch run can read them. The batch step reads students' names from filenames, so pick one naming convention — "P3 - Rivera - Cycle 02" works well — state it in the assignment directions in Cycle 01 or 02, and never change it. Platforms that copy the deck per student automatically (Google Classroom) put the name on for you; platforms that rely on file upload (Schoology, Moodle) need you to enforce the convention early.

Update between cycles, never underneath students mid-cycle. Once students have copied a deck, their copies have separated from yours; replacing the original changes nothing for them and leaves two versions in circulation. When an updated deck ships — a product update or your own edit — swap it in the gap between cycles, replacing the contents of the authoritative file behind the same link where possible (in Google Drive, Manage versions on the PowerPoint file keeps the link stable); otherwise relink once, in the one place the link lives. If you must fix something mid-cycle, such as a broken simulation link or a typo, announce it and fix it going forward rather than recalling student copies.

If you ever find yourself holding Cycle02_final, Cycle02_final2, and Cycle02_USE_THIS, the cause is always the same: the file was uploaded into the platform instead of linked from the drive. Return to one authoritative copy and links to it.

If you use Schoology

Course structure. Deck distribution. Canvas has no built-in per-student copy of a slide file. Privacy. Unpublished Files and Modules are invisible to students, but the safer rule still holds: keep your teaching materials out of the course shell entirely.

If you use Moodle

Structure. Set the course format to Topics and make one section per cycle, in arc order. Inside each section put a URL resource pointing at the place students get the deck, an Assignment activity set to accept file submissions including PowerPoint files, and a URL resource for the fiddle.

Privacy. Moodle has hidden sections and stealth activities, but their behavior varies with theme and version, so do not rely on them; keep teacher materials out of the course instead.

Moodle's default upload size limit is often smaller than a media-heavy deck, and some decks run to between forty-five and fifty-seven slides. Check the course's maximum upload size before Cycle 01, and raise it or move to the Drive link route if student decks start bouncing.

If you use something else

For Brightspace, Teams for Education, itslearning, or anything similar, do not look for feature parity with the platforms above. Ask four questions and wire up whatever answers you get.

  1. Can it put an editable copy of a slide file, speaker notes intact, into each student's hands — either a native per-student copy or the view-only link with the Make a copy routine?
  2. Can students submit that file to one obvious place, once per cycle?
  3. Can you retrieve all the submissions in bulk, into one folder?
  4. Can you post an entry that records completion only?

If all four answers are yes, the platform works: organize by cycle, keep one authoritative copy of every file, keep teaching materials out of the course, and apply the general rules above. If any answer is no, the adaptations below fill the gap. Never let a platform limitation push you into distributing PDFs — that is the one substitution that quietly kills the revision loop.

Adapting to your devices and your printer

The decks were built assuming one device per student — the simulations, the links, and the deck submission all depend on it. Everything below the first case is an adaptation, and each names what it costs you.

One device per student — the designed environment. The fiddle tab is open when students arrive, each student has their own deck copy, the simulations run at desks, and notes are written and submitted before the bell. Build the submission step into the last five minutes of every block; the decks' own Think, Write, Submit checklist slide is that buffer made visible.

Shared devices, one per pair or per station. The simulations can be shared; the artifact cannot. Run the contrasts, simulations, and activities on the shared device, and stagger the writing so each student types their own first answer into their own deck during the activity rotation — while one partner works the energy tank model sliders, the other writes, then they swap. If the rotation is too tight, first answers can go on paper and be typed in at the student's next device turn, which must happen before Day 3, because the revision prompt needs the first answer in the deck. The cost is transcription time and a weaker guarantee that everything is submitted before the bell.

No student devices at all. Print the response slides as an answer sheet for the cycle: the question, a box for the first answer, and a box for the revised answer, both staying visible — exactly the slide layout on paper. Simulations and games become things you project while students call the moves, along the lines of "drag the transfer efficiency down — what happened to the hawks' tank?" The fiddles shift to the no-technology ones every cycle already carries, such as the Cycle 01 cup sort, the DOG SAT puzzle, and the PTC strips. For the Day 3 revision without student AI access, run the revision prompt yourself on a projected sample of anonymous first answers so the room sees the move, then have students revise on paper from the contrast, the pattern-break (the deliberately surprising case the lesson turns on), and the discussion. The batch run can read photographed or scanned sheets, though expect friction with handwriting. The cost is the single artifact and the individual AI pass per student — the heaviest adaptation of the set.

Plenty of printing available. Print freely wherever the design calls for print anyway: the species card sets, the station cards, the sort cards, the lab sheets, the Cycle 20 argument template, and above all the Conceptual Growth Reports, where print is the designed artifact — "something a ninth grader holds, keeps, and shows." Laminate the reusable sets — cards and checksheets — once. The Master Supply System tracks which items you print once and which per class.

Limited printing. When pages are rationed: Growth Reports come first, the one print the whole system is built around. Card sets and station cards come second — printed once, laminated, reused all year and next. Lab sheets come third, and several can go digital: linked Google Docs already exist for the Cycle 03 station checksheet and the Cycle 06 trace worksheet, or they can be projected with the answers in the deck. Everything else stays on screen. Never spend your ration duplicating something the deck already shows.

Students can work at home. Home access is for recovering from an absence, not for shifting work into the evening. The slides "carry a fair amount of text — to support students who were absent or are reviewing," so an absent student opens their deck copy at home, reads the slides, writes their first answers, and picks up the revision step on return. The notes-due policy still stands for students who are present — it exists to "help your brain process today's lesson while it is still fresh," and moving the writing home defeats that.

Class time only, with no home access. The system already works this way; everything is designed to finish inside the block. Protect two things: the submission buffer at the end of every block — devices leave students' hands after they have submitted, not before — and a seat for catching up, since an absent student's first move on returning is their own deck copy during the fiddle window and activity time, not a packet to take home.

Wiring Up Assignments and the Gradebook

Everything in this section is optional. It records one teacher's working process so you can take whichever parts fit your school. Katherine uses an AI assistant — specifically Claude — for the repetitive work of setting up each cycle (the curriculum's repeating unit: one core idea run across about three class blocks) in Schoology and posting completion to the gradebook. You can run every cycle by hand if you prefer.

Her own caveat governs the whole of it: "I am not describing a product, and I am not recommending a particular tool. I am describing what one teacher does when she points a general-purpose machine at the specific, stubborn production problem that was eating her presence. The principle is portable; the particular way I do it probably is not."

The principle is the part that transfers. LMS setup, grade entry, and messages home are production work rather than teaching — the "roughly forty percent" of the job that used to eat her hours. As she puts it, "AI helps make the work doable. It is not the doer." The assistant drafts, organizes, and carries the pattern from cycle to cycle; she decides, verifies, and publishes. Every word that reaches a student or the gradebook passes through her first.

How the pieces are arranged

Katherine uses two pieces of the same tool, doing different jobs.

  • The desktop application, where the thinking and drafting happen: assignment text, the teacher batch over the submitted decks, the Growth Reports and class summaries, the completion spreadsheet, and email drafts.
  • The browser extension, where the work inside web pages happens. It operates inside her own logged-in Chrome session, so it can work in Schoology, Drive, and Gmail as her, on screen, while she watches. Her submission flow already runs this way: student decks upload from Drive through the extension into a class folder, which is why the student checklist in every deck reads "I turned the deck in to the folder."

Screen recordings of this process will cover: handing a cycle's context to the assistant, creating the cycle's two Schoology assignments, creating and naming the class submission folders, the student-side upload from Drive, getting the completion spreadsheet into the Schoology gradebook along with the roster check that precedes it, pasting the assignment links back into the Unit Agenda and the teacher slides, the email draft flow, distributing editable deck copies to students, and setting up the gradebook category for completion-only grading.

What you give the assistant to work from

An assistant is only as reliable as what it is looking at, so each session starts from the operating documents, never from memory.

  • The current Unit Agenda, with the Master Agenda as the year's index. Together they say what this cycle is, which activities are graded, and which link lines are still empty.
  • The deck's own teacher slide, the authoritative record for the cycle. It carries the cycle name and length — "Cycle 2 · Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships — Unit 1 · 3 blocks" — plus the essential claim, the objectives, and the line naming the graded activity.
  • Your class roster. The rule is explicit: "Class membership from the Dashboard (source of truth), never from filename/folder."
  • The folder of submitted decks, for the batch run: one annotated deck per student, first and revised answers both on the response slides.
  • The standing conventions that must not drift: the gradebook records completion only, the column name matches the one already there, and the report standards hold — including the rule that no coding vocabulary ever reaches a student.

What you ask it to do

The teacher batch. After Day 3, the submitted decks run through the batch prompt, which extracts each student's first and revised answers, runs the integrity check on the first answer only, and drafts one Conceptual Growth Report per student — the printed, teacher-checked report tracing a student's progression through each critical aspect of the cycle's concept — plus the class summary and the completion spreadsheet. The full flow and the prompts live in AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth. This is by far the largest job the assistant carries.

The completion spreadsheet, which Katherine describes as "the only thing that reaches Schoology." One row per student, one column per lesson; completion means both answer areas are filled.

Folder organization. A class folder for each cycle receives the finished decks as students upload them from Drive.

Assignment work in Schoology. The pattern from the teacher slides: one assignment per graded activity, plus the deck submission assignment. The assistant can drive the Schoology screens through the extension while you watch, or just draft the text for you to paste in — a matter of preference.

Individual student email, prepared as drafts. For a flagged first answer, a missing submission, or a follow-up on a report, the assistant prepares a Gmail draft. Drafts only; nothing sends until she has read it and pressed send herself. Email is not her first instinct anyway: "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it."

Drafting the assignments themselves

Katherine's account of the assistant's output, in her words: "It drafts lesson outlines I then adjust. It generates slides I scan and talk over rather than read. It drafts feedback in a register I have specified. It writes IEP-accessible versions of an assessment I would otherwise have written by hand at midnight. None of that is teaching." Applied to assignments, that comes down to four things.

The assignment text is drafted from the deck's own language and then adjusted. A Cycle 02 deck submission description does not need composing from nothing: the deck already says what students must do, in the Think, Write, Submit sequence and the line "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." It also says why notes are due before the bell: "When you wait until later: you remember less, you're more likely to copy, you miss the learning that the notes are designed to create." The assistant assembles; you adjust.

The due dates carry the policy. The deck submission is due at the end of Day 3; notes are due before students leave the room, so the due time is the class period, not midnight.

Accessible versions sit on the same conceptual bar. Katherine's stance: "I differentiate not by lowering the conceptual bar but by removing the linguistic obstacles." The assistant drafts the accessible version; you check that the bar did not move.

The links get pasted back. The decks carry empty link placeholders on their teacher slides, and the Unit Agenda carries matching link lines. Creating the assignment produces the address; that address goes back into both places.

Why this helps with the repetitive part of setup

The administrative loop has the same shape twenty times a year: open the next Unit Agenda, create the cycle's two assignments, paste the links in, distribute student deck copies, print from the Master Supply System list, and queue the fiddle tab (a fiddle is a short hands-on task students pick up at the start of class without being told how). None of it is intellectually hard, and all of it is the "invisible labor that consumes teacher attention" the book describes. The reason for handing it off: "if AI can help organize materials, generate drafts, process repetitive workflows, create feedback structures, or assist with planning, then something more important may become possible: the teacher can remain cognitively available inside the classroom itself."

Three kinds of repetition get absorbed. First, the sameness from cycle to cycle: Cycle 03's setup is Cycle 02's with new names, new links, and one new graded activity — the station checksheet instead of the food web build. The assistant carries the pattern; you supply the differences. Second, the batch, the reports, and the spreadsheet — the whole of the processing after Day 3 that used to be "a stack of papers to grade." Third, drafting that repeats with small variations: assignment descriptions, parent versions, accessible versions, individual emails.

One boundary keeps this honest. The time freed is for presence, not more production: "Standing at the counter, crouching beside a stuck student, running the dialogic register for more of the room." A setup workflow that generates more setup has missed the point.

How assignments connect back to the Unit Agenda

The Unit Agenda is the page you teach from, and so the index of record for what exists in your LMS. It names the cycle's assignments before they exist — for Cycle 02, the deck submission due at the end of Day 3, and the food web build if you are grading it. The agenda is the instruction; the platform is where it gets carried out.

Once the assignments exist, their addresses go back into the agenda's link lines, and the rule from the setup section still holds: fill the link lines before Day 1. Working links are the difference between teaching from one page and hunting through your platform mid-class.

The Master Agenda holds the whole year and the Year Arc explains what each cycle is for. An assistant can read all three, which lets it draft the next cycle's assignments without being briefed from scratch. Even so, the agenda, not the platform, stays the authority on what a cycle contains.

How completion and proficiency get recorded

Here the rule is firm and fully spelled out.

The gradebook records completion and nothing else. In Katherine's words, "Schoology records completion: both answer areas filled = done. Never quality." One column per cycle deck, one row per student. The statement students see, on the closing checklist of every deck, is "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade."

Proficiency lives only in the Growth Report, and in the class summary and rollups on your side. "All proficiency/growth lives in the Growth Report, never the gradebook." Nothing the assistant produces for the gradebook carries a judgment about quality.

Integrity flags never post as flags. A flagged first answer appears as an item for you to review — visible to you, resolvable by you. It needs to be, because "detection misfires, on ESL students and atypical writers especially." The completion entry stays held while the review is pending; nothing posts on its own. An AI-written first answer is never failed automatically: it gets interrogated with the student so they improve it, and it is rated on the same proficiency scale alongside the revision so the change is visible. Because the gradebook is completion-only, "a wrong flag costs a cycle's feedback, not a grade."

Grades stay heavy enough to matter. "I am not going to pretend ninth-graders will engage purely for intrinsic reasons, and they need the grades to navigate the system." Recording completion only is not the same as doing away with grades.

If your school requires a graded proficiency column, the document Assessment: Completion, Copying, and Proficiency lays out that variation as Option B.

What you read yourself before anything publishes

This part does not bend, and it is the architecture rather than a caveat attached to it: "I read every piece of it before it goes to a student, and I adjust it as needed... The AI's draft is exactly that: a draft, produced fast, for me to check against what I actually know about the student and the concept... The reading is not quality control bolted on. It is the place the human judgment lives."

You read every one of the following, in full, before it goes anywhere.

  • Every Growth Report, before you print it and hand it back. The dangerous failure is persuasive error, not clumsy writing: an AI "can affirm reasoning that is actually mistaken, or invent a 'real part' of a student's answer that is not there, in fluent, encouraging prose." Only someone who knows the student and the concept catches that.
  • Every assignment before it publishes: the text, the link, the due date, and, where it applies, that the accessible version kept the conceptual bar where it was.
  • The completion spreadsheet, checked against the dashboard roster before it touches the gradebook — one row per student, completion marks that match what was actually submitted.
  • Every email draft, before you press send. The assistant never sends.
  • Every integrity flag, resolved by you personally: restore the false positives, act on the real ones.

Timing stays yours as well. The batch runs when you run it, "on-demand only... never at session start." Nothing in this workflow fires on a schedule of its own.

One last thing: your instructions about register and report standards "are not set once; they have to be reasserted, and checked, against the grain of what the tool wants to do." So spot-check every batch against your standards, not only the first one.

A checklist for each cycle

Before Day 1:

  • Open the Unit Agenda; give the assistant the agenda and the deck's teacher slide.
  • Create the two assignments: the deck submission, due at the end of Day 3, and the graded activity if the cycle has one — for Cycle 02, the food web build. Read the assignment text before you publish it.
  • Paste both addresses into the agenda's link lines. The links need to be live before Day 1.
  • Distribute student deck copies, and confirm the submission folder is ready.

After Day 3:

  • Run the batch over the submitted decks — when you choose to, not automatically.
  • Resolve every integrity flag yourself.
  • Read every Growth Report, every word, against what you know about the student. Adjust it. Then print it.
  • Check the completion spreadsheet against the dashboard roster, and enter completion only. No proficiency, no quality, no flags in the gradebook.
  • Read and adjust any student email drafts. You press send.
  • Skim the class summary for the next instructional contrast. That read is what the whole pipeline exists for.

The standing rule over all of it: if a word is going to reach a student or the gradebook, you have read it first.

The Year, Cycle by Cycle

The year is twenty cycles, not a day-by-day pacing guide. A cycle is one core idea run all the way through in three blocks: the first carries the most teaching, the second gives most of its time to a lab or long activity, and the third is where students see their own thinking change. A typical block schedule gives you eighty-five to ninety blocks a year; twenty cycles at three blocks each uses about sixty. The remaining twenty-five or so are not slack: they are room for reteaching, the lab that runs long, review, and the holidays, testing windows, weather days, assemblies, and fire drills no one can schedule in advance.

Each cycle carries two or three critical aspects and never more. A critical aspect is a single discernment a student must make for anything downstream to hold together, not a fact to memorize. In Katherine's words: "A lesson, in my experience, can carry two or three critical aspects that actually shape understanding. Not eight facts. Two or three discernments, each of which, if a student fails to make it, leaves everything downstream incoherent." Every aspect runs the same arc: students commit a first answer on a response slide before any teaching, work a deliberate contrast between two cases that differ in exactly one way, build a rule in their own words, then meet a pattern break, a case chosen to break that rule because it was too simple. On day three each student opens the deck's DRAFT slide, the one slide where they assemble their answers, copies the three-step coaching prompt from its speaker notes, runs it against their own first answer, and writes the revised answer directly under the first on the response slide. Both stay visible, because the change between them is the point, and the finished deck is the single artifact that feeds the Conceptual Growth Report you generate and print for each student, plus your class summary. Completion means both answers are present; the growth shows up in the report, not the grade.

Two more things before the cycles. First, they are threaded: most decks close with a "Keep going" question whose answer is the next cycle, so the leftover atoms of Cycle 03 become the carbon of Cycle 04, the stored sugar of Cycle 05 becomes the respiration of Cycle 06, and the two separated forests of Cycle 18 become the speciation of Cycle 19. If you compress or reorder, protect that chain. Second, pace differences are handled inside the structure, not by rushing. The full set is twenty-four decks across the twenty cycles: Cycles 07, 12, 15, and 16 each carry a CORE deck plus an EXTEND deck, chosen by pace: the core deck alone for a class that needs more time, the second added when a class is ready for more. Every other cycle ships one deck. Within every deck the "Keep going" stretches, mastery tiers, and optional third-day challenges give fast thinkers somewhere to go without new logistics, and every cycle opens with a Fiddle, a simulation or puzzle left open on arrival to cover attendance, transitions, and early finishers. Katherine's own summary of why this works: "Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly. And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now."

Cycles 01 through 07 form the ecology arc, and Cycle 02 is the free demonstration unit. The interactive tank and flow models named throughout the year, which let students move a slider and watch a quantity fill, drain, or level off, all live at answerable-biology-models.netlify.app.

Cycle 01 — Classifying Organisms

This cycle opens the ecology arc and asks what makes one way of grouping organisms better than another, and why DNA groups more accurately than appearance. The first arc contrasts grouping the same eight animals by habitat against grouping by relatedness, asking which lets you predict something, and breaks on bats and birds, since "flying animals" is not a real group at all. The second contrasts appearance with DNA, where the dolphin sorts with cows rather than sharks, and breaks on the triple convergence of shark, dolphin, and ichthyosaur. Cladogram practice and the HIV forensic case, in which a phylogenetic tree was built for a jury, close the deck.

Activities: a Linnaean seven-level cup sort at lab stations, where students call you over when they think the order is right, the "What's in a Drop?" pond dip lab, and the HHMI click and learn on DNA and phylogeny with its answer document. Students commit and revise on two response slides, one on what makes a grouping useful and one on DNA versus appearance. The Fiddle is a hunt on the OneZoom tree of life: find a pair that look almost the same but sit far apart, and a pair that look very different but are close cousins. The cladogram slides suit early finishers, and the extension is a what-if about an organism whose DNA matches none of the three domains: add a fourth domain, or is the whole tree wrong?

You will want labeled cups for the seven classification levels at each station, printed pond dip lab sheets, the HHMI answer documents, and devices. Outside resources: OneZoom, the pond dip guide, the HIV case article, and a three domains video.

Cycle 02 — Ecosystems and Feeding Relationships

This is the free demonstration unit; its claim is that organisms in an ecosystem are connected by the transfer of matter and energy through feeding relationships. The first arc traces dinner back to sunlight, contrasting a meadow holding ten thousand calories of grass with the ten or fifteen hawks it can feed, then breaks the tidy energy-passes-upward rule with decomposers, which eat dead things at every level. The second contrasts bees with flowers against mosquitoes with humans, and breaks the who-benefits rule with orchids that mimic female bees and get pollinated without paying any nectar.

Students build a food web over two days using Pacific Northwest species cards, including the energy pyramid math that follows the ten percent rule, and run a roughly twenty-minute observational investigation on the bobtail squid on day two. The two response slides cover energy flow and symbiosis type, and day three adds a what-if, what happens if every decomposer vanishes, with the decomposers video watched first. The extension is "Break the web": remove one species, trace the arrows, predict the ecosystem one year and one generation out, then ask whether any single species could be removed with barely any change. The closing question, why life has not run out of energy, opens Cycles 03 through 05.

You will need printed Pacific Northwest species card sets, food web sheets, markers for the arrows, and devices. The Pacific Northwest species PDF and the bobtail squid investigation are online. For the Fiddle, students remove one species from a food web in the BioMan ecology games and watch what happens, or play with the energy tank model sliders.

Cycle 03 — Energy Flow and Trophic Pyramids

The claim: energy decreases at each step up a food chain because most is lost as heat, with only about ten percent passed on. The first arc puts a blade of grass and a rabbit in the same sunlight, since you cannot stand in the sun and make lunch, then breaks the sunlight rule with hydrothermal vent bacteria, producers with no sun at all. That break redefines a producer as the doorway for new energy into the community, not something green. The second follows a thousand units of energy from grass to rabbit to fox, roughly a thousand to a hundred to ten, and breaks on the food chain with eight or nine levels that cannot exist.

Day one runs Energy Flow Stations with a station checksheet: a card match, a build-the-pyramid task, and a photosynthesis versus respiration sort. Day two is Do the Energy Math, pushing ten thousand units through four levels and asking what that says about why top predators are rare. Students revise on two response slides, on producers as the entry point and on the ninety percent loss. The third-day challenge gives two ecosystems with the same plant energy where only one supports a top predator, and asks for an argument from the ten percent rule: "Write a short claim with reasoning, not just an answer, but WHY." The closing question about where the atoms go leads into Cycle 04.

Print the station cards for terms, organisms, and processes, the station checksheets, and pyramid sheets. For fiddling and early finishers: the Feed the Dingo ecosystem balancing game, the energy tank model sliders, and a concept question practice deck.

Cycle 04 — Cycles of Matter: The Carbon Cycle

If energy is used once and gone, why isn't matter? The claim is that carbon, unlike energy, is not lost but cycles continuously between the air, living things, and the Earth. The first arc pits Maya's soil against Dev's air as explanations of where a plant's body comes from, lets the weighed soil settle it, then breaks on the mushroom growing on a dead log with no sunlight and no carbon dioxide drawn from the air. The second watches the same campfire twice, once following the energy, gone for good, and once following the carbon, back inside a leaf next spring, and breaks on coal and oil, where carbon sits almost frozen for millions of years.

Day one is a video investigation with a predict-first protocol built around Veritasium's "Where do trees get their mass?". Day two is a journey through the carbon cycle, a dice and stations game in which each student plays a single carbon atom and logs its moves. The two response slides cover where wood comes from and cycling against one-way flow. The challenge after the checklist asks students to design one realistic change in how we travel, build, or grow food that would help the carbon cycle balance, watching UCAR's material on how humans change the carbon cycle first. The closing question, how a leaf grabs a gas and locks it into wood, opens Cycle 05.

You need dice, printed station cards, and student logs for the game. The Fiddle is the carbon tank model sliders plus a skim of the carbon hiding places diagram.

Cycle 05 — Photosynthesis

How does a plant turn light into food, and what is its body actually built from? The claim: photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in glucose, and the plant builds its body from air and water, not soil. The first arc contrasts two jars of waterweed, one in bright light producing bubbles and sugar and one in a dark closet producing nothing, and breaks on the gardener's plant, watered faithfully but kept dark, which pales and dies. The second runs Van Helmont's willow, five pounds of tree becoming a hundred and sixty-nine while two hundred pounds of soil lose about two ounces over five years, and breaks on the redwood built mostly out of a gas you can barely feel.

This is the year's first real lab. Day one is the leaf disk float lab with a no-carbon-dioxide control, debriefed on day two once students see the floating disks are full of oxygen the plant just made. Day two also carries the Van Helmont handout and a build-a-sugar-molecule task with model kits, six carbon dioxide plus six water yielding glucose and six oxygen, where every atom must be used. The two response slides cover what photosynthesis does with the sun's energy and where a plant's material comes from. The stretch prediction moves the lamp closer and asks what happens to the float rate and why, followed by a round of the BioMan photosynthesis and respiration game. The closing question, how a living thing gets the energy back out of the sugar, opens Cycle 06.

The deck introduces lab safety in plain terms here: today's lab uses only water, baking soda, and spinach, all safe, but you build the habits now for the later labs that use real chemicals.

Gather baking soda, water, dish soap, fresh spinach, a hole punch, needleless plastic syringes, clear cups, a lamp, timers, measuring spoons, and goggles, plus molecular model kits and the printed lab and Van Helmont handouts. Counting floated disks each minute is built-in waiting-time work, and the photosynthesis rate model sliders serve as the Fiddle.

Cycle 06 — Cellular Respiration and Fermentation

How do your cells cash in the energy in food, and what happens when the oxygen runs out? The claim: cells release the energy stored in glucose, with fermentation as the backup pathway when oxygen is gone. The first arc contrasts a marshmallow burning in a single flash with a cell releasing the same energy in tiny steps trapped as ATP, and breaks on fever, where respiration leaks straight to heat like a tiny flame. The second contrasts roughly thirty-four ATP with oxygen against roughly two without, and breaks on Raquel sprinting at mile twenty: fermentation adds no ATP at all, so what is it for?

A teacher demonstration runs quietly through the whole block: a fish in bromothymol blue turns the water from blue through green to yellow while the control cup stays blue, and the deck's instruction is to hold back the words "respiration" and "carbon dioxide" and let students infer. Day one uses the "Releasing the Energy in Food" trace worksheet. Day two is the yeast balloon fermentation lab with a no-sugar control, where the balloon seals out oxygen and carbon dioxide inflates it anyway, proof the yeast made energy without air. The two response slides cover the fast burn against the slow release, and the oxygen payoff. The extension is the NAD+ recycling puzzle: run glycolysis on paper, discover both NAD+ carriers stuck full as NADH, and work out why the cell cannot continue without fermentation even though fermentation yields no ATP.

Cycle 07 — Population Ecology and Succession

Why does a population's size level off instead of growing forever, and how does a community rebuild after disturbance? This is a two-deck cycle: "Population Ecology" is the core deck, "Populations & Succession" the extend. The core deck opens on a video game bellringer, the short arrival question that starts the block, asking what stops enemies from filling the screen, then sets the anchoring phenomenon: the Aleutian Islands collapse, and what happened to the sea otters? The first arc contrasts the J curve of exponential growth with the S curve of logistic growth and breaks on the human population's apparently endless J. The second builds limiting factors and carrying capacity and breaks on sea otters crashing below carrying capacity. The extend deck runs three short arcs: bare ground moving toward a climax community, broken by the place that burns again and again; pioneer species making soil on bare rock; and the old farm field that looks bare but is secondary succession, and therefore fast, rather than primary and slow.

Students work the anchoring phenomena research assignment and the "Feeding Relationships in the Pacific Northwest" activity, which uses the same species PDF as Cycle 02, and model a common population sampling technique with quadrats. The core deck has two response slides, on growth shapes and carrying capacity; the extend deck has three, on climax communities, pioneers, and primary against secondary succession. The what-ifs: a rabbit island with unlimited resources and no predators, what shape the curve takes and whether it could last, and a world with no pioneer species at all.

You need the research assignment handouts, the Pacific Northwest feeding cards, and quadrat materials. The live world population clock makes a self-running Fiddle, and the video game spawn bellringer works cold. The Rebirth of Yellowstone is the follow-up video.

Cycle 08 — Cells and Organelles

What makes a cell the smallest living unit, and how do its parts work together as a system? This single deck builds cell theory from the history of cell discovery. Students contrast a bacterium with an animal cell, where the real difference turns out to be where the DNA lives, then a paramecium doing every job in one cell with an alligator that splits those jobs among trillions. The deck closes on the microbiome: you are covered in prokaryotic microbes, and most of them are good for you. Its two response slides cover prokaryote against eukaryote and one cell against many.

The bellringer, asking for the smallest living piece of you, works cold. Cell coloring practice is ideal for attendance and transitions; print the coloring sheets and have colored pencils on hand. If your class is ready to go organelle by organelle, structure fitting job, rough against smooth endoplasmic reticulum, plant cell against animal cell, a graphic organizer plus the coloring sheets support a teacher-led factory tour, backed by the organelle review video. The what-ifs: is a cell with ribosomes and DNA but no nucleus a prokaryote or a eukaryote, and what happens to an animal cell that suddenly loses all its mitochondria? The follow-up video is the microbiome video.

Cycle 09 — Cell Membrane and Transport

The claim: the cell membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell, keeping it stable, which is homeostasis. The first arc contrasts two broken cells, a sealed bag and an open net, both dead for opposite reasons, and breaks on insulin and GLUT4: the cell takes in more glucose by adding more doors, no pushing involved. The second contrasts a small oily molecule slipping through the oily middle of the bilayer with a charged salt ion being pushed away, and breaks on water, which is polar and crosses fast anyway through aquaporins.

Day one is the Screen Door Sort: students sort oxygen, water, a large protein, a salt ion, a virus, and carbon dioxide into what crosses freely and what is blocked or needs help. Day two is Build a Bilayer, drawing and labeling eight phospholipids arranged against water. The two response slides ask what a living membrane does and why the oily middle decides. The extension pairs an oil and water mini lab with the PhET membrane channels simulation run as predict then test. The closing question, what controls how fast a cell breaks down its food, opens Cycle 10.

Print the Screen Door Sort cards, have paper for the bilayer drawings, and bring oil, water, and a clear container. For the Fiddle, students play a round or two of Cell Defense, stopping invaders at the membrane, or work the osmosis model sliders; the sort cards re-sort easily for early finishers.

Cycle 10 — Enzymes

How can an enzyme speed up a reaction and come out untouched? The claim: enzymes speed up specific chemical reactions without being used up. The cycle opens on the potato and peroxide demonstration, foam on the potato and nothing on the plate, with the instruction not to say "enzyme" or "catalase" yet. The first arc contrasts peroxide alone with peroxide plus catalase, and breaks when you scoop the same catalase into fresh peroxide and it fizzes again, meaning it was never consumed. The second builds lock and key specificity and breaks on denaturing, where the key never changed but the lock did. Day two calls the demonstration back with a boiled potato that will not foam.

Day one includes the activation energy hill, a marble rolled over a cardboard hill that you then lower. Day two runs a lock and key match with paper cutouts, one with a deliberately warped active site, and the temperature and pH lab, graphing catalase rate against both variables with the optimum and the zero point marked. The two response slides ask what the enzyme changes and does not change, and why shape decides. The third-day extension is the liver catalase lab, reusing the same piece of liver in fresh peroxide and varying the amount. The closing question, what happens if the DNA copying enzyme works too fast or copies wrong, opens Cycles 11 and 14.

You will need three percent hydrogen peroxide, raw potato, liver if you want a stronger reaction, a white plate, goggles, paper towels, a marble and cardboard, scissors and paper, temperature baths and pH solutions, a boiled potato for the callback, and graph paper. The lock and key cutouts double as transition puzzles, and the Amoeba Sisters' three-minute enzymes video works as a Fiddle alongside the BioMan enzyme simulation.

Cycle 11 — The Cell Cycle and Cancer

The claim: cells divide on a tightly regulated cycle, and cancer is what happens when that regulation fails. The cycle starts from a scraped knee and where the brand new skin cells came from, then moves to the checkpoints and the questions they ask: am I big enough, did I copy all my DNA, is any of my DNA damaged? The first arc contrasts a clean-copy cell passing a checkpoint with a cell carrying ultraviolet damage being stopped, and breaks on a broken checkpoint that waves the damage through. The second contrasts benign and malignant tumors and breaks on the survival paradox: cancer cells are extremely good at surviving, so how do cells that survive so well end up harming the whole body?

Day one is Be the Checkpoint, where groups read condition cards and rule pass, pause and repair, or self destruct, writing one reason for every stop. Day two is a cancer case study in which students find where a checkpoint failed and mark the tumor benign or malignant, with the HHMI cell cycle and cancer click and learn as the digital option, showing how a broken p53 lets damaged cells divide. The two response slides ask what a checkpoint reads and why winning cells sicken the body. The extension is a what-if about chemotherapy: why does attacking fast-dividing cells hit cancer hardest and also make hair fall out?

Print the condition cards and the case study handout. The checkpoint cards re-deal as a quick game, and the cell cycle model sliders and a click-through cell division simulation serve as the Fiddle. The closing question, what would have to change to make cells that are half and different, opens Cycle 12.

Cycle 12 — Reproduction and Meiosis

Why does making a sperm or an egg require halving and reshuffling the chromosomes? This is a two-deck cycle. The core deck, "The Process of Meiosis," works through meiosis I and meiosis II and what happens to chromosome number, with the pop bead meiosis lab as its hands-on work. The extend deck, "Reproduction & Meiosis," claims that meiosis halves the chromosome number and reshuffles genes, so sexual reproduction yields offspring that differ genetically from their parents and from one another, and works three focus points: asexual reproduction means one parent and identical clones, sexual reproduction means two parents and genetic variety, and mitosis drives the first while meiosis drives the second.

In the extend deck the first arc contrasts one parent producing identical clones with two parents producing varied offspring, and breaks on the Komodo dragon, which produces offspring from a single parent. The second contrasts mitosis making identical cells with meiosis making gametes carrying mixed genes, and breaks on siblings: same two parents, so why don't siblings look identical? The content runs through binary fission, budding, fragmentation, gametes, fertilization, and crossing over, arriving at more than eight million possible combinations. Two response slides ask about identical against varied offspring and about which kind of division is at work and why.

The extend deck runs on reading, video, and simulation rather than materials: Snurfle Meiosis as warm-up, video fact checks, a Quizlet vocabulary set that works as a transition drill, and the two-to-the-twenty-third-power counting challenge for fast finishers, landing at roughly eight and a half million gamete combinations. The other extension asks what happens when a species that reproduces by cloning meets a virus. For the core deck you will need pop beads.

Cycle 13 — Mendelian Genetics and Punnett Squares

This unit dismantles the two confusions students arrive with, that dominant means stronger and that common means dominant, and puts probability in their hands. The deck works two critical aspects in its own words. The first is inherited against acquired: a trait is a characteristic passed from parent to offspring through genes, while a scar, a suntan, or pierced ears are gained during life and are not inherited. The second is that common does not mean dominant, since dominant means an allele masks the other allele, not that the trait is the most widespread. The first arc contrasts eye color with a scar and breaks on identical twins after one summer, same genes but one scarred and tanned. The second contrasts a straight hairline, common but recessive, with polydactyly, rare but dominant at about one in a thousand. The deck carries two response slides.

Students also learn Punnett squares here as probability tools: the square shows the chance of each outcome, so three-to-one is a probability, not a guarantee, and a carrier holds a recessive allele hidden by a dominant one, which is how two healthy parents can have an affected child and a disease can seem to skip a generation. A real family of four offspring that all came out purple makes the chance-not-promise point vividly.

Students take a traits inventory and pool individual results into class data. For hands-on probability work you can add a PTC taste test, a fast opener students engage with immediately, drag-and-drop sorters, and practice crosses using rabbits, roses, or Among Us crewmates. The Fiddle is Tour of the Basics, with Snurfle Meiosis and Genetics as a second option for early finishers. The what-ifs imagine a world where children are born with their parents' skills and scars, and a world with no hidden alleles at all, asking whether carriers would still exist. Print the traits inventory sheets and have PTC test paper strips ready if you run the taste test. Supporting videos: an introduction and two comparison videos, here and here.

Cycle 14 — DNA Structure and Replication

The claim: DNA's double helix and base pairing are exactly what let it be copied faithfully before a cell divides. The first arc contrasts copying a strand from memory, where you could guess wrong, with following the pairing rule, where every A gets a T and there is nothing to guess, and breaks on the wrong base that slips through anyway, the slide that introduces mutation without naming it. The second contrasts copying that keeps one whole old molecule and builds one whole new one against copying that splits and shares, settles it by asking how color-tagging the old strands would prove which happened, the Meselson and Stahl experiment in classroom form, and breaks on whether a strand your cells built today could still be in your body years from now.

Day one is Unzip and Rebuild: build a partner strand from a given strand, then trade and compare. Day two is the Replication Assembly Line, three stations for helicase, DNA polymerase, and ligase, with a paper strand processed through each. The two response slides cover the pairing rule and semiconservative copying. The extension is the Chargaff Check: if a sample is thirty percent A, what is the percentage of T, and of C and G, then explain why the percentage of A always equals the percentage of T in any sample of DNA, even one you have never seen.

You need paper or card DNA strand strips, base cutouts, and station signage. The Fiddle is the Build a DNA simulation, where students snap pieces together and work out what has to be true for them to fit; the base pairing strips double as transition puzzles. Activity guides are online for Unzip and Rebuild and the Assembly Line. The closing question, what reads the code, is answered in Cycle 15's protein synthesis deck; if you run core decks only, close that loop out loud on day one of the next cycle.

Cycle 15 — Genes and Chromosomes

How is a trait stored, and how does a cell read the code to build a protein? This is a two-deck cycle: the core deck is "Genes & Chromosomes," the extend deck "Protein Synthesis." The core deck's goal is that students can tell apart DNA, a gene, a chromosome, a locus, and an allele, working three critical aspects: a gene is a section of DNA while a chromosome carries many genes; a gene sits at a fixed address, its locus, on homologous chromosomes; and alleles are different versions of the same gene. It runs three tight arcs, breaking on six feet of DNA folded into a cell you cannot see, on siblings differing even though their gene order is identical, and on the distinction that a gene is the slot while an allele is which version fills it, then ties all six terms into a single explanation of one trait.

The extend deck claims that a cell reads the order of DNA's code to build proteins, with DNA copied into mRNA in transcription and the mRNA read in codons to assemble amino acids in translation. Its two aspects: the message must be copied to leave the nucleus, since the DNA stays safely at home, and codons are read in order to build the protein, with the pattern breaks doing the work of unsettling first answers. The core deck carries three response slides and the extend deck two.

Students build chromosomes from pipe cleaners, make a gene map, and run a class allele survey in the core deck, and model transcription with letter tiles and practice the path from DNA to mRNA to protein with codon charts in the extend deck. The Fiddle is Tour of the Basics, explicitly opened for students on arrival. The what-ifs: what would the world look like if every person carried exactly the same allele for every gene, and what would happen to living things over millions of years if mRNA were copied with zero mistakes forever, the question that seeds Cycle 16. You will need pipe cleaners, survey recording materials, letter tiles, and printed codon charts.

The Gizmo protein synthesis simulation is subscription content, so you will need your own access to use it.

Cycle 16 — Mutations and Genetic Change

How does the genetic code change, and what decides whether a change matters, spreads, or is passed on? This is a two-deck cycle: the core deck is "Mutations & Gene Expression" and the extend deck is "Stem Cell Differentiation," run in that order when you use both.

The core deck's signature model is THE DOG SAT: delete one letter and read it again in threes, and you get HED OGS AT. Its first arc contrasts a substitution with a frameshift, where both change one nucleotide and students must work out why one is far worse, since a frameshift inserts or deletes one letter and shifts everything after it. Its second contrasts a silent mutation with a missense one, since some codons code for the same amino acid, and breaks on sickle cell, the one-letter disease. The stem cell deck works unspecialized and self-renewing cells and the difference between embryonic and adult potency, arriving at the same-DNA paradox: a muscle cell and a stem cell hold identical DNA, so why can only one of them become anything?

The BioMan cell specialization activity carries the stem cell deck's hands-on work, and each deck has two response slides, four across the cycle. THE DOG SAT puzzle works cold with no technology, as does a sunburn inheritance bellringer, asking whether a sunburn can be passed to your children, and the Build a DNA simulation works as a Fiddle. The what-ifs cover a helpful mutation in a cold climate and an advantage carried in the germline, both deliberately seeding natural selection in Cycles 17 and 18, along with the ethics of CRISPR in embryos and turning a skin cell back into a stem cell. The cycle is largely paper and devices.

Biotechnology and genetic disorders are natural teacher-supplied extensions: selective breeding against genetic engineering, Bt corn crossing the species barrier, insulin made in bacteria, somatic against germline mutations, and Down syndrome as a whole extra chromosome all sit one step from the core deck's ideas, and a virtual gel electrophoresis lab, a desert-bones DNA identification case, and a karyotype sort, which doubles as a Fiddle, work well as additions. The follow-up videos support the decks and the extensions alike: Mutations Explained, TedEd on CRISPR, DNA fingerprinting, CRISPR and sickle cell, GMOs, the rainbow papaya, and hemophilia in the royal bloodline.

Cycle 17 — Darwin and the Evidence for Evolution

At fifty-seven slides this is the year's largest deck, and its concept is evolution as inherited change in populations over generations: individuals do not evolve, populations do. It carries four critical aspects, more than any other cycle: populations change over generations, traits fit environments, variation gives selection its options, and the evidence shows that life changed over time. The four arcs are woven through a teaching spine of deep time laid out on a football field, Lamarck against Darwin, and the voyage of the Beagle to the Galapagos. The first arc breaks on acquired change, asking why a scar, a bigger muscle, or a stretched neck may change an organism yet usually would not count as evolution. The second sets the same trait in two environments, buttress roots against desert roots. The third works finch beaks and what variation gives selection to act on. The fourth puts fossils beside island tortoises and asks why either line of evidence alone is less convincing than the two together.

Students run the PhET natural selection bunny lab, watch HHMI's "The Making of a Theory" with guided questions over about thirty minutes, place events on the football field timeline, take deep time digital notes with one species chosen for closer study, and complete a Galapagos postcard task in Google Earth in about twenty minutes. There are four response slides, one per critical aspect, and the what-if asks what happens when the environment changes but every organism carries exactly the same inherited trait. The PhET simulation is also the deck's labeled Fiddle, opened on arrival with two questions: what changes when you add mutations or change the environment, and what stays the same?

Print the guided question sheets, prepare physical timeline materials, and have devices ready. Videos: deep time on a football field, the HHMI film, the Galapagos, and endosymbiotic theory.

Cycle 18 — Natural Selection and Adaptation

The claim: when the environment selects among heritable variations, helpful traits become more common over generations. The two critical aspects are that variation is the raw material for selection and that the environment decides which traits survive. Peppered moths carry the entire deck. The first arc contrasts a moth population with variation, where dark moths spread as the trees darken, against one with none, where every moth is equally visible and there is no darker moth for selection to favor, and breaks on a population of pale beetles that turns mostly dark even though no individual beetle ever changed. The second drops the same dark wing trait into two forests, a lifesaver in one and a death sentence in the other, and breaks on the historically true reversal in which clean air laws pale the trees again and the winning trait starts losing. The environment sets the test, and it only asks which trait works here, right now.

Four activities all generate data students can argue from: the peppered moth one-minute hunt with catch data from a light forest and a dark one, the bean population lab comparing variation with no variation across three rounds of predation and graphing the result, a card activity putting the same trait in two environments, and Battle of the Beaks, where tweezers, clothespins, and spoons compete over changing seeds. The two response slides ask where new variation comes from and what the environment decides. The third-day extension is Gene Pool Beads, tracking one allele across four generations of bead draws and then answering the question that matters: no single bead ever changed color, so what actually evolved?

You will need dried beans in several colors, colored backgrounds, trait and environment cards, tweezers, clothespins and spoons, two seed types such as rice and lima beans, cups, thirty blue and twenty white beads per group, and graph paper. The peppered moths game makes a good Fiddle, its one-minute rounds transition-sized by design, alongside the allele tank model sliders. The closing question, whether two forests separated for thousands of years still hold one species, opens Cycle 19.

Cycle 19 — Speciation and Biodiversity

The claim: selection and isolation over long spans of time produce new species and the branching diversity of life. The two critical aspects are how new species form and what makes a grouping useful. The first arc opens on a Chihuahua and a Great Dane against a wolf and a coyote, enough to show that looks do not define a species, then contrasts an island of lizards with a river barrier against one without across ten thousand years, and breaks on the squirrels on the two rims of the Grand Canyon, separated and different in color but still able to breed, speciation caught in progress. The second deliberately reprises the grouping arc from Cycle 01, the same eight animals and the same bats-against-birds break, a designed bookend to the deck that opened the year.

Day one is Lizards on Islands, the HHMI "Lizards in an Evolutionary Tree" click and learn with a check sheet. Day two is exploring the tree of life on OneZoom, finding look-alike pairs that are distant on the tree and different-looking pairs that are close cousins. The two response slides ask what makes two species and what makes a grouping useful. The challenge extends the convergence idea: dolphins and sharks look alike but a dolphin is closer to a cow, so find one more pair of look-alikes that are not close relatives and check them on OneZoom. Print the check sheets for the lizard activity and have devices ready. OneZoom also serves as the Fiddle, along with the PhET bunny simulation, where students split a population and watch the groups drift apart. The closing question about cutting branches faster than new ones can form opens the capstone.

Cycle 20 — Human Impact Capstone

The capstone aims the year's ideas at one urgent problem: human activity reshapes ecosystems and biodiversity, and the question is what an evidence-based fix would actually look like. The first critical aspect is renewable against nonrenewable, worked by contrasting two identical forests cut at forty and at six hundred trees a year, making the point that renewable is not a promise and the rate rather than the resource decides, and broken by sunlight, the one resource you cannot use up. The second is non-native against invasive, worked by contrasting a backyard tomato with Burmese pythons in the Everglades, and broken by fertilizer-fed native algae choking a lake, which forces the question of the real cause if being foreign is not it. The closing slide is labeled full circle, back to the food web, a synthesis rather than a third arc.

Students run the footprint calculator and name the single choice in their day that pushed the number up the most, then complete Track an Invader, a guided web investigation of a python, a lionfish, a feral pig, or something they find themselves, predicting the ecosystem in ten years if nothing is done. The capstone product is a one-page proposal on a structured argument template, naming a real place, the impact, the mechanism, meaning how it bends an energy or nutrient cycle, and an evidence-based fix. There are two response slides, on sustainable use and invasion prediction, and the proposal is submitted with the deck. The third-day extension is cost against benefit: list your fix's benefits and costs, find one real trade-off, and defend whether it is still worth doing, having first watched the collapse of Easter Island, a real trade-off gone wrong.

The final move hands the year back to the students: pick one of the big ideas and connect it to one change in your own meals or energy use that ripples back through the food web. As the deck puts it, there is no right answer, and the connection is yours. Print the structured argument templates and have devices available. The live population counter is a Fiddle that needs no setup at all, along with the footprint calculator and the carbon tank sliders, and National Geographic's invasive species resource supports the second arc.

Fitting the Year to Your Schedule

Answerable Biology runs as twenty cycles, and a cycle is three class blocks rather than a week. Below are three worked examples of the cycles on real calendars: an alternating block schedule, daily ninety-minute blocks, and forty-five-minute periods five days a week. They show how the rhythm feels, not a pacing guide you must match. If a cycle takes you two blocks or five, you have not fallen behind.

Three things that are true on every calendar

You may move faster or slower than anything here, and the cycles are built to let you. In Katherine's words, "Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly. And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now."

The CORE deck — the main slide deck for a cycle — is a complete path on its own. Some cycles also carry an EXTEND deck, a second deck that goes further into the same material, and every deck carries an EXPLORE layer: the "Keep going" stretch questions, the What-if prompts, the optional Day 3 challenges, the Mastery tier of each concept question, and the fiddles. A fiddle is a simulation or short task left open on the projector before the bell, giving arrival, transitions, and early finishers somewhere to go. That layer is where pace differences live: a fast class absorbs it, a slower class skips it without losing anything structural. As Katherine says, "Use the core deck alone for a resistant-learner pace, or add the second to extend." A few cycles ask you to supply an activity of your own; your day-to-day operating page names which ones.

The unit of planning is the three-block cycle, not the week. Day 1 carries the most teaching plus a shorter activity. Day 2 is a shorter lesson with a longer activity or lab. Day 3 finishes the activity, returns students to their first answers, and runs feedback and revision. Each deck has day-divider slides marking those seams; wherever your bell schedule forces a cut, cut at a seam.

One cutting rule for every schedule: a pattern-break is the example that arrives right after students build a rule, there to test whether the rule still holds — keep the rule-building and its pattern-break in the same sitting. The two clean stopping places are after a response slide (the slide where each student writes a first answer into their own copy of the deck) and at a day divider. Stopping after a response slide leaves students complete, and it fits the standing policy that notes are due before students leave.

A. An alternating block schedule

This is the schedule the decks were built on: roughly ninety-minute blocks meeting every other school day — about five meetings per two weeks. Three blocks make a cycle, so five meetings carry one full cycle plus two thirds of the next at full speed. Cycles drift across the two-week boundary on purpose.

An eighteen-week semester gives you about forty-five meetings — fifteen cycles of capacity — and the whole year roughly eighty-five to ninety blocks against the sixty the cycles actually need. Nobody should run at full speed; that gap is the design. Katherine's own teacher slide for Cycle 02 reads "3 blocks, Sep 07 to 18": three blocks of teaching in a full two-week span early in the year, the rest absorbed by start-of-year routines, an activity that ran long, and the ordinary frictions of a real classroom. That is the system working, not running behind.

A realistic year puts Cycles 01 through 10 in the first semester — the ecology arc, then cells to enzymes — and Cycles 11 through 20 in the second: the cell cycle, the long genetics stretch of Cycles 12 through 16, and the capstone. Ten cycles is thirty blocks of about forty-five either semester, leaving roughly a third of your meetings free for the first week of routines, labs that spill over, reteaching, testing windows, and an EXTEND deck or two where a class is moving well.

The off day between meetings is a working asset. Students get a night between committing a first answer and meeting the cycle's second critical aspect (its second key idea), and you get the same gap to skim their first answers. Katherine's own habit: "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it."

B. Daily ninety-minute blocks

Same block length, meeting every day. Teaching the whole course in one semester of about ninety daily meetings is the alternating-block year compressed: a cycle every three days, about a cycle and a half per week, and twenty cycles inside roughly thirteen or fourteen weeks of an eighteen-week semester. The remaining weeks are your slack.

At this speed the overnight thinking time disappears, so protect Day 3 — do not fold revision into Day 2 to save a block. Revising against the first answer is the payoff of the whole arc; as Katherine puts it, "Seeing your thinking change is the point."

Daily blocks across a full year give you enormous room: twenty cycles use about sixty of roughly one hundred seventy-five meetings. Run the EXTEND decks liberally, give the labs their own full blocks, and let the EXPLORE layer breathe.

There are three sensible ways to divide a cycle across daily blocks, using the day dividers as stopping points.

  • Three consecutive days, Monday through Wednesday, is the tightest run. Best when the Day 1 and Day 2 activity is short or digital, as with the Screen-Door Sort and Build-a-Bilayer in Cycle 09, or Unzip and Rebuild in Cycle 14.
  • Three days spread across a week — Monday, Wednesday, Friday — is closest to the native rhythm. The buffer days take the overflow: the food-web build that needs a third sitting, absent students, the reteaching your class summary surfaced. Cycle 06 benefits particularly: both the fish and bromothymol blue demonstration and the yeast balloons reward time between looks.
  • Days 1 and 2 back to back with Day 3 after a gap gives a lab its momentum: the Leaf-Disk Float Lab in Cycle 05 gets debriefed on Day 2 while the result is fresh, and students still get some distance from their first answers before they revise.

If a block gets cut short by an assembly, a drill, or an early dismissal, stop at a response slide. With first answers written and the rule intact, you can pick up at the next slide tomorrow.

A few things matter more when periods are short. Give the lab a clean period of its own — period 4 above — and keep the setup light. Demonstrations meant to sit in the background of a whole block convert well: the fish in bromothymol blue in Cycle 06 becomes an observation you check once at the start of period 3 and once at the end of period 4, and the color change needs that much time anyway.

Fiddles earn more of their keep here, because arrival and transition time is a larger fraction of a short period. A simulation already open when students walk in — OneZoom, PhET Natural Selection, or Tour of the Basics — protects the minutes the lesson actually needs.

The notes-due rule applies per period rather than per cycle day — whatever thinking a period asked for gets written before students leave: "Students who are present complete and submit their notes before leaving class."

Finally, resist letting a cycle sprawl past six periods as a matter of course. If a class needs more time, that is what the slack is for — spend it deliberately on the cycles that need it.

The slack is structural

On any of these calendars, twenty cycles do not fill a thirty-six-week year, and that is deliberate. Sixty blocks of cycle time against eighty-five to ninety blocks of school leaves roughly a third of your meetings unassigned. That slack absorbs, in rough order of predictability: the first week of routines, holidays and testing windows, drills and assemblies, labs and builds that deserve a second sitting, reteaching your class summaries surface, EXTEND decks for a fast class, and the days a class simply needs to sit with an idea longer. Katherine's diagnosis is why the count is twenty and not twenty-eight: "the pace itself often destroys the possibility of depth. Students are asked to touch ideas briefly, perform understanding quickly, and move on before conceptual relationships stabilize." The three schedules above are ways of spending the room this structure buys you, not quotas to hit.

The Day 3 loop and grading, the same every cycle

Every cycle ends the same way, so it is written once here rather than twenty times below.

  1. Students open the deck's DRAFT slide — the one slide where they assemble their answers — copy the three-step coaching prompt from its speaker notes, and run it against their own first answer. Planning a Single Unit

A Unit Agenda is the one-page document you make for a single unit and teach from. Copy the shape below, fill it in from the deck and the Year Arc, and keep it beside you in class; the real test of a good agenda is whether you can read it at a glance while teaching.

It is not a script or a pacing guide. It holds only the unit's questions, the links you will open, the materials to have ready, and the Day 3 revision workflow. Design reasoning lives in Start Here, the year at a glance in the Year Arc and your operating page, calendar placement in the schedule examples, and ecology teaching depth in the Ecology Unit Guide.

How to fill one in

Open the unit's deck or decks and copy from the teacher reference on slide 1, and from the Teacher Navigation slide if there is one: the cycle number, essential claim, critical aspects, and any activity or learning management system links are already stamped there. Pull the central question, activity list, Fiddles, supplies, and remaining links from the matching cycle entry in the Year Arc, where they are already inventoried.

Keep every field to about the length in the worked example below; anything longer belongs in a unit guide, because an agenda you cannot read while teaching has stopped being an agenda. If something is genuinely missing, write that down rather than leaving the line blank.

Fill in your own class folder and learning management system links before Day 1 — the one part of this document nobody else can do for you.

What goes in each field

The agenda opens with the unit title, the cycle or cycles it covers — a cycle is the curriculum's repeating three-block unit built around one essential question — and the blocks it will take. On the cycle line, list the deck or decks that ship for the cycle (most carry one; a few carry two) and note which you are running this year. On the blocks line, three per cycle is usual; write in your own dates.

  • Unit arc. Two or three sentences: what students can do by Day 3 that they could not on Day 1, and what the unit hands forward. Write it from the essential claim, not the topic list.
  • Central questions. The question the cycle turns on, from the Year Arc entry, plus the critical aspects copied from the deck word for word. A critical aspect is the specific distinction a student must see before the claim makes sense; each gets its own arc in the deck.
  • Lessons. The deck name, whether it is CORE (the deck every class runs) or EXTEND (optional extension material), where the file lives, and one line per day. Day 1 carries the most teaching: the first critical aspect worked through first answer, contrast, rule, and pattern-break — the example that breaks the rule students just formed — then a short closing activity. Day 2 is a shorter lesson with a longer activity: the second critical aspect on the same arc, plus the main activity or lab. Day 3 is where thinking visibly changes: finish the activity, revise, and run the What-if or challenge.
  • Activities. One line each: name it, say in a sentence what students actually do, and give the materials link.
  • Labs. Name it, say the one thing the lab exists to show, and link the method. Delete this heading if the unit has no lab.
  • Fiddles. A Fiddle is the low-stakes simulation or task you leave open on arrival; note it here, plus what early finishers reopen when done.
  • Feedback and revision. The Day 3 loop, the same in every unit, written out in the example below.
  • Assessments. How many response slides the unit has, the reminder that the gradebook entry is completion only, and one line on what proficiency looks like here.
  • Supplies. Three short lines: what you print, what you gather, and what needs a device.
  • Optional follow-ups. The deck's extension or challenge, any video worth showing afterwards, and where the closing Keep-going question bridges to next.

A filled example: Ecosystems and Feeding Relationships

This is the free demonstration unit, worth reading as a model of length as much as content. It covers Cycle 02, which ships a single CORE deck, across three blocks; the deck's own dates read September 7 to 18 on an alternating block calendar.

Unit arc. Students start from their own dinner and end able to trace energy and matter through a food web they built themselves. The first arc establishes that energy thins out drastically at each feeding step; the second, that a relationship between species is defined by outcomes — who gains and who loses — not appearances. The closing Keep-going question, asking why life has not run out of energy, hands the problem forward to Cycles 03 through 05.

Central question. What connects every organism in an ecosystem? The essential claim is that "Organisms in an ecosystem are connected by the transfer of matter and energy through feeding relationships." The two critical aspects, copied from the deck, are "Critical aspect: Energy flow through trophic levels" and "Critical aspect: Symbiosis and species relationships."

Activities, Models, and Labs

Answerable Biology runs on activities, but it does not treat them all as the same kind of thing. A card sort, an open exploration, a working model, and a wet lab each have a distinct instructional job. This section explains each kind's job, shows real examples from the slide decks, and ends with a cycle-by-cycle catalog of the year.

Vocabulary first. The curriculum is organized into twenty cycles — short units of a few days, each built around two or three critical aspects, the specific conceptual features a student has to see and tell apart before the idea makes sense. Standards do not attach to individual activities; they live on each deck's teacher reference slide and in the standards documentation.

The one test every activity has to pass

Everything here was chosen or built against one question, in Katherine's words: "does this vehicle make the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students, and is it the best available vehicle for that purpose?"

The activity is never the point: "The shared object is not the slide. It is not the sim, the lab bench, or the artifact. Those are vehicles. The shared object is the conceptual feature the vehicle is pointing at." Each critical aspect gets an activity built for discerning it — wherever possible, something students play around with rather than being told. Her working test: "does it isolate the aspect, so that the thing the misconception gets wrong is the one thing the student cannot help noticing? If it does, it earns its place."

The design lineage is Montessori's pink tower: "The pink tower varies one thing — size — while holding color, shape, and material constant, so that size is the only difference the child's hand and eye can find. The contrast on the slide, the variable in the sim, the artifact pair on the counter — these are my attempt to do at scale, and for abstract ideas, what the pink tower does in the hand for size."

The four kinds below are not a hierarchy; a card sort has a different job than a lab, not a lesser one. What ruins an activity is misusing it — treating a vocabulary sort as proof of deep understanding, or burying a lab's one moment of noticing under twelve procedural steps.

Matching and card activities: the shared floor

Card work establishes vocabulary, classifications, and structures so every student holds the same basic pieces before the thinking questions arrive. Matching lowers both language load and cognitive load, because the terms sit on the table instead of in memory — the same job the word banks do on concept question slides when they say use any, modify any, or use none.

Card work is graded as completion. A finished sort is participation, not demonstrated proficiency, and should never be presented to a student, parent, or gradebook as evidence of deep understanding. The slides say it out loud: completion means both answers are present, and growth shows in the printed Conceptual Growth Report — the teacher-generated summary of a student's conceptual growth, handed over on paper — not in the grade. Proficiency is read from the revision artifacts instead, because a student can match every term and still reconstruct the whole misconception once the cards are put away. As Katherine puts it, "A student who has memorized the vocabulary but not discerned them has understood nothing."

Real examples from the decks:

  • Linnaean cup sort (Cycle 01, Classifying Organisms). Lab groups order cups labeled with the seven classification levels from most general to most specific, then call the teacher over when they think the order is right. Vocabulary becomes objects in the hand, the check is a conversation, and a completion moment is built into the directions.
  • Energy Flow Stations (Cycle 03). A station rotation that is mostly card work: match each term or organism to its role; sort animal cards by feeding type and stack them producers on the bottom, top predator on top; sort cards into the process that stores the sun's energy versus the one that releases it. Students visit in any order, do the task, write the answer, check the box.
  • Screen-Door Sort (Cycle 09). Six cards — oxygen, water, a large protein, a salt ion, a virus, and carbon dioxide — sorted into what crosses freely versus what is blocked or needs help, then a one-sentence generalization. The sort builds the floor; the closing sentence is the step up toward selective permeability, and the part worth reading.
  • Lock & Key Match (Cycle 10). Paper enzyme and substrate cutouts matched by shape, including one deliberately warped active site that matches nothing — a built-in pattern break, a case designed not to fit so the miss itself teaches the point.
  • Same Trait, Two Environments (Cycle 18). Trait and environment cards paired and reasoned about; the same trait card wins in one environment and loses in the other — the cycle's second critical aspect made physical.
  • Karyotype sort (Cycle 16, Mutations & Gene Expression). Chromosome images sorted to spot the extra chromosome — classification work in service of telling a gene mutation apart from a chromosomal one.

Two working rules. Card work belongs at the front of a cycle — Day 1 or the floor-setting step of a station day — before the contrast and pattern break ask students to think with the terms rather than hold them. And finished card sets are reusable: the Screen-Door cards re-sort for early finishers, the Lock & Key cutouts make good transition puzzles, and the checkpoint cards deal again as a quick game.

Open activities: manipulate, compare, notice

An open activity puts the critical aspect under students' fingers and lets them find it: students manipulate something, change a variable, compare cases, or build a model of their own, and they notice the critical relationship because it is the one thing the activity lets vary. Katherine's design statement, in full:

"Most of the activities I design are not for output. They are for noticing. I want students to handle something, notice something about it, and have a place to record what they noticed. The activity exists to put the critical aspect under their fingers and in front of their eyes. The output, if any, is a small note about what changed, what surprised them, what they have a question about. Drawing, when it serves the noticing, is a legitimate form."

Her worksheet contrast is the clearest statement of the approach: "A worksheet fits meaning to a form the student has not made. A noticing activity gives the student something to make meaning of."

Productive messing around is a legitimate learning phase, not off-task time. In her mapping of Ito's phases onto variation theory — the theory that students discern a feature by experiencing what varies against what stays constant — "Messing around corresponds to contrast and separation. Students begin to poke at differences to feel the limits of their initial generalization." The decks say it plainly: "Play one minute in the LIGHT forest, then one minute in the DARK forest. Notice which color moth you catch fastest in each" (Cycle 18), and "Play through one round. Notice what has to go IN for the plant to make food, and what comes OUT" (Cycle 05). The verb is notice, and it appears on the slide.

  • The two-day food web build (Cycle 02). Students sort species cards into producers, consumers, and decomposers and draw the arrows themselves to show who eats whom; on the second day they stack four organisms into a pyramid and run ten thousand units of energy through the ten percent rule. Because the web is something they made, the later Break the Web challenge works: remove one species, trace every arrow that touched it, and predict a year and a generation out. Arrows drawn wrong are something to talk over at the table, not a failure to remediate.
  • Journey through the carbon cycle (Cycle 04). Each student plays a single carbon atom, rolls dice at stations, and logs the moves. The debrief carries the noticing: did everyone's atom take the same path, and was there a station where you got stuck a long time, and why?
  • Build-a-Bilayer (Cycle 09). Students draw eight phospholipids arranged against water and label the water-loving heads and water-fearing tails — drawing in service of noticing why the oily middle decides what crosses.
  • Unzip and Rebuild, and the Replication Assembly Line (Cycle 14). Students build a partner strand from a given strand, trade and compare, then process a paper strand through three stations named helicase, DNA polymerase, and ligase. The students are the enzymes; the pairing rule is the thing they cannot help using.
  • The Cycle 18 quartet, the year's clearest case of students generating their own data. The Peppered Moth One-Minute Hunt collects catch data in a light forest and then a dark one. The Variation versus No Variation bean population lab makes two bean populations — one a single color, one a mix — lets a predator remove the easiest-to-see beans for three rounds, and graphs how each changes. Battle of the Beaks gives each student a beak tool — tweezers, clothespin, or spoon — and thirty seconds to pick up seeds, then changes the environment so only large tough seeds remain. Gene-Pool Beads closes with the question that ties the four together: no single bead ever changed color, so what actually evolved? Four rounds of one discernment — selection acts on variation and no individual ever changes — each generating numbers the students made themselves.
  • Galapagos postcard task (Cycle 17) and the OneZoom exploration (Cycles 01 and 19): open exploration given a specific hunt — find a pair that look almost the same but sit far apart on the tree, and a pair that look very different but are close cousins.

What you collect at the end is the small note — what changed, what surprised you, what you have a question about. Do not convert an open activity into a worksheet by bolting a page of questions onto it; the output has to stay light enough that noticing remains the actual work.

Models: the variable they move is the aspect itself

Models are open activities with one special property: the variable students manipulate is the critical aspect. Katherine's reasoning about simulations transfers to every model, physical or digital: "What makes the sim work is not that it is interactive or digital. It is that the variable they manipulate is the critical aspect itself. They are not being asked to memorize a dependency; they are given an object that lets them feel it."

The interactive tank-and-flow models are the house-built version of this idea, using one visual metaphor all year so it accumulates meaning: tanks holding stored energy or carbon, flows between them, and sliders on the one thing that matters. The family spans the year: the Energy Tank Model in Cycles 02 and 03, where students drag sliders to change transfer efficiency and watch the tanks respond; the Carbon Tank Model in Cycles 04 and 20; the Photosynthesis Rate Model in Cycle 05; the Respiration ATP Model in Cycle 06; the Osmosis Model in Cycle 09; the Enzyme Rate Model in Cycle 10; the Cell Cycle Model in Cycle 11; the Allele Tank Model in Cycle 18; and the Biodiversity Tank Model in Cycle 19. All live at answerable-biology-models.netlify.app, so one link on your class page opens the whole set.

Physical models do the same job with hands instead of sliders:

  • Pop Bead Meiosis lab (Cycle 12): chromosomes are snap-together beads; the halving and reshuffling is performed, not described.
  • Build a sugar molecule (Cycle 05): molecular model kits rearrange six carbon dioxide and six water molecules into glucose and six oxygen, under the constraint that carries the point — every atom used, none thrown away. Conservation of matter is enforced by the kit itself.
  • Pipe cleaner chromosome build, gene map, and class allele survey (Cycle 15): gene, locus, and allele become places and versions on a thing the student made.
  • Modeling Transcription with letter tiles (Cycle 15, Protein Synthesis): the default path for that lesson, needing no subscription.
  • The Activation-Energy Hill (Cycle 10): a marble rolled over a cardboard hill, then the hill is lowered. The enzyme's job in a single move.
  • THE DOG SAT (Cycle 16): a sentence read in threes, delete one letter and re-read — HED OGS AT. The frameshift gets felt in language before it is named in genetics, with no materials at all.

One caution from Katherine's book applies to every model and labeled diagram: "Students can memorize a labeled diagram of a feedback loop and still miss how a system regulates itself. Students see the representation and mistake it for the thing." The target of a model activity is always the dynamic — what happens when the slider moves, the letter is deleted, the beads are drawn — never the labeled parts. If your debrief question can be answered from the diagram without running the model, it is the wrong debrief question.

Outside simulations and games are used the same way, usually in the fiddle or bellringer slot with one line naming what to notice. A fiddle is Katherine's term for a small exploration already open and waiting when students walk in, needing no more than a single line of direction; the bellringer is the short written question that follows it. The fiddles documentation covers that slot in full, including a no-technology version of every fiddle.

Labs: hands-on is not automatically better

Katherine's lab philosophy runs against the grain of most science curricula:

"Labs done soberly. Labs cost time, setup, and class periods. I do them when the lab is the right way to teach the critical aspect — not because the curriculum prescribed a lab this week, and not because the lab is the photogenic part of biology. The question I ask before scheduling one is whether the embodied nature of the lab actually matters for what I want students to discern. If yes, it is worth the time. If no, the lab is theater that takes time from the real lesson, and I have learned to refuse the theater."

She is equally direct about directions: "I used to treat labs like they were being prepared for FDA approval — serious steps, fine-print directions. Now I am messier. Directions are scant. If someone does it wrong, that is something to think about, not a failure to remediate. The lab is more honest when the student's noticing is what the lab is for, rather than adherence to a procedure."

Four operating rules follow:

  1. A lab earns its blocks by the same test as everything else: does the embodied part actually matter for the discernment? If a contrast slide or a simulation teaches the aspect as well, use the cheaper vehicle and keep the time.
  2. Simplify the directions. When the direction count grows, attention goes to compliance — did I do step seven? — instead of the phenomenon. Complexity belongs in the design of the lab, not in directions students have to decipher.
  3. Name what students are supposed to notice, on the slide, in plain words. The Leaf-Disk Float Lab tells students that floating disks are full of oxygen the plant just made — live proof photosynthesis is happening — and the Yeast Balloon lab names its noticing as proof the yeast made energy with no air, since the balloon seals out oxygen and carbon dioxide inflates it anyway. The scientific purpose comes first; the procedure serves it.
  4. Build the control into the noticing. The no-carbon-dioxide cup that never floats gets its own debrief slide asking what that tells you a plant needs to make its food; the no-sugar bottle's balloon stays flat; the bromothymol blue cup with no fish stays blue. The control is not a formality but one side of the contrast, laid out as case A and case B on the lab bench.

The labs of the year, run in that spirit:

  • Leaf-Disk Float Lab (Cycle 05), the year's first wet lab and where lab safety gets taught once, in plain terms: today's lab uses only water, baking soda, and spinach, all safe, but you build good habits now for later labs with real chemicals. Counting floated disks each minute is deliberately built in as waiting-time work.
  • Yeast Balloon Fermentation Lab (Cycle 06), run with a no-sugar control bottle. Watching the balloons inflate over fifteen to twenty minutes is transition work by design.
  • Temperature and pH Lab, with the Liver-Catalase extension (Cycle 10): catalase rate graphed against temperature and against pH. The second-day callback — a boiled potato will not foam — turns the opening demonstration into evidence for denaturing.
  • Variation versus No Variation bean population lab and Battle of the Beaks (Cycle 18), both described above as data students generate with their own hands.
  • Several shorter pieces: the "What's in a Drop?" pond dip (Cycle 01), the PTC taste test and the Cheek Cell Lab (Cycle 13), quadrat sampling (Cycle 07), and the Oil, Water and the Bilayer mini lab that runs as the third-day challenge in Cycle 09.

What a fiddle is

A fiddle is a small exploration — usually a simulation, sometimes cards or a puzzle — that is already open and running when students walk in. It sits on the board or their Chromebooks before the bell, needs no directions beyond a single line, and points at the same idea the lesson is about to teach. Katherine's own slides name the slot: one reads "Fiddle sim (open for students on arrival)" and another simply "Explore first (fiddle)." Her timing notes on the Darwin deck give the rhythm to aim for: five to seven minutes of simulation entry, three to five minutes on the bellringer, then teaching. The bellringer is the short written question that follows the fiddle and hands the class over to the lesson.

One fiddle quietly covers several real classroom jobs at once:

  • Bell ringer exploration. Students meet the cycle's idea by poking at it before anyone explains it. The Cycle 17 fiddle asks exactly that: what changes when you add mutations or change the environment, and what stays the same?
  • Attendance time. The first minutes are yours for roll, late passes, and the student who needs a word, while the room is already working.
  • Transitions. Between lab cleanup and the next slide, or between an activity and the concept question. The Peppered Moths game in Cycle 18 runs in one-minute rounds — exactly transition sized.
  • Work for early finishers. The student who is done has somewhere real to go that is still this cycle's content, not an unrelated puzzle sheet.
  • Extension. Most fiddles have a deeper layer right behind them: the PhET simulation becomes the Bunny Lab, OneZoom becomes the convergent evolution hunt.
  • An alternative to phones. The open tab competes for the same idle minutes, and it happens to be the lesson.
  • Breathing room for slower workers. When faster students have a fiddle and a challenge to reach for, you can give a slower student ten more minutes without dragging the class behind. This is the pace principle in Katherine's words: "the pace itself often destroys the possibility of depth."

None of this is busywork, because a fiddle's variable is usually the cycle's critical aspect itself — the specific conceptual feature students must be able to see before the idea makes sense. As Katherine puts it about simulations, "What makes the sim work is not that it is interactive or digital. It is that the variable they manipulate is the critical aspect itself. They are not being asked to memorize a dependency; they are given an object that lets them feel it." Messing around is a legitimate learning phase in this system, and the fiddle is where it happens on purpose.

Running one is simple. Open the tab or set out the cards before students arrive, put one line of instruction on the slide, and do not debrief it — it feeds the bellringer, and the bellringer feeds the first-answer question. Every fiddle below also has a no-technology alternative for the day the wifi dies, the cart goes missing, or a student has lost device privileges.

Grading: Completion, Copying, and Proficiency

This is the assessment reference for Answerable Biology: what completion and proficiency mean, why they are kept apart, what to do about copying, and how to set up your gradebook either way your school requires. Examples come from Cycle 02, Ecosystems and Feeding Relationships, the free demonstration unit. A cycle is one teaching arc of a few class days built around a single set of ideas, and it always turns on a response slide in the student deck: a question with two labeled spaces beneath it, one for the answer written before the idea has been taught and one for the answer written after.

Two different things a grade can mean

Most gradebooks blur two separate questions into a single number. This system keeps them apart on purpose.

Completion is participation in the thinking process: the student wrote a first answer in their own words, built a rule out of the contrasts, met the pattern break (the case the lesson deliberately chooses to strain the rule just built, such as a decomposer that does not sit neatly in a food chain), ran the revision prompt, and wrote a revised answer. Completion says the student showed up to the thinking; it says nothing about whether the thinking is correct yet.

Proficiency is what a student can understand, explain, distinguish, and apply — can they trace energy through a food web, or tell mutualism from parasitism in a case they have never seen? It is read from evidence, mainly the revised answer at the end of each cycle, and it lives in the Conceptual Growth Report — the one-page document you generate for each student each cycle, read and adjust, and hand back in print. It does not live in the gradebook.

The reason for holding them apart is structural. Every cycle asks students to commit to a first answer before the idea is taught, because that is where the misconception surfaces. The moment the first answer carries a correctness grade, students stop putting incomplete thinking on the page. Keeping completion in the gradebook and proficiency in the printed report is what makes the first answer safe enough to be honest.

The standing rule appears on the closing checklist of every deck, addressed to students: "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." The speaker notes on the same slide tell you that the finished deck, first and revised answers side by side, is the single artifact that produces each student's printable Growth Report and your class summary, and that completion means both boxes are filled.

Grades still matter here. In Katherine's words: "Grades heavy enough to matter — I am not going to pretend ninth-graders will engage purely for intrinsic reasons, and they need the grades to navigate the system. What I was not willing to simplify was the thinking itself."

Why developing work is credited rather than scored

A confidently wrong first answer is doing its job. Katherine's reasoning for refusing to score it runs along four lines.

Testing too early measures nothing: "Testing students before they have conceptual footing does not measure understanding. It confirms its absence, which they already know."

Ninth graders need room to expose incomplete thinking, and safety is what produces it: "students surface partial knowledge they would not risk in front of the full class. A student offers an idea they are not sure about, half-finished, sometimes wrong in a way more interesting than being right." That only happens when half-finished carries no gradebook penalty.

Silence at a hard question is usually not disengagement: "Students are not yet confident enough to commit to a position in public, especially when there is no right answer to hide behind. But I have stopped reading that silence as disengagement. It is, more often, the silence of genuine thinking — of students working out what they believe before risking it. That working-out is exactly what I want."

And the risk has preconditions: "Before students have the confidence to dialogue in public, they need confidence in their own thinking." The calibration that goes with it is worth holding onto as you teach: "I watch for the threshold where the task tips from challenging into defeating. Too hard and they stop; too easy and they are bored. The window is narrow and it moves."

So developing work — first answers, rule-building sentences, annotations, the guesses a slide tells students to keep making — is credited for being done, not scored for being right. Katherine states the credit standard constantly: "I care about your thinking, not the answer. 'Because' is not an answer. 'I don't know' is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy."

That line is the whole completion bar, and it is not a policy of tolerating blanks. A single word like "Because" does not complete a slide; a sentence that shows genuine contact with the question does, even when it turns out to be wrong.

Cycle 02 shows the bar in practice. Day 1 ends at a three-tier concept question: "What has to be true about how organisms use energy for there to always be more producers than consumers?" Students choose their own tier. Getting Started asks how many of the 10,000 calories a meadow stores in its grass reach the hawks, showing the math. Working On It asks why there can never be more foxes than rabbits in a stable ecosystem, even though foxes are faster and better hunters. Mastery asks what would have to change for a seven-level ecosystem to be stable, given that the 10% rule limits most ecosystems to four or five trophic levels. A word bank sits alongside, offered as "use any, modify any, or use none." Whatever tier a student chose, if what they wrote shows thinking, completion is earned — the student who chose Getting Started and did the arithmetic honestly has completed the work as fully as the one who reached for Mastery.

Copying, and why the design does most of the work

When an assignment defines success as obtaining the correct words, copying is not a character flaw but the efficient strategy: on a worksheet with blanks or a question whose answer sits back on slide nine, the copied answer and the understood answer look identical on paper, and the copied one takes less time. Students respond to the incentive an assignment actually creates.

Copying also tends to happen when work is delayed. Written to students on the slide explaining why notes are due: "When you wait until later: you remember less, you're more likely to copy, you miss the learning that the notes are designed to create." The rule that notes are due before the bell is a structure against copying before it is anything else.

The curriculum's answer is design rather than surveillance — the work is built so copying stops paying off. The thought questions have no source to copy from: "The thought questions do not have answers you can copy; they require students to apply a distinction they have just made to a new case, or to notice where a pattern breaks down." The what-if questions have no answer at all, as in "If energy is lost at every step and never comes back, why hasn't life run out of energy? There's no answer to look up." The pattern break asks students to test their own rule against decomposers or the orchid that mimics a female bee — something a borrowed sentence cannot do. And first and revised answers sit side by side on one slide while the Growth Report quotes the student's own words back to them, so a copied first answer produces a hollow report about nobody. Above all, the grade a student could protect by copying is a completion grade, so there is nothing to gain: the honest wrong answer and the copied right answer earn identical credit, and only the honest one buys feedback worth having.

What Katherine praises shows where the value sits. Of one student's work: "his own sentences, his own working-out, nothing copied — I said: Hey, all this you wrote in your own words? It's very good. Please continue."

The integrity gate

The pipeline still checks, because authentic feedback requires authentic work. The integrity gate is the check the batch run performs on student decks before it drafts anything.

  • It runs on the first answer only. The revision is exempt by design: students are told to run the coaching prompt through their own AI, so AI involvement in the revision is the assignment, not a violation.
  • A copied first answer is flagged for your review. The flag holds that cycle's completion entry until you resolve it; nothing posts on its own.
  • A first answer that appears to be written by an AI is flagged for your review, never auto-failed. The response is more work, not a zero: the student interrogates the AI-written answer — questions it, tests it, improves it in their own words — and that answer is rated on the same proficiency scale alongside the revision, so the change between what the machine wrote and what the student made of it is visible.
  • Flags stay visible to you, and you decide. Automated detection misfires, most often on students learning English and writers with unusual style, so a flag is a case to look at, not a verdict. If it is a false positive, restore that student's feedback. Because the gradebook records only completion, a wrong flag costs one cycle's feedback, not a grade.

The gate protects the feedback loop, not a score. A first answer authored by a machine can only produce a report about the machine's thinking; making the student question and improve that answer puts their own thinking back into the artifact — which is what keeps the Growth Report worth printing.

What not to police

Do not run integrity enforcement on low-level matching and vocabulary work: the Linnaean cup sort, the Lock and Key enzyme match, card sorts, labeled diagrams, and key-terms work. That work exists for exposure — building a shared floor of vocabulary, classifications, and structures so the thinking work later has words available. Students helping each other or looking a term up is that floor being built, not cheating; it is completion work either way and was never evidence of proficiency. Save your integrity attention for the one place it matters: the first answer on the response slides.

Proficiency: where it lives and how to read it

Proficiency is judged per critical aspect — one of the two or three discernments a cycle's topic actually depends on — rather than per unit or per test, and it means the student can make that discernment and use it. Vocabulary is not the bar, as Katherine puts it: "A student who discerns those has understood natural selection, even if their vocabulary is still rough. A student who has memorized the vocabulary but not discerned them has understood nothing."

The primary evidence is the revised answer, written in the student's own words after the contrasts, the pattern break, the activity, and the feedback, and sitting directly under the first answer so the movement between them is visible on one slide. The Growth Report reads that pair and reports each critical aspect as a position on a path, using the same plain labels as the tiered questions: Getting Started, Working On It, and Mastery. The class summary from the same run gives you the room-level read: "who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed."

Two rules protect the report itself. Proficiency is always reported as "a position on a path, never a deficit," and every affirming line is anchored to something the student actually wrote. No scoring vocabulary reaches students beyond those three labels — no codes, no jargon; the scales below are for you.

Two worked scales from Cycle 02

A usable scale describes the progression of the discernment itself, not a quantity of correct words. The three tiers built into every concept question — Getting Started, Working On It, Mastery — are the same progression seen from the student's side, and students locate themselves on it when a slide asks them to rate where they were before the lesson and where they are now. Here are the two Cycle 02 scales; write your own for other cycles on the same pattern: one scale per critical aspect, three levels, each a description of what the student can actually do.

For energy flow through trophic levels: a student at Getting Started describes who eats whom and may treat energy as passed along whole, as in "the hawk gets the grass's energy." A student at Working On It states the 10% rule and does the math, taking 10,000 calories of grass down to roughly 100 that reach the hawks, but loss is still a fact to recite — they cannot say where the other 90% went or why the pyramid has to narrow. A student at Mastery explains where the energy goes — heat through respiration, waste, material left uneaten or dead — and puts the explanation to work: why a meadow feeds thousands of rabbits but only ten to fifteen hawks, why chains stop at four or five levels, and where a decomposer fits without the rule breaking.

For symbiosis and species relationships: a student at Getting Started labels relationships by memorized example, such as bees and flowers being mutualism, with no rule underneath that transfers. A student at Working On It sorts clear cases by outcome — who gains, who loses, who is unaffected — and gets bee and flower, mosquito and human, and pilot fish and shark right on that basis. A student at Mastery applies the outcome rule to a case built to break it: the orchid that mimics a female bee is pollinated and pays no nectar, and the student tracks the actual costs and benefits instead of reaching for a memorized label, then says what the relationship would become if the payoffs changed.

Reading the Cycle 02 pair

On Day 1 a student writes, as their first answer, "you get all the energy because you ate the whole cow." Completion is earned: it is a committed, honest answer. Proficiency reads as Getting Started — and it is precisely the misconception the lesson exists to break.

On Day 3, after the meadow contrast, the decomposer pattern break, the food web build, and the two questions their AI asked them, the same student writes underneath: "you only keep about 10% because the cow used most of its energy being alive — it went off as heat, and some was never eaten. That's why there's way more grass than hawks." That is proficiency evidence — the 10% figure, the mechanism, and the consequence for the pyramid, in the student's own words. The Growth Report quotes it back under the heading "Evidence from my final answer." Nothing about this pair enters the gradebook except that both boxes were filled and the cycle is complete.

Setting up your gradebook

There are two workable configurations: the system as Katherine runs it, and an adaptation for schools that require proficiency in the gradebook, built to satisfy that requirement without dismantling the protections that make the first answer safe.

Option A: completion only

The gradebook records completion; all proficiency lives in the printed Growth Report.

  • One completion entry per cycle, where complete means both answer areas are filled on each response slide. Cycle 02 has two response slides, and the closing checklist tells students exactly this. Katherine's pipeline posts the entry as a CSV to Schoology, one row per student and one column per lesson, headed DRAFT.<Lesson> or Notes.<Lesson>, but a column you keep by hand works identically.
  • Completion of notes and annotations can be a second recurring entry if you collect them, since notes are due before the bell.
  • Matching work, card work, and vocabulary work are completion entries as well, if you enter them at all.
  • Nothing about answer quality ever posts. Proficiency reaches students only through the printed Growth Report, and you through the class summary.
  • Integrity outcomes touch completion and nothing else. A copying or AI flag holds that completion entry pending your review, and nothing posts automatically; an AI-flagged first answer earns completion once the student has interrogated and improved it.

Keep the grades heavy enough to matter: weight the completion category so a student who skips the thinking sees it in their grade. Exactly how heavy belongs to your school context.

Option B: completion plus proficiency

This variant uses two categories.

Completion stays identical to Option A: every cycle, both boxes filled, credit for participation in the thinking. This category must stay untouched, because it is what makes the first answer safe to write honestly.

Proficiency becomes a small number of entries per marking period — two to four, not one per cycle — each scored from revised answers only against the critical aspect scales above, where 3 is Mastery, 2 is Working On It, and 1 is Getting Started. Enter the score after the Growth Report run has finished, so you have the report's read alongside your own; the judgment recorded is yours, not the pipeline's.

Three lines have to hold for this variant not to destroy the design it sits inside. Never score the first answer for correctness — the moment it carries a proficiency grade, students stop exposing incomplete thinking; score only where the arc ends. Score few rather than all — grading every revised answer turns revision into a test and re-creates the incentive to copy it, whereas a handful of checkpoint cycles per marking period gives the gradebook what the school needs while most cycles stay pure feedback. Keep the scale plain for students — if a proficiency score posts where students can see it, it carries the same labels the report uses, never rubric jargon or codes.

If your school mandates percentage grades rather than a 1–3 scale, map the levels onto bands — Mastery 90–100, Working On It 80–89, Getting Started 70–79 — rather than inventing finer distinctions the evidence cannot support.

Either way

The reporting order never changes: student thinking produces a revised answer, the revised answer produces the Growth Report, and only then, if your school requires it, does a proficiency entry appear in the gradebook. The gradebook sits downstream of the report and never substitutes for it. What students hear is the same sentence in both configurations: your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade.

AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth Reports

One finished student deck becomes three kinds of feedback: coached revision while the learning is still happening, a printed Conceptual Growth Report for each student — a one-page narrative of how that student's thinking moved during the cycle, which you generate and hand back on paper — and a whole-class read for you. This section explains all three and where your judgment sits. Grading — completion, copying, the integrity gate, gradebook setup — is in the grading section. Examples come from Cycle 02, Ecosystems and Feeding Relationships, the free demonstration unit.

The loop

Every cycle — each repeating lesson arc in the course — ends the same way, and the feedback system runs on that ending: student, AI feedback, student revision, then you as the human gate.

  1. Student. In class, the student commits to a first answer on the response slide, in their own words, before the idea is fully taught. The deck is their thinking made visible.
  2. AI feedback. On Day 3 the student opens the deck's DRAFT slide — the one slide where they assemble their answers — copies the three-step coaching prompt from its speaker notes into their own AI, and pastes in their first answer. The prompt is built so the AI cannot give them the answer; it can only help them see their own thinking.
  3. Student revision. The student writes a revised answer directly under the first one on the response slide, in their own words, and both stay visible. As every deck tells them, "Seeing your thinking change is the point."
  4. Teacher, the human gate. The finished deck, first answer beside revised answer, is the single artifact submitted. You run the batch over the folder of decks; it drafts a Growth Report per student and a class summary for you. You read every word before anything reaches a student, and adjust what you find. In Katherine's words: "The reading is not quality control bolted on. It is the place the human judgment lives."

Nothing else is collected — no exit tickets, no separate reflection document, no email. One artifact, one loop, every cycle. The loop is gated by a person at both ends: the student's own words go in (that is what the integrity gate protects), and your judgment signs off on what comes out. Neither checkpoint is overhead a better tool could remove.

The prompt students run for themselves

The coaching prompt sits, word for word, in the speaker notes of the DRAFT slide — one copy per deck, on the slide where students assemble their answers — and students copy it into their own AI.

Copy everything between the lines into your AI, and paste your first answer where it says [YOUR FIRST ANSWER]. The AI will NOT give you the answer — it will help you see your own thinking so you can improve it yourself.

-----

I am a high school student. Below is a science question and my first answer. Do NOT rewrite my answer and do NOT give me the answer. Instead:

  1. Tell me one thing I got right, and quote my own words back to me.
  2. Ask me two questions that make me look again at one idea I might have wrong or left out.
  3. Name one distinction or example worth thinking about.

Keep it short and in plain language, then stop so I can write my own revised answer.

QUESTION: [the slide's question]

[YOUR FIRST ANSWER]:

-----

Now write your revised answer in "Your revised answer" on the slide — in your own words.

Every constraint is doing work. Quoting the student's words back reads them as a knower — their sentence was worth quoting. The two questions send them back to their own idea, not toward a correct version of it. The one distinction gives them something to think with rather than transcribe. The stop keeps the AI from becoming the doer; the revised answer has to be the student's own. This coached revision is the student's whole AI step — it produces no report. Reports come later, from you.

What a good run looks like

In Cycle 02 the response slide asks, "At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy — or something else? Why?" A student's first answer reads, "you get all the energy because you ate the whole cow." A well-behaved run returns something close to this:

You're right that the energy in your food came up the chain from the grass — "because you ate the whole cow" shows you tracking where it came from. Two questions: What was the cow doing with its energy during all the years it was alive? If you ate the whole cow, did you also eat the heat it gave off? One thing worth thinking about: there is a difference between eating an animal's body and getting all the energy that ever passed through it. Now write your revised answer.

That is the target: short, plain, warm, thinking left to the student.

The feedback register

The coaching prompt governs what students run for themselves. The register below governs everything the AI drafts on your side — Growth Reports and any written feedback. It is Katherine's pedagogy turned into instructions:

"Do not grade. Do not produce a corrected version. Read the student as a knower. Name where their reasoning was already pointing at something true, even when the final answer was not. Name what their sentence reveals about how they are thinking. Name where their thinking has become more skilled than it was last time, so the growth is visible to them and not only to me. Never use the language of deficit. Speak to them the way I would crouch beside them in class — only on paper, and for every student, including the ones I missed that week."

That last clause is why the system exists: the problem the AI solves here is arithmetic, not pedagogy. In Katherine's account: "a single teacher cannot personally hear from every student in the room every day. Lezir got several minutes that Tuesday. Other students got none. Across a week, the arithmetic catches up. The shy student making real progress who never volunteers; the student who was absent and came back without anyone noticing what she wrote; the student who is half-right in a way that needs to be named for them to take the next step — these students sit at the edge of my attention." None of this is what the machine does on its own; the cautions below explain what it will do instead if you let it.

Inline feedback during learning

The first of the three feedback forms is the revision loop inside the deck, described above — the only form students drive themselves, run while the idea is still live, inside the cycle rather than after it. It works because it is aimed at exactly one thing — the answer the student just wrote on the critical aspect the lesson turns on — it arrives right before revision while there is still something to do with it, and it cannot substitute for the student's thinking because it is forbidden to contain the answer.

Your part here is presence, not processing. Day 3 is for circulating: students run the prompt and revise while you find the student whose AI response confused them, the student whose first answer was blank, the student whose revision is a copy of the question the AI asked. Katherine's habit for work that needs a person is direct: "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it."

The individual Conceptual Growth Report

The second form is the Conceptual Growth Report: one per student per cycle, generated by you from the finished deck in your batch run, read and adjusted by you, then printed and handed back on paper. Print is the designed artifact — something a ninth grader can hold, keep, and show to someone — though a digital copy can ride along. The report is where all proficiency lives; the gradebook holds completion only. It reads the first answer and the revised answer as a pair and tells the story of the movement between them, in the student's own frame, first person, under plain headers.

  • Where my thinking started — the initial thinking, stated plainly and without judgment; the first answer was doing its job.
  • How my thinking changed — the distinction or correction that happened between the two answers, named clearly enough that the student can see it.
  • Evidence from my final answer — the student's own words carrying the change, quoted word for word; for the Cycle 02 student above, "the cow used most of its energy being alive — it went off as heat." Their sentence comes back as the proof.
  • Current understanding — progression bars, one per critical aspect of the cycle (in Cycle 02: energy flow through trophic levels; symbiosis and species relationships). The labels are plain — Getting Started, Working On It, Mastery — and each is always a position on a path, never a deficit.
  • My next step — the remaining conceptual target as one forward, doable move, an invitation rather than a remediation.
  • What I understand now that I didn't before — left blank for the student, so the report ends in their handwriting rather than the machine's.
  • An idea worth chasing — when there is one: a generative idea in the student's work, named even at low proficiency, with an invitation to bring it to class. Wrong-but-original thinking gets named as an asset, because that is what it is.

As the year accumulates, the rollup across units produces a year arc for each student, with later cycles' reports as evidence that an earlier revision held. A student who broke the rule that energy passes upward whole in Cycle 02 meets that reasoning again in the arithmetic of Cycle 03 and the carbon cycling of Cycle 04, and the rollup can point at it.

The standards every report meets

Deeply positive and true. Every affirming line is anchored to something the student actually did — a word-for-word quotation or a move you can name. No generic or unearned praise; teenagers read that as fake, and it buries the next step.

Readable by a striving ninth grader. Plain language throughout, without dumbing down the science.

No coding vocabulary reaches the student. No NGSS codes, no rubric or research jargon, none of "epistemic," "warrant," or "agency," no proficiency-scale terminology. That vocabulary lives only on your side, on a separate research rail students never see.

Print friendly. One page, formatted to hand back.

The register above governs every sentence. The AI drafts the report, you read every word, and the report that reaches the student is yours.

The whole-class view

The third form is the class summary, produced by the same batch run — the read at the scale of the room, for you only. It answers four questions each cycle.

Student by student — each student's position on each critical aspect, integrity flags visible. Your intervention list: who needs you this week, before the next cycle builds on this one.

Concept by concept — the spread of proficiency on each critical aspect, or in the unit descriptions' phrasing, "who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed."

Where the class is stuck — recurring misconceptions, quoted word for word where possible. If a third of the room still writes "the hawk gets the grass's energy" after revision, that is one class problem, not thirty individual ones.

What needs whole-class reteaching — stated as the next instructional contrast to teach, not a topic to repeat: a sharper pairing of Case A against Case B that isolates exactly what the room has not yet discerned.

Across cycles, the course-level rollup shows misconceptions and growth trends over the twenty cycles and your four marking periods — the read that tells you in November what to build differently in February. This is what the report system buys you beyond what students get: "You see what the class is ready for next. No stack of papers to grade."

Culminating versions for conferences and meetings

The same evidence base — first answers, revised answers, the progressions, the year-arc rollup — can be compiled for occasions when you need to talk about a student's development with someone else in the room. Parent-teacher conferences: the cycle reports plus the year arc, showing where a student's thinking started in September and what it looks like now, in their own quoted words. Student conferences: the year arc as the agenda — which revisions held, which next steps got taken, what is worth chasing next. Parent meetings outside the conference calendar: the same artifacts, current to the latest cycle. IEP-related meetings: the reports as classroom evidence of what this student can do on each aspect, in their own written work, over time. The same holds for counselors, co-teachers, and administrators.

Two rules hold for every one of these versions, without exception. Grounded only in observable classroom work: everything traces back to something the student actually wrote or did, and nothing else belongs. No diagnoses, ever: these documents describe what a student's work shows about their developing understanding of biology, and never characterize ability, attention, processing, disability, or anything clinical. In an IEP context especially, the report is classroom evidence brought to a meeting, not an evaluation instrument.

Katherine's stance on differentiation travels with you into these meetings: the AI "writes IEP-accessible versions of an assessment I would otherwise have written by hand at midnight," and "I differentiate not by lowering the conceptual bar but by removing the linguistic obstacles."

What to be careful about

Katherine names three cautions; between them, they are the reason the human gate exists.

First, the AI is confidently wrong in a particular and dangerous way. In her words: "It can affirm reasoning that is actually mistaken, or invent a 'real part' of a student's answer that is not there, in fluent, encouraging prose. A book whose whole argument is that the deepest harm is misreading a student as a knower cannot pretend that an instrument which sometimes misreads — beautifully, persuasively — is harmless. That is exactly why I read everything before it goes out." The failure mode is not clumsy feedback you would catch at a glance but persuasive feedback that quotes a sentence the student never wrote or praises a misconception warmly. Only someone who knows both the student and the concept catches that — you, at the gate.

Second, the defaults have to be overridden every single time. Left alone, "the AI does the opposite of what I want. Its native register is to judge: right or wrong, here is the corrected version, moving on. Pointed at a partial answer, it calls the answer deficient. Pointed at a divergent answer... it calls it wrong." The register instructions "are not set once; they have to be reasserted, and checked, against the grain of what the tool wants to do. The work of telling it where its pedagogical lines are is itself a pedagogical act, and it belongs to the teacher." In practice: the prompts ride along in every run — in the DRAFT slide's speaker notes for students, in your batch instruction for reports — and you check outputs against the register on every batch, not only the first one.

Third, the default configuration harms the students already being harmed. As Katherine puts it: "Most teachers using AI right now are not overriding the defaults. They are using it the way it ships: as a generator of correct outputs and a scorer of student performance against them. Used that way, AI does not relieve the problem... It accelerates it, with a friendly interface and the word 'personalized' attached. The students who were already misread will be misread faster, more confidently, and at greater scale." It is the same tool either way, and "The pedagogy underneath it is the whole difference."

The boundary that holds all of this together: "AI helps make the work doable. It is not the doer." What it does is production — drafts, reports, differentiated versions, assessments made accessible for students with an IEP. "None of that is teaching. Teaching is the shared object, the dialogic catch, the read on who is lurking and who is ready to be pushed, the moment a student's face changes when she finds out her thinking has weight. The AI does none of that, and it must not be allowed to, because that part is what students need from a person and from no one else."

One more caveat: "I am not describing a product, and I am not recommending a particular tool... The principle is portable; the particular way I do it probably is not." What this section gives you is the prompts, the register, the report standards, and the gate. Which AI you run them through is your call; every part of the system works with any capable general-purpose model.

The integrity gate, briefly

Authentic feedback requires authentic work, so the batch checks the first answer — only the first answer — before it drafts anything. A first answer that appears copied or AI-written is flagged for your review, and the flag holds that student's completion entry; nothing posts automatically, and no answer is ever auto-failed. An AI-written first answer is handled by interrogation, like everything else here: the student is coached to question and improve it, and it is rated on the same proficiency scale alongside the revision, so the change is visible. The revision is exempt by design — students are told to use their AI there. Flags stay visible and you decide, since detection misfires, most often on students learning English and writers with unusual style; and because the gradebook records only completion, a wrong flag costs one cycle's feedback, not a grade. The full policy, including what not to police, is in the grading section.

The honest workload framing

What the system costs and what it buys, in Katherine's own numbers, with the hedge she attaches to them:

"The production work — the slides, the differentiated versions, the feedback drafts, the IEP-accessible assessments — is roughly forty percent of what my job used to demand of my hours. When AI takes on that forty percent, the time comes back to me, and it goes into presence: standing at the counter, crouching beside a stuck student, running the dialogic register for more of the room. My rough sense is that this shows up as somewhere between a twenty and sixty percent increase in the time I actually spend present with students, depending on the week. I want to be plain that this is an estimate from my own practice — a feel, not a measurement. I offer it because the rough proportion is the point, not because the number is precise."

Notice what the freed time is for: not more production, not a shorter workday, but presence. The reading time the gate costs is real — a class set of reports read word by word — but it replaces composing thirty pieces of feedback from nothing with checking thirty drafts against what you already know about each student, and the promise still holds: no stack of papers to grade. If you are tempted to skip the reading, read the three cautions again. The system without its gate is simply the default configuration, and the default configuration is the thing this system exists to refuse.

Using Classroom Technology to Notice Thinking

This section covers the technology in the room while you teach: the smartboard, and any screen monitoring software your school has installed on student devices. LMS setup and deck distribution live in the platform guide; the feedback pipeline lives in the section on AI feedback and conceptual growth. Everything below assumes you have read the opening section and know how a cycle is shaped.

A few terms first. A cycle is one complete unit of this curriculum, normally three class days. The deck is the slide file each student works in — the one artifact they build, write in, and hand back. A response slide is where students write an answer in their own words; what they write before instruction resolves the question is their first answer, and on the third day they write a revised answer directly underneath it, leaving the first version showing.

The test for any classroom tool

Katherine's test is short enough to hold in your head while deciding whether to open a piece of software: the difference that matters is "the difference between a tool that returns me to them and a tool that takes me further away." Everything in this section follows from that sentence.

What the tool is being asked to extend, in her own words: "The work has moved from managing activities to noticing agency. My practices have become simpler — two things per class, real questions, thinking rewarded rather than correctness. What has become more complex is what I notice. I am watching differently now — listening for the beginning of interpretation, for the moment a student starts to revise what they thought, for the places where the room opens and the places where it closes down."

What she does with that noticing is the part no machine takes over: "The vehicle can hold the room. It cannot answer his specific question. Only a person can do that, in real time, in the seconds before the moment closes." So the standard is not classroom control but something narrower: does the tool let you read more of the room's thinking, sooner, so you can respond while the moment is still open? A dashboard aimed at catching off-task tabs is policing behavior; the same dashboard aimed at thirty first answers as they are written is extending your noticing — and the difference shapes what students believe class is for.

Keep one more line in view, because monitoring software invites the opposite habit: "Students feel when teaching becomes accelerated management rather than shared attention. They experience the difference between a teacher who is cognitively available and one who is merely trying to survive the period."

Where the noticing moments are in every cycle

The deck builds writing moments into every block, and every writing moment is a chance to read the room. Cycle 02, Ecosystems and Feeding Relationships, is the free unit, so it serves as the example here.

Day 1 opens with a bellringer, the short question waiting on screen as students walk in: "Think about the last meal you ate. Where did the energy in that food originally come from? Write your best guess." Thirty guesses appear while you take attendance. Soon after comes the first response slide, where students commit in writing at each step of grass to cow to you — do you get all of the grass's energy, or something else, and why? This is the richest read of the cycle: first answers are working models written before anyone has been told anything. Then a rule-building slide sends the same stem to every student: "Finish this sentence as a rule: 'Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ______.'" One sentence per student, all on the same stem, is the fastest possible picture of where the room actually is.

Later on Day 1 comes the decomposer slide, the cycle's pattern break — a case deliberately chosen to break the too-simple rule students have just built, here the idea that energy simply passes upward. What students write in the minutes afterward tells you who is revising and who is defending. Across Days 1 and 2, the three-tier concept questions offer the same concept at three entry points, labelled Getting Started, Working On It, and Mastery; the tier a student chooses, and what they do inside it, is information in itself. Day 2 brings a second first answer (a bee and a flower contrasted with a mosquito and a human) and a second rule-building sentence. Day 3 is revision, written directly under the first answer; watching those revisions appear is watching the point of the cycle happen, and it is where you read whether the class is ready to move on.

Every thinking slide carries the same instruction line: "Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write." Three channels, asking different things of you: the silence is not to be interrupted, the table talk is to be listened to, and the writing is to be read. Most of what follows is about reading the writing at scale; the last part of this section covers the first two channels.

The smartboard

Katherine's habits with projected content are worth copying: "My slides carry a fair amount of text — to support students who were absent or are reviewing — but I scan each one and then talk freely. Reading the slides as written would be deadening. I highlight important words with my marker. I draw where it helps."

That is the whole philosophy. The projection is a shared object the room looks at together, not a script you perform, and the deck is built for that use: day dividers, bellringers, CASE A and CASE B contrasts side by side so the comparison is visible at a glance, and simulation links you can open for the whole class. Mark it up and draw the arrows.

Project answers, never students. The smartboard is the natural place to put student thinking in front of the room, and a few habits make that safe. Copy two or three first answers or rule sentences into a blank slide, names stripped, before you show anything. First answers are developing thinking, written under a promise of safety — the grading rule that completion means both answers are present exists precisely so students will risk being wrong. Projecting a named wrong answer breaks that promise once, and the writing in your room gets cautious for the rest of the year.

Choose the answers you project for what they notice, not for what they get wrong. Katherine's stance is that partial understandings are "partial understandings of something, whose divergent answers are evidence of what they are noticing." Put the answer that says the cow uses some energy to walk around next to the one that says energy passes up the chain, and the room sees for itself that the first has hold of something the second has not found yet.

Compare responses as a class using the rule-building stems — the stem does the anonymizing for you. Three completions of "Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next" can go up side by side — it gets smaller, ninety percent is lost as heat, the animals higher up need more food — and then you ask which rule survives the decomposer slide. That is the deck's contrast move run on the students' own sentences, so the contrast does the teaching and nobody is singled out. If one student's answer really is worth the room's attention, ask that student first, privately; the decision stays theirs.

Screen monitoring systems

Most schools that issue student laptops issue a monitoring system alongside them — GoGuardian Teacher, Hapara, Lightspeed, Securly, or LanSchool. These tools arrive framed as surveillance: closing tabs, blocking sites, off-task alerts. Used only that way, the tool takes you further from your students — a watcher of screens instead of a watcher of the room. Pointed instead at the writing moments the deck has already built, the same dashboard lets you read every developing answer while it is still forming. That is the use described here, in the order a cycle tends to produce the moves.

  1. Read the responses while students are writing. At a first answer slide, open the dashboard's grid of student screens rather than circulating cold. In two or three minutes you can read every answer as it forms: who has a working model, who has a blank box, who has written a single hedged word. Katherine's standard for this read: "I am watching not for accuracy but for whether students have a working model at all — whether they can produce a sentence that shows genuine contact with the concept." The dashboard simply applies that standard to thirty screens instead of the six desks you can physically reach.
  2. Identify misunderstandings early. You already know each cycle's predictable wrong turns — they are what the lesson's critical aspects were chosen against. In Cycle 02, watch the forming answers for: the claim that you get all of the grass's energy (no loss at all); the claim that the energy is destroyed (loss but no destination); the judgment that mosquitoes are parasites because they are bad (a moral verdict in place of an outcome); and decomposers slotted in as a fourth trophic level. Spotting three screens making the same wrong turn while students are still writing — rather than tonight while you read the decks — is the entire payoff of the tool.
  3. Send a private message. Most systems can push a short message to one screen. Use the register the rest of the system runs on: no deficit language, never the answer itself. Quote something the student has right, then ask a question — "'The cow uses energy to move' — yes. Where does that energy go after the cow uses it?" or "Your rule says energy passes upward. Check the decomposer slide before you settle." A private nudge reaches the student who would never raise a hand, and costs them nothing in front of their table.
  4. Pull one student aside for two minutes. When a screen shows a student genuinely stuck rather than thinking, call them over or crouch beside their desk — the digital form of a habit Katherine describes directly: "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it." The dashboard moves that hunt from after grading to during writing, while the moment is still open. Keep it short and two-sided: name one thing their answer has right, ask one question, send them back.
  5. Send that student back to teach their table. When the conversation lands — you will see it in the face and hear it in the sentence — send them back with a job: "Explain that to your table. See if they agree." Teaching the explanation is the strongest evidence the idea took hold, and it matches what Katherine listens for: "you can hear who is beginning to geek out by listening for the student who is teaching." It also turns a student who was pulled aside into someone who knows something, in front of their peers.
  6. Harvest thinking for the front of the room. The dashboard speeds up the smartboard moves above: copy two or three forming answers, names stripped, into a blank slide while students are still writing, then run the comparison the moment the writing ends. The same rules apply — answers rather than names, chosen for what they notice, rule stems framing the comparison.
  7. Show the room its writing is seen, without singling anyone out. Report patterns, not people: "About half the rules in this room say energy disappears, and the other half say where it goes. That difference is the next slide." Never project the dashboard grid itself, and never name an off-task screen from the front of the room — walk over instead. Proximity was the move before this software existed and is still the better one: "I move among students during activities and labs — talking over aspects together, noticing their ideas, crouching beside someone who is stuck."
  8. Decide whether the class is ready to move forward. This is the decision the whole scan serves. Katherine's criterion: "I calibrate by feel: after a few slides of lurking, do I start to see enough confidence to risk an answer out loud? When answers become more thought-out and less random, we move. When they don't, I don't push." The dashboard turns that feel into thirty sentences you have actually read. If most rule sentences survive the pattern break, advance; if the same wrong turn sits on a third of the screens, stay and reteach the contrast rather than the vocabulary. On Day 3 the revised answers give the same read at the scale of the cycle: revision happening across the room means the class is ready for the next one; first answers copied unchanged into the revision box mean it is not.

What a dashboard cannot tell you. A dashboard reads activity, and activity is exactly what Katherine's framework says not to trust: "The student with their head down has often been mislabeled disengaged when they were lurking." A still screen during the "Think first" stretch may well be "the silence of genuine thinking — of students working out what they believe before risking it. That working-out is exactly what I want." A tab that looks off task during an open exploration window may belong to a student who has finished. Read screens for thinking, not compliance, and when the dashboard and your own read of a student disagree, trust the student. The deepest harm the book names is misreading a student, and a dashboard misreads faster and more confidently than a person does.

One boundary applies to all of this: nothing described above sends student first answers or revisions anywhere. The scan is you reading your own students' work in your own room. The AI step stays where the system puts it — on Day 3, gated by you, through the three-step coaching prompt in the speaker notes of the deck's DRAFT slide, the one slide where the student assembles their answers — and the monitoring system plays no part in it.

When the devices are not there

Every move above has a paper-and-feet version, and those versions came first. If your room has no monitoring system, or the network is down — which at some point it will be — the cycle runs unchanged.

The clipboard lap. During first answer writing, walk the room with your class list and mark one of three things beside each name: an answer is forming, the box is blank, or come back to this one. A single lap covers the room in the two or three minutes the dashboard would have taken. The roster is what keeps the quiet middle of the room from slipping past you — the exact group Katherine built the written feedback for, described as "the shy student making real progress who never volunteers."

Whiteboard reveal. For the compare-as-a-class move, use small dry-erase boards, or a torn half sheet of paper. Everyone writes their completion of the rule sentence and holds it up on the count of three. Revealing together means nobody answers alone, you read thirty rules in about ten seconds, and you can pull two contrasting boards to the front for the comparison you would have projected. It works identically for the CASE A and CASE B commitments: choose A or B and hold it up.

Listen during partner talk. The instruction line has already scheduled your listening time, since "Discuss with a partner" sits between thinking and writing. Stand near a table and listen without joining in, for the same two things the screens would have shown: the wrong turn currently circulating, and the student who has started teaching. Two tables of listening usually predicts what the whole room is about to write.

Paper exit slips. The rule-building stem is a ready-made slip: put "Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next" on a quarter sheet and collect it at the door. Sort the stack into piles by wrong turn while your next class works its bellringer; the size of those piles is your decision about tomorrow. On Day 3 the equivalent slip is one sentence asking what changed between the first answer and the revised one.

None of this is a degraded fallback. These versions keep you at the tables, crouched beside the student who is stuck — which is where the technology was supposed to return you in the first place.

Supplies and the Master Supply List

Answerable Biology ships with a spreadsheet called the Master Supply System, and this section explains what it does and how to keep it healthy. The idea behind it is simple: you maintain one editable list of everything the year needs, and that single list produces every quarter checklist, the full year shopping view, and the cost totals, so that you are never maintaining supply information in five different places.

The one rule

Edit only the MASTER sheet. Every other sheet in the workbook, meaning the four quarter views, the full year view, and the costs sheet, is built from formulas that read MASTER. Change a quantity, a class count, or a price once, and every view and every total updates on its own. If you ever catch yourself retyping a supply into a second list, whether that is a lab prep sheet, a shopping note, or a quarter plan, stop and use one of the views instead. Two lists always drift apart eventually, and the one that drifted is reliably the one you grab on lab day.

This is the same pattern the rest of the system runs on. The deck is the single student artifact, the Master Agenda is the single operating calendar, and MASTER is the single supply inventory. The Master Agenda and each Unit Agenda carry a one-line supply summary for each cycle so you can see at a glance what is coming, but the full checklists live in the workbook and nowhere else.

What is in the workbook

  • README is a short version of this section kept inside the workbook itself, including the legend for which cells you are meant to edit.
  • MASTER holds one row for every supply in every cycle. The columns give you the quarter, unit, cycle, lesson or deck, activity, supply, quantity per group, groups per class, number of classes, the calculated total, whether the item is reusable or consumable, preparation, storage, safety, approximate cost, a substitute, and a store link where the original activity supplied one. Changing your class counts

Two columns are yours to change, and they are shaded blue in the workbook so you can find them quickly: Groups per class, which arrives set to 8, and Number of classes, which arrives set to 3. The Total needed column is a formula that multiplies quantity per group by groups per class by number of classes, so changing either blue number updates the total and the quarter views, and nothing else needs touching.

Two deliberate conventions keep that arithmetic honest, and it is worth knowing why they are there before you decide something looks wrong. The first is that reusable rows have the number of classes set to 1, because one physical set serves every section. The Pacific Northwest card sets from Cycle 02 go back into their zip bags and come out again next block, and again in Cycle 07; the Cycle 05 goggles are sanitized between classes; the same eight lamps light every section's leaf disk lab. Multiplying reusable items by your class count would have you buying three of everything you only own once. If you teach five sections rather than three, your consumables scale up and your card sets do not.

The second convention is that rows describing something shared across the whole class have groups per class set to 1, because a teacher demonstration or a shared stock is not a per group item. One borrowed fish runs the bromothymol blue demonstration in Cycle 06 for every class you teach, and one box of baking soda mixes the leaf disk solution for all of them. Those rows say as much in their preparation cell, so you are not left guessing.

Handout rows assume four students per group, so a quantity per group of 4 means one copy per student, and eight groups of four comes to thirty-two students. If your sections run larger than that, raise the quantity on the row rather than starting a second list somewhere else.

Reusable, consumable, and what the costs sheet tells you

Every row is marked either reusable or consumable. Reusable items are the ones you buy once and keep, such as the card sets, goggles, lamps, molecular model kits, pop beads, and beans. Consumable items are rebought each year, and they include spinach, hydrogen peroxide, yeast, balloons, PTC taste strips, and pipe cleaners, along with every printed handout, which costs nothing beyond the school copier.

The costs sheet splits the money along that line, so you can see what starting up costs you once, what each September costs after that, and which quarter each purchase belongs to. The expensive lines are few, and all of them carry a cheap substitute on their own row in MASTER: window light or a couple of shared lamps in place of a full lamp set, paper circles and tape in place of molecular model kits, and chained paper clips in place of pop bead kits. Treat the prices as rough estimates rather than quotes, since they were entered to help you plan a budget rather than to place an order.

The shape of the year is worth knowing before you plan your spending. The first quarter ends with the year's first wet lab in Cycle 05, which is where the goggles and lamps get bought. The second quarter is the wet lab stretch, with the fish and bromothymol blue demonstration, the yeast balloons, and the potato and peroxide work. The third quarter is genetics and is almost entirely printing and laminating, apart from the pop beads. The fourth quarter needs the Cycle 18 kit of beans, beaks, and beads, and then finishes with a capstone that needs nothing but paper.

That is what the quarter views filter on and what the per quarter cost breakdown is built from. Four abbreviations run through the NGSS documents and through this section. A performance expectation, abbreviated PE, is the standard itself, stated as something students should be able to do. Every performance expectation is built from one core idea, one practice, and one crosscutting concept.

Where the assessment evidence lives

Every cycle produces one primary artifact, which is the finished student deck with the first answer and the revised answer sitting side by side on each response slide. Grading separates two different things. Completion means both answers are present, and it records participation in thinking. Proficiency means demonstrated understanding, and it is read from the Conceptual Growth Report and the teacher's class summary rather than from the grade in the gradebook. The rule students see, in the words the decks use, is "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." AI drafts the feedback and the reports, and you read everything before it reaches a student, which is a deliberate part of the design rather than a convenience.

The practices are evidenced by particular pieces of student work, and it helps to be able to name them.

Constructing explanations is the practice the whole arc is built to produce, and the artifact captures it twice for each critical aspect of the concept. Students commit to a first explanation in their own words, as in Cycle 02, where they are asked whether they get all of the grass's energy at each step or something else, and why. They then compress that explanation into a rule by completing the sentence "Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ______." They test that rule against a pattern break, meaning a case chosen to break the too-simple rule they have just built, such as decomposers eating dead things at every level, and are asked whether the rule still holds. Finally they write a revised explanation directly under the first. The pair of answers is a before and after record of explanation construction, which is to say it is not a claim that explanation happened but the two versions themselves.

Developing and using models shows up in nearly every cycle. Students drag the transfer efficiency slider in the tank and flow models and watch what happens to the energy tanks, build a food web over two days using species cards and do the pyramid math inside it, build a membrane bilayer, assemble sugar molecules from six molecules of carbon dioxide and six of water with every atom accounted for, twist pipe cleaners into chromosomes, and run the replication assembly line. The products of that work are collectable evidence, and the response slides show you whether the model actually changed the student's explanation.

Analyzing and interpreting data comes directly out of the cycles that generate data: the energy math that traces ten thousand units through four trophic levels, leaf disk float counts against a control with no carbon dioxide, catalase rate graphed against temperature and pH, the moth hunt catch data, the bean population lab across three rounds of predation, the Gene Pool Beads allele counts across four generations, and the Chargaff percentages. The tiered questions then ask students to reason from that data, and the entry level question in Cycle 02 is itself a data question, asking how many of a meadow's ten thousand calories reach the hawks and telling students to show their math.

Using mathematics and computational thinking appears in the ten percent rule computations in Cycles 02 and 03, the four level energy math, the gamete combinations challenge built on two to the twenty-third power, Punnett square probability, and the allele frequency counts in Cycle 18.

Engaging in argument from evidence is what the third day of each cycle is designed around. Cycle 03 asks for a short claim with reasoning rather than just an answer, Cycle 02 asks students to predict what happens when the web breaks and then defend the prediction, and Cycle 20 asks for a defense of a cost and benefit trade-off alongside a one page proposal grounded in evidence.

The tiered questions do a second job as differentiated evidence. Every concept question offers Getting Started, Working On It, and Mastery as entry points, with a word bank students may use, modify, or ignore. The tier a student answers at, the movement between tiers from the first answer to the revised one, and the shift across the Then and Now self rating together show where that student sits on the practice, all on one scale and without separate tracks.

For administrators and observers

This part is written so that you can hand it to an evaluator or use it in a pre-observation conference. Nothing below assumes any prior knowledge of the system.

The model in one paragraph. Answerable Biology runs each core idea through an arc of commitment, development, and revision, grounded in Variation Theory. Students commit to a first explanation in writing before instruction resolves the question. The lesson then develops the idea through deliberately designed contrast cases and a pattern break, meaning a case that breaks the too-simple rule the students have just built. Students then revise their original answer, leaving the first version visible beside it. Formative work is graded on completion so that students can safely expose thinking that is still developing, and proficiency is judged separately by the teacher from a written growth record produced for every student in every cycle.

What an observer will see in the room.

  • Students writing developing explanations in their own words. Question slides stop the lesson and require a written formulation before any answer has been given. The teacher deliberately does not resolve the question from the front of the room; students check the slides, discuss with a partner, or come and ask. Every thinking slide carries the same instruction, which is to think first, discuss with a partner, and then write. Silence during those stretches is thinking rather than disengagement.
  • Contrast cases doing the teaching. Concepts are taught by holding two cases side by side that differ in exactly one respect, labelled CASE A and CASE B, and then introducing a case that breaks the rule students have just built. In the free unit that means a bee and a flower beside a mosquito and a human, followed by an orchid that tricks bees into pollinating it for nothing, with the question of whether the rule still works if you apply it carefully.
  • A visible revision loop. On the third day students return to their first answers and revise them on the same slide, with both versions left showing. The deck is the record, so an observer can pick up any student's deck and watch the thinking change.
  • Tiered questions on a single scale. Every concept question offers three entry points, labelled Getting Started, Working On It, and Mastery, with an optional word bank. All students face the same concept, and the differentiation is in how they enter it rather than in separate tracks or visibly different tasks.
  • The teacher among the students. Direct instruction is short and comes early; the teacher then circulates through the activity or lab, questioning, noticing, and crouching beside students who are stuck. The design allows two things per class period, done well: one conceptual lesson and one activity aimed at the same critical aspect.
  • Purposeful entry and transition work. A simulation or exploration task is open on screens when students arrive and fills transitions and early finisher time, so that arrival minutes and waiting time are productive by design rather than by luck.

How this maps to common evaluation language. Objectives are on the board and on the teacher reference slide of each deck, with the performance expectations on that same slide and collected in this section. Engagement is structural, since every student commits an answer on every question slide and participation is not sampled by hand raising. Checks for understanding happen every cycle, per student, because the first answers are read and they drive the feedback students receive on the third day. Differentiation runs through the tiered questions, the word banks, the choice between core and extension decks according to the pace of the class, and phrasing that is accessible to students with individualized education programs, so that the conceptual bar stays fixed while linguistic obstacles are removed. Higher order thinking is built into the rule building, the pattern breaks, and the what-if questions, none of which have an answer available to copy, because each requires applying a distinction to a new case. Feedback reaches every student in writing each cycle, targeted and non-evaluative, drafted with AI and then read and adjusted by the teacher before release, and it is followed by a required revision. Data comes from the class summary, which gives the teacher a read for each cycle of who has each idea and who does not, and from the Growth Reports, which document individual growth across the year.

One caution for observers. Completion grades on formative work are deliberate rather than lax. Grading developing answers for correctness teaches students to hide incomplete thinking, and this system needs the incomplete thinking on paper, because that is precisely what the feedback and the revision work on. The rigor lives in the proficiency read, which is the Growth Report and the revised answers, rather than in red ink on first drafts. In Katherine's own words to her students: "I care about your thinking, not the answer. 'Because' is not an answer. 'I don't know' is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy."
