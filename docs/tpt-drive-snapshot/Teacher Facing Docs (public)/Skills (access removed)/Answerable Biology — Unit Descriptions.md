## The through-line (what makes every unit the same — and defensible)

Every unit runs each core idea through one **Variation Theory** arc: students commit to a **first answer**, the lesson develops the idea through deliberate **contrasts** and a **pattern-break** (a case that breaks the too-simple rule), and then students **revise**. The finished deck — first answer beside revised answer — is the single artifact that runs through one AI feedback step to produce a **one-page Conceptual Growth Report** for each student and a **class summary** for you. Students see how their thinking changed; you see what the class is ready for next. No stack of papers to grade.

**What every deck is built with (across all units):**

- **Plain language.** Questions are written plainly, because tangled wording measures reading, not the concept (Abedi & Lord, 2001).
- **Clean, not busy, graphics.** Simple visuals keep students' attention on the thinking rather than on decoration.
- **A simple demo first,** where the content has one — students see the phenomenon before they are asked to name it.
- **A stock-and-flow (Tank-and-Fill) model,** used only where the content actually has a quantity that accumulates and drains (Meadows, 2008) — never forced onto a unit that doesn't.
- **Beyond-the-frame questions** that push students to name a concept's critical aspect in their own words and use it to explain effects on ecology, evolution, or the organism.
- **Longer and shorter activities embedded for long and short days,** so a deck flexes to a full block or a 45-minute period without rebuilding the lesson.

**Suggested settings for each listing:** Grades 9–12; Subjects Biology + Basic Principles (Science); Format PPTX; Tags such as Lessons, Activities, Critical Thinking and Problem Solving. Price per your premium-anchor plan (these are paid units, not free).

## 1. Course Launch & Lab Safety (Cycle 1)

**Title (64):** Course Launch & Lab Safety | Back-to-School Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
Cycle 1 is where the class becomes a place students will actually think out loud — before any biology content is at stake. It builds the platform the whole year runs on, in two parts. *How we think:* the year's single throughline (life needs matter and energy, and moves them around), an honest pretest students aren't meant to know yet, and their first writing about why they're here and how biology connects to their own lives. *How we work:* lab safety, and a classroom that runs on students rather than on the teacher.

Students meet the same first-answer-then-revise routine they will use all year, but on classroom culture and safety, so the routine is familiar before the biology gets hard. The jobs system turns the room into a working lab: about 21 roles across daily operations, lab operations, and governance, which students apply and interview for, with a small Democratic Board for the agreements the class makes together.

*What's included:* the Course Launch deck (welcome, year roadmap, the pretest, and relate-to-me writing); a Lab Safety deck plus a Lab Safety Symbols reference, study guide, and activity; the student Jobs decks (overview, job descriptions, applications and interviews); and a teacher guide to running the jobs board and hiring rounds.

## 2. Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships

**Title (65):** Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
This is the unit that sets up the whole year in ecology: organisms are connected to each other by the transfer of matter and energy through feeding relationships. Rather than have students memorize a vocabulary list of producers and consumers, this deck has them build the connections themselves — a food web they start on Day 1 and finish on Day 2 — and then reason about what those connections mean.

Over three class blocks, students work two critical aspects — **energy flow through trophic levels** (a trophic level is defined by *how* an organism gets its energy) and **symbiosis** (how two species affect each other) — committing to a first answer, testing it against contrasts and a pattern-break, then revising. The finished deck becomes each student's Conceptual Growth Report and your class summary: who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed.

*What's included:* a 32-slide 3-block deck; a two-day food-web build; a bobtail-squid symbiosis investigation; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the first-answer/revised-answer response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 3. Energy Flow & Trophic Pyramids

**Title (64):** Energy Flow & Trophic Pyramids | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
The idea students most often miss about food chains is *why they are short* — and the answer is quantitative. This unit builds toward the 10% rule: energy decreases at each step because most of it is lost as heat, so only about a tenth passes to the next level. That number is what explains why you rarely see a fifth or sixth link in a chain.

Across three blocks, students reason through two critical aspects — **producers are the only entry point for energy** and **only about 10% of energy passes to the next level** — from a first answer, through contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer, doing the energy math themselves. Each student's Growth Report and your class summary show who can predict what limits a chain's length and who is still treating each level as if it kept all its energy.

*What's included:* a 28-slide 3-block deck; an interactive energy-pyramid model; the Energy Flow Stations activity; a "do the energy math" 10%-rule activity; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 4. Cycles of Matter: The Carbon Cycle

**Title (63):** The Carbon Cycle | NGSS HS Biology Ecology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
This unit turns on one contrast that reorganizes everything students think about ecosystems: unlike energy, matter is not lost. Carbon cycles continuously between the air, living things, and the Earth, so the same atoms are used again and again. Setting that against the one-way flow of energy from the previous unit is what makes each idea finally hold still.

Over three blocks, students reason through two critical aspects — **carbon moves from the air into living things** (the surprising source of a tree's mass) and **matter cycles while energy does not** — from a first answer, through deliberate contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer that connects the carbon cycle to photosynthesis and respiration. The growth loop reports who can trace a carbon atom through the cycle and who is still confusing what cycles with what runs out.

*What's included:* a 28-slide 3-block deck; an interactive carbon-cycle model; the "Where do trees get their mass?" investigation; a "journey through the carbon cycle" activity; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 5. Photosynthesis

**Title (56):** Photosynthesis | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
Two ideas about photosynthesis are genuinely hard, and this unit is built to hold students to both. The first is that light energy becomes chemical energy stored in glucose — an energy conversion, not just "plants making food." The second is more counterintuitive still: a plant builds its body from air and water, not from soil. The unit uses Van Helmont's classic willow-tree experiment to force that realization.

Across three blocks, students work two critical aspects — **light energy becomes chemical energy in glucose** and **a plant's material comes from air and water, not soil** — from a first answer, through contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer, testing their thinking against the leaf-disk float lab and the willow-tree evidence. Each student's Growth Report and your class summary show who can explain where a plant's mass actually comes from and who still believes it eats the soil.

*What's included:* a 31-slide 3-block deck; the Leaf-Disk Float lab; the Van Helmont willow-tree investigation; a build-a-sugar-molecule activity; three-tier concept questions with word banks; an optional stretch challenge; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 6. Cellular Respiration & Fermentation

**Title (66):** Cellular Respiration & Fermentation | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
This unit completes the energy story photosynthesis began: cells release the energy stored in glucose, and respiration is essentially photosynthesis run in reverse. The second idea gives students the "why" behind a familiar experience — when oxygen runs out, fermentation is the backup pathway, which is what makes muscles burn and what makes bread rise.

Over three blocks, students reason through two critical aspects — **cells release glucose's energy slowly** and **oxygen lets a cell get far more ATP** (with fermentation as the no-oxygen backup) — from a first answer, through contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer that treats respiration as the reverse of photosynthesis. The growth loop reports who can explain why oxygen matters to a cell's energy yield and who is still treating fermentation as a separate, unrelated process.

*What's included:* a 29-slide 3-block deck; an interactive respiration model; the "Releasing the Energy in Food" activity; the Yeast Balloon Fermentation lab; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 7. Population Ecology & Succession

**Title:** Population Ecology & Succession | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
Populations are where ecology stops being about single organisms and becomes about numbers over time. This unit builds two linked ideas: why a population's size levels off instead of growing forever, and how a community rebuilds itself after disturbance. It is designed so resistant learners get solid footing on the core idea, with a second deck to extend the unit when a class moves faster.

Students work each idea through the Variation Theory arc — a first answer, deliberate contrasts and a pattern-break, then a revision. The core deck builds carrying capacity and the limiting factors that hold a population in check; the extension deck builds ecological succession — how a bare lot or rock is recolonized over time. Your class summary shows who can explain what stops a population from filling the screen and who still expects unlimited growth.

*What's included (choose by pace):* **Population Ecology** (44-slide deck; carrying capacity and limiting factors) and **Populations & Succession** (35-slide deck; ecological succession). Bellringers, contrast sets, three-tier concept questions with word banks, and the VT writing slides that feed the growth report. Use the core deck alone for a resistant-learner pace, or add the second to extend.

## 9. Cell Membrane & Transport

**Title (63):** Cell Membrane & Transport | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
Most cell-membrane resources ask students to label a diagram. This one asks them to *reason* from structure to function. Across three class blocks, students build one idea they can actually use: the membrane is selective because of what it is made of, and its structure predicts what can and cannot cross.

Students commit to a first answer, then work through two critical aspects — **selective permeability** and **bilayer structure** — using an interactive membrane model and side-by-side contrasts, before revising their thinking. The finished deck becomes each student's Conceptual Growth Report and a class summary for you: who understands why a large or charged molecule is stopped, and who still needs the next contrast.

*What's included:* a 28-slide 3-block deck; an interactive bilayer "tank" model; hands-on activities; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the first-answer/revised-answer response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 10. Enzymes

**Title (46):** Enzymes | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
Enzymes are where students first meet the idea of a biological catalyst, and two things trip them up: that an enzyme lowers a reaction's activation energy *without being used up*, and that its specific shape is what makes it work at all. This unit holds them to both.

Across three blocks, students reason through two critical aspects — **enzymes lower activation energy and aren't consumed** and **shape, specificity, and denaturing** (the lock-and-key fit, and how heat or pH changes the shape and stops the enzyme) — from a first answer, through contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer. Each student's Growth Report and your class summary show who can explain why a denatured enzyme stops working and who still thinks the enzyme is consumed in the reaction.

*What's included:* a 30-slide 3-block deck; the Activation-Energy Hill model; a Lock & Key matching activity; a Temperature & pH denaturing lab; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 11. The Cell Cycle → Cancer

**Title (57):** Cell Cycle & Cancer | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
Students often memorize the phases of the cell cycle and never grasp what the cycle is *for*. This unit builds the idea that division is tightly regulated — and that cancer is what happens when that regulation fails. That single throughline turns a vocabulary unit into a cause-and-effect story students can explain.

Over three blocks, students reason through three critical aspects — **checkpoints control the cycle**, **benign vs. malignant**, and **cancer = regulation failure** — committing to a first answer, testing it against deliberate contrasts and a pattern-break, then revising. Their growth on the mechanism shows up in each student's Growth Report and your class summary.

*What's included:* a 29-slide 3-block deck; an interactive model; activities; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 12. Reproduction & Meiosis

**Title:** Reproduction & Meiosis | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
Meiosis asks students to hold two things at once: a bookkeeping fact — that the chromosome number is cut in half, 46 to 23 — and the reason it matters, that this halving and reshuffling is where genetic variation comes from. This unit is built for resistant learners to secure the process first, with a second deck to connect it to reproduction and variation when a class is ready.

Students work each idea through the Variation Theory arc — first answer, contrasts and a pattern-break, revision. The core deck builds the process itself (Meiosis I and Meiosis II, and why a cell that starts with 46 chromosomes ends with 23); the companion deck connects meiosis to sexual reproduction and the variation it produces. The growth report shows who can explain why gametes carry half the chromosome number and who is still treating meiosis like ordinary cell division.

*What's included (choose by pace):* **The Process of Meiosis** (27-slide deck; Meiosis I and II, chromosome number, Pop Bead Meiosis lab) and **Reproduction & Meiosis** (41-slide deck; sexual reproduction and the variation meiosis creates). Bellringers, contrast sets, three-tier concept questions with word banks, and the VT writing slides that feed the growth report.

## 13. Mendelian Genetics & Punnett Squares

**Title:** Mendelian Genetics & Punnett Squares | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
Inheritance is where students most often collect rules they can't reason with — "dominant means stronger," "common means dominant." This unit is built to dismantle exactly those confusions and put probability in their hands. The core deck secures Mendelian inheritance for resistant learners; the extension deck adds Punnett squares and carriers for classes ready to push further.

Students work each idea through the Variation Theory arc — first answer, contrasts and a pattern-break, revision. The core deck separates inherited from acquired traits and breaks the "common = dominant" misconception; the extension deck builds the Punnett square as a probability tool and the idea of a carrier. The growth report shows who can predict a cross and explain why a recessive trait can skip a generation, and who is still ranking alleles by strength.

*What's included (choose by pace):* **Mendelian Genetics** (28-slide deck; inherited vs. acquired, common ≠ dominant) and **Genetics Continued — Punnett Squares** (86-slide deck; Punnett = probability, carriers; cheek-cell lab). Bellringers, contrast sets, three-tier concept questions with word banks, and the VT writing slides that feed the growth report.

## 14. DNA Structure & Replication

**Title (65):** DNA Structure & Replication | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
The point of DNA's structure is not the picture — it is that the structure is *what makes faithful copying possible*. This unit builds exactly that connection: base pairing (A–T, C–G) means each strand is a template, which is why replication is semiconservative and accurate.

Across three blocks, students work two critical aspects — **each strand is a template** and **semiconservative replication** — from a first answer, through contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer that explains the mechanism, not just the diagram. The growth loop reports who can connect structure to copying and who is still describing the shape.

*What's included:* a 28-slide 3-block deck; an interactive model; activities; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 15. Genes, Chromosomes & Protein Synthesis

**Title (69):** Genes, Chromosomes & Protein Synthesis | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
This unit takes students from how a trait is *stored* to how the code is *read*. The foundational deck makes the vocabulary hold still — what a gene is, what a chromosome is, and how *gene*, *locus*, and *allele* nest together instead of blurring into synonyms. The extension deck builds the central dogma: how the stored code is transcribed and translated into the proteins that actually do the work. It is built so resistant learners secure the storage idea first, with the protein-synthesis deck to extend into how the code is expressed.

Students work each idea through the Variation Theory arc — first answer, contrasts and a pattern-break, revision. The core deck reasons through three critical aspects — **a gene is a section of DNA and a chromosome carries many genes**, **a gene sits at a fixed address (locus) on homologous chromosomes**, and **alleles are different versions of the same gene**; the extension deck builds transcription and translation as one directed flow from gene to protein. The growth report shows who can place a gene inside a chromosome and trace the code to a protein, and who is still using the terms as synonyms.

*What's included (choose by pace):* **Genes & Chromosomes** (34-slide deck; gene/locus/allele as a nested system; pipe-cleaner chromosome build, gene map, class allele survey) and **Protein Synthesis: The Central Dogma** (transcription and translation — how the stored code becomes a protein; transcription modeling with letter tiles). Bellringers, contrast sets, three-tier concept questions with word banks, and the VT writing slides that feed the growth report. Use the first for the storage idea, add the second to build how the code is read.

## 16. Mutations & Genetic Change

**Title:** Mutations & Genetic Change | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
This is the unit's widest topic — how the genetic code changes and what those changes do — and it is built as four lesson decks so you can go as deep as a class can handle. Resistant learners can hold to the core idea of what a mutation is; faster or more advanced classes can add gene expression, genetic disorders, and stem-cell differentiation.

Students work each idea through the Variation Theory arc — first answer, contrasts and a pattern-break, revision. The four decks build: what a mutation is and where variation comes from; how gene expression and biotechnology put that variation to use; how mutations lead to genetic disorders (and why a sunburn's DNA damage is not inherited — somatic vs. germline); and how stem cells differentiate into specialized tissues. The growth report shows who can distinguish a change that is inherited from one that isn't, and who is still treating every DNA change the same.

*What's included (choose by pace, a suggested order):* **Genetic Mutations** (30 slides), **Mutations & Gene Expression / Biotechnology** (43 slides), **Mutations & Genetic Disorders** (31 slides; somatic vs. germline, karyotypes), **Stem Cell Differentiation** (26 slides). Bellringers, contrast sets, three-tier concept questions with word banks, and the VT writing slides that feed the growth report. Use one or several depending on your class's pace.

## 17. Darwin & the Evidence for Evolution

**Title (63):** Evolution: Darwin & the Evidence | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable

**Description:**
Evolution is where students most need to separate what changes from what stays the same: individuals don't evolve, populations do. This unit builds the mechanism from the ground up and then turns to the evidence that life has changed over time.

Across the unit, students reason through four critical aspects — **populations change over generations**, **traits fit their environments**, **variation gives selection something to act on**, and **independent lines of evidence show life has changed over time** — from a first answer, through contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer, testing their thinking against a natural-selection simulation. Each student's Growth Report and your class summary show who can explain population-level change from variation and selection, and who still pictures individuals adapting.

*What's included:* a large multi-block deck (57 slides); the PhET natural-selection simulation; contrast sets and pattern-breaks on variation, environment, and evidence; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 18. Natural Selection & Adaptation

**Title (68):** Natural Selection & Adaptation | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
The hardest part of natural selection is not the definition — it is that a whole population changes even though no single individual ever changes. This unit builds toward exactly that, and it holds students to the mechanism with data they generate themselves (a moth hunt, a bean-population test, a beak-and-seed round, an allele-frequency count).

Over three blocks, students reason through two critical aspects — **variation is the raw material for selection** and **the environment decides which traits survive** — from a first answer, through deliberate contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised explanation. Each student's Growth Report and your class summary show who can explain population-level change and who still thinks individuals adapt.

*What's included:* a 30-slide 3-block deck; an interactive allele model; four hands-on data activities; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; an optional gene-pool challenge; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 19. Speciation & Biodiversity

**Title (63):** Speciation & Biodiversity | NGSS HS Biology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
Two ideas that usually stay separate — how one species becomes two, and how we map the diversity of life — are built here as one story: isolation over long time produces new species, and that repeated branching is why we group living things by shared ancestry rather than by looks.

Across three blocks, students reason through two critical aspects — **how new species form** (isolation leads to divergence) and **what makes a grouping useful** (shared ancestors, not appearance) — using cladograms and the tree of life, from a first answer through contrasts and a pattern-break to a revised answer. The growth loop reports who can read relatedness from ancestry and who is still grouping by resemblance.

*What's included:* a 29-slide 3-block deck; an interactive speciation model; tree-of-life and lizard-radiation activities; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; a convergent-evolution challenge; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.

## 20. Human Impact Capstone

**Title (67):** Human Impact Capstone | NGSS HS Biology Ecology | Answerable Biology

**Description:**
This is the year's ideas aimed at one urgent problem: human activity reshaping ecosystems and biodiversity. It is a capstone, so it does more than teach content — it asks students to analyze a real human impact, propose an evidence-based fix, and defend it, weighing costs against benefits.

Over three blocks, students reason through the capstone's critical aspects — **renewable vs. nonrenewable**, **non-native vs. invasive**, and the **full circle back to the food web** — from a first answer, through deliberate contrasts and a pattern-break, to a revised answer, and then write a one-page structured argument. Their Growth Report and your class summary show who can trace a human choice through an energy or nutrient cycle and back.

*What's included:* a 30-slide 3-block capstone deck; an interactive carbon/atmosphere model; footprint and invasive-species investigations; a structured one-page proposal task; three-tier concept questions with word banks; the response slides that feed the growth report; an optional cost-vs-benefit challenge; teacher-reference objectives and pacing.
