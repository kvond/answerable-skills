# Assessment: Completion, Copying, and Proficiency

Purpose: how grading works in Answerable Biology — what completion means, what proficiency means, why they are kept apart, what to do about copying, and how to set up your gradebook either way your school requires.

This is the assessment reference. Day-to-day operation lives in the Master Agenda and each Unit Agenda; the report students receive is described in "AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth"; the whole system is introduced in Start Here. Examples below come from Cycle 02 (Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships), the free demonstration unit — see the Ecology Unit Guide and the Year Arc for context.

---

## 1. Two different things a grade can mean

Most gradebooks blur two separate questions into one number. This system keeps them apart, on purpose.

**Completion is participation in the thinking process.** A student earned completion when they did the intellectual work the lesson asked for: wrote a first answer in their own words, built a rule, met the pattern-break, ran the revision prompt, wrote a revised answer. Completion says: this student showed up to the thinking. It says nothing about whether the thinking is right yet.

**Proficiency is what a student can eventually understand, explain, distinguish, apply, or use.** Can they trace energy through a food web? Can they tell mutualism from parasitism in a case they have never seen? Proficiency is read from evidence — mainly the revised answer at the end of each cycle — and it lives in the Conceptual Growth Report, not in the gradebook.

The standing rule, exactly as it appears on the closing checklist of every deck, addressed to students:

> "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade."

And the teacher note in every response slide's speaker notes:

> "TEACHER: the finished deck (first + revised answers) is the single artifact that runs through the AI pipeline → each student's printable Growth Report + class summary. Completion = both boxes filled."

Grades still exist, and they still matter. In Katherine's words: "Grades heavy enough to matter — I am not going to pretend ninth-graders will engage purely for intrinsic reasons, and they need the grades to navigate the system. What I was not willing to simplify was the thinking itself."

## 2. Why developing responses are completion-based

Every cycle asks students to commit to a first answer before they have been taught the idea. That is the point of the design: the first answer is where the misconception surfaces so the contrasts and pattern-break have something to work on. A first answer that is confidently wrong is doing its job.

Grade that answer for correctness and you break the machine. Katherine's reasoning:

- Testing too early doesn't measure anything. "Testing students before they have conceptual footing does not measure understanding. It confirms its absence, which they already know."
- Ninth graders need room to expose incomplete thinking. At the skull counter she saw what safety produces: "students surface partial knowledge they would not risk in front of the full class. A student offers an idea they are not sure about, half-finished, sometimes wrong in a way more interesting than being right." That only happens when being half-finished carries no grade penalty.
- Silence at a hard question is usually not disengagement. "Students are not yet confident enough to commit to a position in public, especially when there is no right answer to hide behind. But I have stopped reading that silence as disengagement. It is, more often, the silence of genuine thinking — of students working out what they believe before risking it. That working-out is exactly what I want."
- The risk has preconditions. "Before students have the confidence to dialogue in public, they need confidence in their own thinking." And the calibration: "I watch for the threshold where the task tips from challenging into defeating. Too hard and they stop; too easy and they are bored. The window is narrow and it moves."

So the developing work — first answers, rule-building sentences, annotations, "Keep going" guesses — is credited for being done, not scored for being right. Katherine's credit standard, stated to students constantly: "I care about your thinking, not the answer. 'Because' is not an answer. 'I don't know' is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy."

That last line is the whole completion bar. Completion is not a blank-tolerance policy. "Because" doesn't complete a slide. A sentence that shows genuine contact with the question does — even when it's wrong.

**Cycle 02 example — the completion artifact.** Day 1 ends at the three-tier concept question (slide 14): "What has to be true about how organisms use energy for there to always be more producers than consumers?" Students pick their tier — Getting Started ("A meadow stores 10,000 calories in its grass. How many reach the hawks? Show your math."), Working On It ("Why can there never be more foxes than rabbits in a stable ecosystem — even if foxes are faster, better hunters?"), or Mastery ("The 10% rule limits most ecosystems to 4–5 trophic levels. What would have to change for a 7-level ecosystem to be stable?") — with the word bank offered as "use any, modify any, or use none." They write their first answer on the response slide. Whatever tier they chose, whatever they wrote, if it shows thinking, the completion box is checked. A student who chose Getting Started and did the math honestly has completed exactly as fully as the student who attempted Mastery.

## 3. Copying

### Why conventional assignments make copying rational

When an assignment's definition of success is "obtain the correct words," copying is not a character flaw — it is the efficient strategy. A fill-in-the-blank worksheet, a definitions list, a question whose answer sits on slide 9: for all of these, the copied answer and the understood answer are indistinguishable on paper, and copying is faster. Students respond to the incentive the assignment actually creates, not the one it intends.

Katherine also names when copying happens: delay. Written directly to students on the why-notes-are-due slide: "When you wait until later: you remember less, you're more likely to copy, you miss the learning that the notes are designed to create." The notes-due-before-the-bell rule is an anti-copying structure before it is anything else.

### How this system reduces the payoff

Answerable Biology's answer is design, not surveillance. The work is built so copying stops paying:

- The questions have no source to copy from. "The thought questions do not have answers you can copy; they require students to apply a distinction they have just made to a new case, or to notice where a pattern breaks down." The what-if questions "have no answer to copy" at all — "If energy is lost at every step and never comes back, why hasn't life run out of energy? There's no answer to look up."
- Students must formulate in their own words, then explain, apply, and distinguish — the pattern-break asks them to test their own rule against decomposers or the bee-mimicking orchid, which a borrowed sentence cannot do.
- They respond to feedback and revise, and the progression is visible: first answer and revised answer sit side by side on the same slide, and the Growth Report quotes the student's own words back. A copied first answer produces a hollow report about nobody.
- The grade a student could protect by copying is a completion grade. There is nothing to gain: the honest wrong answer and the copied right answer earn the same completion credit, and only the honest one buys useful feedback.

What she praises tells students what the currency is — the Toma moment: "his own sentences, his own working-out, nothing copied — I said: Hey, all this you wrote in your own words? It's very good. Please continue."

### The integrity gate

The pipeline still checks, because authentic feedback requires authentic work. The gate (from Katherine's v2 formative pipeline spec):

- It runs on the **first answer only**. The revision is exempt by design — students are told to run the coaching prompt through their AI, so AI involvement in the revision is the assignment, not a violation.
- **Copied first answer → flag** for teacher review. The flag holds that cycle's completion entry until you resolve it; nothing posts automatically.
- **AI-written first answer → auto-flagged for failure, confirmed by you.** The entry is held pending your review and fails only after you confirm the flag.
- **Flags stay visible to you, and you decide.** Automated AI-detection misfires — on ESL students and atypical writers especially — so a flagged case is a case to look at, not a verdict. If it is a false positive, restore that student's feedback. Because the gradebook is completion-only, a wrong flag costs a cycle's feedback, not a grade.

The rationale in the spec is worth keeping in view: failing an AI-written first answer "protects the feedback loop, not a score." An AI-authored first answer can only yield a report about the AI's thinking; the failure — auto-flagged, confirmed by you — is what keeps the Growth Report worth printing.

### What not to police

Do not run integrity enforcement on low-level matching and vocabulary work — the Linnaean cup sort, the Lock & Key enzyme match, card sorts, labeled diagrams, KEY TERMS work. Its job is exposure: establishing a shared floor of vocabulary, classifications, and structures so the thinking work has words to use. Students helping each other, checking a neighbor's sort, or looking terms up is that floor being built, not cheating. It is completion work either way, and it was never evidence of proficiency, so there is nothing to protect. Save your integrity attention for the one place it matters: the first answer on the response slides. `GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES` — Katherine does not state a matching-work integrity policy in her own words; this paragraph is derived from the system's design rule that matching work establishes a shared floor and is completion work, not proof of deep proficiency. `REVIEW WITH KATHERINE`

## 4. Proficiency: where it lives and how to read it

Proficiency is judged per **critical aspect**, not per unit or per test. Each cycle names two or three critical aspects — the discernments the topic depends on — and proficiency means the student can make that discernment and use it. Vocabulary is not the bar: "A student who discerns those has understood natural selection, even if their vocabulary is still rough. A student who has memorized the vocabulary but not discerned them has understood nothing."

The primary evidence is the **revised answer** — written after the contrasts, the pattern-break, the activity, and the feedback, in the student's own words, sitting directly under the first answer so the movement is visible. The Growth Report reads that pair and reports each critical aspect as a position on a path, with plain labels (Developing / Almost there / Proficient — final label wording is Katherine's call, `REVIEW WITH KATHERINE`). The class summary gives you the same read at room scale: "who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed."

Two rules from the pipeline that protect the report:

- Proficiency is always "a position on a path, never a deficit," and every affirming line is anchored to something the student actually wrote.
- No scoring vocabulary reaches students — no scale names, no codes, no jargon. The scales below are for you.

### Proficiency scales by critical aspect

A usable scale describes the progression of the discernment itself. The three tiers already built into every concept question — Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery — are the same progression from the student's side; students even self-locate on it ("Rate yourself ... Before this lesson: Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery. Right now: ..."). Here are the two Cycle 02 scales as worked examples; write yours for other cycles by the same pattern, one scale per critical aspect, three levels, each level a description of what the student can do.

**Critical aspect: Energy flow through trophic levels**

| Level | What the student can do |
|---|---|
| Developing | Describes who eats whom; may treat energy as passed along whole ("the hawk gets the grass's energy"). |
| Almost there | States the 10% rule and does the math (10,000 calories of grass → ~100 reach the hawks), but "loss" is a fact to recite — can't say where the 90% goes, or why the pyramid must narrow. |
| Proficient | Explains where the energy goes (heat through respiration, waste, uneaten and dead material), uses it — why a meadow feeds thousands of rabbits but only 10–15 hawks, why chains stop at 4–5 levels — and can place a decomposer without the rule breaking. |

**Critical aspect: Symbiosis and species relationships**

| Level | What the student can do |
|---|---|
| Developing | Labels relationships by memorized example ("bees and flowers = mutualism") without a rule that transfers. |
| Almost there | Sorts clear cases by outcome — who gains, who loses, who is unaffected — bee/flower, mosquito/human, pilot fish/shark. |
| Proficient | Applies the outcome rule carefully to a case designed to break it: the orchid that mimics a female bee is pollinated and pays no nectar — the student tracks actual costs and benefits instead of reaching for a memorized label, and says what the relationship would become if the payoffs changed. |

### Reading the Cycle 02 pair

First answer (slide 15, Day 1): a student writes "you get all the energy because you ate the whole cow." Completion: earned — it's a committed, honest answer. Proficiency: Developing, and exactly the misconception the lesson exists to break.

Revised answer (same slide, Day 3, after the meadow contrast, the decomposer pattern-break, the food-web build, and the AI's two questions): "you only keep about 10% because the cow used most of its energy being alive — it went off as heat, and some was never eaten. That's why there's way more grass than hawks." That sentence is proficiency evidence: the 10% figure, the mechanism, and the pyramid consequence, in the student's own words. The Growth Report quotes it back under "Evidence from my final answer." Nothing about this pair goes in the gradebook except: both boxes filled, complete.

## 5. Gradebook implementation

### Option A — completion-only (the system as designed)

This is Katherine's actual model. The gradebook records completion; all proficiency lives in the Growth Report.

- One completion entry per cycle. Complete = both answer areas filled on each response slide (Cycle 02 has two response slides; the closing checklist tells students exactly this). Katherine's pipeline posts it as a CSV to Schoology, one row per student, one column per lesson (header `DRAFT.<Lesson>` or `Notes.<Lesson>`) — but a hand-kept column works identically.
- Notes/annotation completion can be a second recurring entry if you collect it (notes due before the bell).
- Matching, card, and vocabulary work: completion entries too, if you enter them at all.
- Nothing about answer quality ever posts. Proficiency reaches students only through the printed Growth Report, and reaches you through the class summary.
- Integrity outcomes interact with completion only: a copying or AI flag holds that completion entry pending your review — nothing posts automatically. An AI-written first answer fails the entry only after you confirm it: auto-flagged for failure, confirmed by you.
- Keep the grades "heavy enough to matter" — weight the completion category so that not doing the thinking actually shows in the grade. How heavy is a school-context decision. `GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES` — Katherine's sources give the completion rule and the "heavy enough to matter" principle, not category weights or column structure beyond the CSV header.

### Option B — completion + proficiency (for schools that require graded proficiency)

Some schools require that proficiency appear in the gradebook. This variant keeps the system's protections while satisfying that requirement. It is a build-provided adaptation, not Katherine's practice — `REVIEW WITH KATHERINE`.

Two gradebook categories:

1. **Completion** — identical to Option A. Every cycle, both-boxes-filled, credit for participation in thinking. This category stays untouched: it is what makes the first answer safe.
2. **Proficiency** — a small number of entries per marking period (2–4, not one per cycle), each scored from **revised answers only**, against the critical-aspect scales above (3 = Proficient, 2 = Almost there, 1 = Developing). Enter the score after the Growth Report run, when you have the report's read plus your own; the human judgment is yours, not the pipeline's.

Three lines you must hold for the variant not to destroy the design:

- **Never score the first answer for correctness.** The moment first answers carry a proficiency grade, students stop exposing incomplete thinking and the revision loop has nothing real to work on. Score only where the arc ends.
- **Score few, not all.** Grading every revised answer converts revision into a test and re-creates the incentive to copy the revision. A handful of checkpoint cycles per marking period gives the gradebook what the school needs while most cycles stay pure feedback.
- **Keep the scale plain for students.** If a proficiency score posts where students see it, it carries the same plain labels as the report — never rubric jargon or codes.

If your school mandates percentage grades rather than a 1–3 scale, map the levels to bands (e.g. Proficient 90–100, Almost there 80–89, Developing 70–79) rather than inventing finer distinctions the evidence doesn't support. `GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES` — banding is a build suggestion.

### Either way

The reporting order never changes: student thinking → revised answer → Growth Report → (only then, if required) a gradebook proficiency entry. The gradebook is downstream of the report, never a substitute for it. What students hear stays the same sentence in both options: your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade.
