# Classroom Technology for Formative Noticing

Purpose: how to use the smartboard and school screen systems (GoGuardian and similar) to extend your noticing of student thinking during a cycle — and what to do instead when the devices aren't there.

This document covers the technology in the room while you teach. Platform setup (Schoology, Google Classroom, deck distribution, submission) lives in the LMS Platform Implementation Guide. The AI feedback pipeline lives in "AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth". If you are new to the system, read Start Here first.

## The test for any classroom tool

Katherine's test, from the book: the difference that matters is "the difference between a tool that returns me to them and a tool that takes me further away." Everything in this document follows from that one sentence.

What the tool is extending is specific. Her own account of what the redesign changed:

> "The work has moved from managing activities to noticing agency. My practices have become simpler — two things per class, real questions, thinking rewarded rather than correctness. What has become more complex is what I notice. I am watching differently now — listening for the beginning of interpretation, for the moment a student starts to revise what they thought, for the places where the room opens and the places where it closes down."

And what only a person can do with what they notice: "The vehicle can hold the room. It cannot answer his specific question. ... Only a person can do that, in real time, in the seconds before the moment closes."

So the standard for a smartboard, a monitoring dashboard, or any screen system is not classroom control. It is: does this let me read more of the room's thinking, sooner, so I can respond while the moment is still open? A tool used to catch off-task tabs polices behavior. The same tool used to read thirty first answers as they are written extends noticing. Same tool; different use; the difference is everything.

One more of her lines to keep in view, because monitoring software invites exactly the opposite: "Students feel when teaching becomes accelerated management rather than shared attention. They experience the difference between a teacher who is cognitively available and one who is merely trying to survive the period."

## Where the noticing moments are in every cycle

The deck rhythm builds writing moments into every block, and every writing moment is a scan moment. Using Cycle 02 (Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships) as the example:

- Day 1, bellringer. "Think about the last meal you ate. Where did the energy in that food originally come from? Write your best guess." Thirty guesses appear while you take attendance.
- Day 1, first answer (response slide). Students commit in writing: at each step of grass → cow → you, "do you get all of the grass's energy — or something else? Why?" This is the richest scan of the cycle — first answers are working models, written before anyone has been told anything.
- Day 1, rule-building. "Finish this sentence as a rule: 'Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ______________________.'" One sentence per student, all answering the same stem — the fastest possible read of where the room is.
- Day 1, after the pattern-break. The decomposer slide breaks the "energy passes upward" rule. What students write next tells you who is revising and who is defending.
- Day 1–2, three-tier concept questions. Which tier a student chose, and what they did with it, is itself information.
- Day 2, second first answer (bee-and-flower vs. mosquito-and-human) and second rule-building sentence.
- Day 3, revision. Students write the revised answer directly under the first. Watching revisions appear is watching the cycle's whole point happen — and it is where you read whether the class is ready for the next cycle.

Every thinking slide carries the same instruction line: "Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write." That sequence hands you three different channels in order: silence you should not interrupt, table talk you can listen to, and writing you can read. The rest of this document is about reading the third channel at scale — and the last section is about the first two.

## The smartboard

Her own projected-content habits, in her words: "My slides carry a fair amount of text — to support students who were absent or are reviewing — but I scan each one and then talk freely. Reading the slides as written would be deadening. I highlight important words with my marker. I draw where it helps."

That is the whole sourced smartboard philosophy, and it is enough: the projection is a shared object the room looks at together, not a script. The deck is built for it — day dividers, bellringers, CASE A / CASE B contrasts side by side, sim links opened for the whole class. Mark it up. Draw the arrows. GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES for anything smartboard-specific beyond this; the projection moves below are build-drafted from her contrast and safety reasoning.

Projecting student thinking (build-drafted):

- Project selected answers, never selected students. Copy two or three first answers or rule-sentences into a blank slide, stripped of names, before showing anything. First answers are developing thinking written under a promise of safety — "Completion = both answers present" is the grading rule precisely so students will risk being wrong. Projecting a named wrong answer breaks that contract once and the writing gets cautious for the rest of the year.
- Pick answers for what they notice, not for what they get wrong. Her stance: partial understandings are "partial understandings of something, whose divergent answers are evidence of what they are noticing." Project the answer that says "the cow uses some energy to walk around" next to the one that says "energy passes up the chain" — the room can see that the first one has hold of something the second hasn't found yet.
- Compare responses as a class using the rule-building stems. The stem does the anonymizing for you: three completions of "Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ___" go up side by side — "it gets smaller," "90% is lost as heat," "the animals higher up need more food." Ask the class which rule survives the decomposer slide. This is the deck's own CASE A / CASE B move, run on the students' own sentences — the contrast does the teaching, and nobody was singled out.
- If a specific student's answer is worth the room's attention, ask them first, privately. Some will be glad. The agency stays theirs.

## Screen systems: GoGuardian and similar

REVIEW WITH KATHERINE — this whole section. GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES: the source corpus contains no GoGuardian or screen-monitoring reasoning from Katherine. What follows is practical build-drafted guidance, written to be consistent with her sourced principles (noticing over policing, feedback without deficit language, safety for incomplete thinking), but it is not her voice and none of the workflow below should be attributed to her.

Most schools that issue Chromebooks issue a monitoring system with them — GoGuardian Teacher, Hapara, Lightspeed, Securly, LanSchool. The default framing of these tools is surveillance: closed tabs, blocked sites, off-task alerts. Used only that way, the tool takes you further away from students — you become a screen-watcher instead of a room-watcher. But the same dashboard, pointed at the deck's writing moments, is a way to read every student's developing answer in real time. That is the use this section describes.

The moves, in the order a cycle produces them:

1. Scan responses while students write. When the room hits a first-answer slide, open the dashboard's screen-view grid instead of circulating cold. In two or three minutes you can read every answer forming — who has a working model, who has a blank box, who wrote one hedged word. Her standard for this read is already defined: "I am watching not for accuracy but for whether students have a working model at all — whether they can produce a sentence that shows genuine contact with the concept." The dashboard just lets you apply it to thirty screens at once instead of the six desks you can reach.

2. Identify misunderstandings quickly. You know each cycle's predictable wrong turns because they are what the critical aspects were chosen against. In Cycle 02, watch the forming answers for: "you get all the grass's energy" (no loss), "the energy is destroyed" (loss with no destination), "mosquitoes are parasites because they're bad" (outcome replaced by moral judgment), decomposers slotted as a fourth trophic level. Spotting three screens making the same wrong turn during writing — rather than tonight while reading decks — is the entire payoff of the tool.

3. Send a private message. Most systems can push a message to one student's screen. Use the feedback register the whole system runs on — never deficit language, and never the answer. Quote something right, ask a question: "'The cow uses energy to move' — yes. Where does that energy go after the cow uses it?" or "Your rule says energy passes upward. Check the decomposer slide before you settle." A private nudge reaches the student who would never raise a hand, without costing them anything in front of the table.

4. Pull one student for quick tutoring. When a screen shows a student genuinely stuck — not thinking, stuck — call them to your desk or crouch at theirs for two minutes. This is the digital version of a habit she describes directly: "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it." The dashboard just moves the hunt from after grading to during writing, while the moment is still open. Keep it short and keep it dialogic: one thing their answer has right, one question, send them back.

5. Send that student back to teach their table. When the two-minute conversation lands — the face changes, the sentence comes out right — don't end it there. Send them back with a job: "Explain that to your table. See if they agree." Teaching the explanation is the strongest evidence the idea took, and it matches what she listens for at the collaborative stage: "you can hear who is beginning to geek out by listening for the student who is teaching." It also converts a pulled-aside student from remediated back to knower in front of their peers.

6. Project selected thinking and compare responses as a class. The dashboard makes harvesting answers for the smartboard moves above fast — copy two or three forming answers (names stripped) into a blank slide while students are still writing, and run the comparison the moment writing ends. All the rules from the smartboard section apply: answers, never names; chosen for what they notice; the rule-stems as the comparison frame.

7. Display and accountability without singling anyone out. If the room needs to feel that the writing is seen, report patterns, not people: "About half the rules in this room say energy 'disappears' — the other half say where it goes. That difference is the next slide." Never project the dashboard grid itself to the class, and never name the off-task screen from the front — walk over instead. Proximity was the move before the software existed and it is still the better one: "I move among students during activities and labs — talking over aspects together, noticing their ideas, crouching beside someone who is stuck."

8. Decide whether the class is ready to move forward. This is the decision the scan actually serves, and her criterion is already stated: "I calibrate by feel: after a few slides of lurking, do I start to see enough confidence to risk an answer out loud? When answers become more thought-out and less random, we move. When they don't, I don't push." The dashboard turns "feel" into thirty read sentences: if most rule-sentences survive contact with the pattern-break, advance; if the same wrong turn is on a third of the screens, that is your signal to stay — reteach the contrast, not the vocabulary. On Day 3, the revised answers are the same read at cycle scale: revision happening across the room means the class is ready for the next cycle; first answers copied unchanged into the revision box means it is not. The class summary from the Conceptual Growth Report pipeline gives you the same readiness read after the cycle; the dashboard gives you a rough version of it during.

What the screen cannot tell you. A monitoring dashboard reads activity, and activity is exactly what her framework says not to trust: "The student with their head down has often been mislabeled disengaged when they were lurking." A still screen during "Think first" may be the silence of genuine thinking — "the silence of genuine thinking — of students working out what they believe before risking it. That working-out is exactly what I want." An off-task tab during a Fiddle window may be a student who finished. Read screens the way she reads the room: for thinking, not for compliance. When the dashboard and your read of the student disagree, trust the student — the deepest harm the book names is misreading a student, and a dashboard misreads faster and more confidently than a person does.

One boundary: nothing above sends student first answers or revisions anywhere. The scan is you reading your own students' work in your own room. The AI step stays where the system puts it — human-gated, on Day 3, through the revision prompt in the response slide's Notes — and the monitoring system plays no part in it.

## No dashboard, no smartboard: the non-device versions

Every move above has a paper-and-feet version, and the paper versions predate the software. If your room has no monitoring system — or the network is down, which it will be — the cycle runs unchanged.

- Circulate with a roster. During first-answer writing, walk the room with the class list on a clipboard and mark three things per student: has an answer forming / blank / talk to me. One lap covers the room in the same two or three minutes the dashboard would. This is her own default made systematic — the roster just keeps the quiet middle of the room from slipping past you, which is the exact population she built the AI feedback for ("the shy student making real progress who never volunteers").
- Whiteboard voting for the compare-as-a-class move. Small dry-erase boards (or a torn half-sheet): everyone writes their rule-sentence completion, everyone holds it up on the count of three. Simultaneous reveal means nobody answers alone, you read thirty rules in ten seconds, and you can pull two contrasting boards to the front — same comparison, no projector. Works identically for CASE A / CASE B commitments: "A or B — hold it up."
- Table-talk eavesdropping. The instruction line already schedules it: "Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write." The discuss phase is your audio channel — stand near a table and listen without joining. You are listening for the same things the screens would show you: which wrong turn is circulating, and who is teaching. Two tables of eavesdropping usually predicts the whole room's writing.
- Paper exit slips. The rule-building stem is a ready-made slip: "Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ___" on a quarter-sheet, handed in at the door. Sort the stack into piles by the wrong turn while the next class does its bellringer — that pile count is your move-forward decision for tomorrow, made the same way the dashboard makes it during class. On Day 3, "one sentence: what changed between your first answer and your revised answer?" is the exit-slip version of the revision read.

The non-device versions are not the degraded fallback. They keep you in the room, at the tables, crouched beside the stuck student — which is where the tool was supposed to return you anyway.
