# First Week — Classroom Conditions

Purpose: the classroom conditions that make Answerable Biology work — names, seats, visible responsibilities, and classroom jobs — and how to establish them during the first week, while Cycle 01 is already running.

## What the first week is actually for

Answerable Biology asks students to do something most classrooms never ask: write down what they actually think before they know the answer, defend a rule they built themselves, and let everyone see the thinking change. None of that happens in a room where students feel anonymous, watched, or interchangeable. In Katherine's words, the goal is a room where "students feel less like objects of my teaching and more like workmates in a shared process. That is not a technique. It is a stance."

The first week builds the conditions for that stance. Not a week of rules and syllabus review, and not a week of icebreakers — a week in which the biology starts immediately (Cycle 01 — Classifying Organisms; see the Year Arc) while five structures get established underneath it:

1. You learn names fast, at the door.
2. Students sit where they already have social safety.
3. Task index cards make group responsibilities visible.
4. New students are placed on purpose, not wherever there's a chair.
5. Classroom jobs give students real ownership of the room — and replace icebreakers as the way students meet each other.

Everything below serves one payoff, due at the sixth class meeting — early in week two on a daily schedule (on A/B blocks it lands later on the calendar; see "How the week fits together"): when Cycle 02 — Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships puts up its first response slide ("At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy — or something else? Why?"), every student risks a genuine first answer. Students only expose incomplete thinking to a teacher who knows who they are, at a table where they are safe, in a room they partly run.

This document covers the conditions. The first-week lesson content is Cycle 01 in the Year Arc and the Ecology Unit Guide; day-to-day operation lives in the Master Agenda.

## Meeting students at the door

REVIEW WITH KATHERINE — this section expands her design direction (printed rosters, fast name-learning, repeated low-pressure recognition) into practical guidance; the book chapters and decks are silent on door routines and names (GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES), so the specifics here are build-drafted.

Stand at the door every day of the first week with a printed roster on a clipboard — one per block, large font, space to write beside each name. Greet each student, ask their name (and what they want to be called, which is often not what the roster says), and mark the roster: preferred name, a pronunciation note, anything that helps you attach the face to the name. Keep using the printed roster until you no longer need it. That is the whole system. No name tents, no name games.

Why it matters enough to do daily: the pedagogy of this curriculum runs on being known. Katherine crouches beside students, hunts a student down when a graded answer shows a problem, invites individuals to the counter by name. The moment she calls the "got me" look — "the look of someone who has just found out that what they think actually matters to the person they are thinking with" — cannot happen between a teacher and a student whose name the teacher is still reaching for. A student who is anonymous in week three has already concluded their thinking is anonymous too, and will write first answers accordingly.

Make the recognition repeated and low-pressure — for you and for them:

- Take attendance from the roster by looking at faces, not by calling names down a list. Silently test yourself; check the roster only when you miss. (The Fiddle covers this time — students are already at a sim, so attendance can be slow without the room idling. See Start Here.)
- Use names during circulation from day one, even when you have to glance at the clipboard first. Reading the name off the roster and then using it is not a failure of the system; it is the system.
- At the door on days three through five, greet by name before the student says anything, and let them correct you without ceremony when you're wrong. Being wrong cheaply and often is how the names stick.
- Never put a student on the spot to test your own recall, and never make name-learning a class activity. The pressure belongs on you, not them. This is the same logic the curriculum applies to student thinking: low-stakes, repeated contact, no public performance before there's safety.

A realistic target: every name cold by the end of week two. Until then, the clipboard goes where you go.

## Seating — friend groups may sit together

REVIEW WITH KATHERINE — her design direction (friends sit together; existing relationships provide immediate social safety; the structure is purposeful) is expanded here into build-drafted guidance; the sources contain no seating philosophy (GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES).

On day one, let students sit with their friends, in table clusters of three to five. Say so plainly: "Sit where you want. Sit with people you know."

This is a deliberate design decision, not a soft opening you'll walk back once you've made a "real" seating chart. The reasoning:

- The curriculum's first demand is intellectual risk. Every thinking slide says "Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write." A student seated next to a stranger on day two has a partner in name only; a student next to a friend has someone they will actually say a half-formed idea to. Existing peer relationships are immediate social safety — the kind that otherwise takes six weeks of forced groupings to rebuild from zero.
- The standard worry — friends together means off-task — assumes the room has nothing for them to do. This room does. From the first day there is a Fiddle open on arrival, a first answer due, a cup sort that a table completes together and then calls the teacher over to check (Cycle 01). Friend groups go off-task when they are under-tasked; that is a workload problem, not a seating problem. (The one incident in Katherine's sources points the same way: the student "joking with friends" was a strong student with nothing in the room that had yet met him — her response was harder work, not a new seat.)
- Purposeful structure is what turns a friend group into a working group, and the next two sections — task index cards and classroom jobs — are that structure. Friend seating without them is just proximity. With them, the cluster has visible responsibilities, and the friendship becomes the grease of the work instead of its competitor.

What to hold onto: you still own the map. Clusters of three to five, arranged so you can circulate to every table; students choose within that structure. Loners and students who know no one get placed by you — warmly, by name, into a cluster you judge can absorb them (same move as for new students, below). And a cluster that demonstrates over weeks that it cannot work is rearranged as a working decision, stated as one: "This group isn't getting the build done. I'm moving two of you." Rearranging a cluster is rare when the tasks are real; keep it rare.

## Task index cards

REVIEW WITH KATHERINE — build-drafted expansion of her design direction; no task-card system appears in the sources (GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES).

At each table cluster, keep a small set of index cards — one card per responsibility a group task needs. Each card names a job in one line, with two or three bullet-sized specifics. When a group task starts, the cards get dealt out at the table; whoever holds a card owns that job for the task. Cards are laminated or in a card stand, live at the table permanently, and rotate by dealing — no negotiation phase, no teacher assignment table-by-table.

A working set for the Ecology arc's group activities:

- MATERIALS — get the card set / cups / sheets from the supply station; return them sorted and complete (see the Master Supply System for what "complete" means per activity).
- RECORDER — the group's written answers go on your sheet; check that everyone's noticing gets in, not just the fastest talker's.
- CHECKER — before the group calls the teacher over, you confirm the group actually agrees. In the Cycle 01 cup sort, the deck's own instruction — "When you think you have the correct order, call your teacher over" — is this card's moment: the Checker makes the call.
- SPOKESPERSON — when the teacher arrives or the class debriefs, you speak first for the group.

Deal only as many cards as the task needs; a two-person job gets two cards. For the two-day food-web build in Cycle 02, the same four cards cover it: Materials owns the Pacific NW species cards, the Recorder owns the web sheet, the Checker owns "are the arrows pointing the right way before we do the pyramid math," the Spokesperson answers when you stop at the table.

What the cards are for: they make responsibility visible, which is what converts a friend group into a functioning classroom group. Without them, every group task quietly assigns itself — the conscientious student does everything, the others watch, and friend seating takes the blame. With them, the group has a structure that doesn't depend on anyone's personality, the workload distributes by card rather than by habit, and you can circulate and address the role instead of fishing for a volunteer: "Who's Checker here? Are you ready for me?" The card also gives a quieter student a legitimate, bounded way to matter to the group — holding the Materials card is participation that requires no public performance, which is the same on-ramp logic as annotation in the lessons: touching the work before being asked to speak for it.

Teach the cards once, during the first group task of Cycle 01, in under two minutes: this is what the cards are, deal them, go. The cards explain themselves in use; do not front-load a lecture about roles.

## New students

REVIEW WITH KATHERINE — build-drafted expansion of her design direction (intentional placement and introduction based on teacher knowledge of the group); the sources address absence support but not new-student arrival (GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES).

Students who arrive after the first week — and in many schools they arrive all year — do not get pointed at an empty chair. Placement is a teaching decision you make with what you now know about every cluster, because you built the clusters and you've been circulating through them daily.

The move has three parts, all small:

1. Choose the cluster on purpose. You are matching a person you barely know to groups you know well, so pick by the group: which cluster has room socially, works reliably, and contains at least one student likely to pull a newcomer in? Avoid both the struggling cluster that needs no new variable and the tight foursome with no social surface. Adjust as you learn who the new student is.
2. Introduce them to the cluster, not to the class. A whole-class introduction is high pressure and zero information. At the table it is cheap and real: "This is Marisol. She's joining your group. You're two days into the food-web build — Recorder, catch her up on what your web says so far, and deal her a card next task." The task cards do half the welcome for you: a newcomer with the Materials card has a role and a reason to talk within ten minutes of sitting down.
3. Close the content gap with the deck, not with a packet. The slides deliberately carry enough text to support "students who were absent or are reviewing" — the same feature covers students who were never here. The new student reads the cycle's slides, writes first answers where the class already has them, and enters the revision loop at the next Day 3. Their Conceptual Growth Report starts from their first cycle, and growth-from-your-own-baseline means joining in November carries no built-in penalty.

Within the first week, run the classroom-job resume with them individually (five minutes, or as homework) and slot them into the jobs system. A new student with a job stops being new faster than anything an icebreaker ever accomplished.

## Classroom jobs

REVIEW WITH KATHERINE — the refusable-task principle below is hers verbatim; the jobs system built around it is build-drafted from her design direction (jobs as ownership; jobs instead of icebreakers; purposeful cluster talk; interviews in clusters). The sources contain no jobs system (GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES beyond the passage quoted).

The one trace of classroom jobs in Katherine's sources is load-bearing. For students already working above the class, she writes: "I sometimes offer what looks like a task that helps the classroom — organizing or making materials — but is really a way of meeting the level they are working at. I tell them: you can say no. You can stop when you want. The agency stays theirs."

Take that as the charter for the whole system. Jobs are real work the room actually needs, offered as a way of respecting what a student can do — and every job is refusable and quittable. A job a student cannot decline is a chore with a nicer name, and it produces compliance, not ownership.

Why jobs are critical to ownership: this curriculum's endgame is a class that owns its knowledge — "By the middle of the year the class has its own corpus — cases, questions, and small disagreements no other classroom will have... The knowledge is theirs, partly, because they made it." A room can't belong to students intellectually while belonging to the teacher operationally. Jobs are how the room itself becomes partly theirs: the materials come out because a student got them, the sims are up because a student opened them, the decks went in because a student swept the room for missing submissions. Ownership of the thinking and ownership of the room reinforce each other; students take care of what is theirs, and they think harder in a room they take care of.

Jobs the room actually needs — draw from these, and add what your room needs (EXAMPLE NEEDED — Katherine's actual job list, if one exists):

- Materials manager — pulls and returns activity materials (card sets, cups, lab trays) against the Master Supply System lists; flags what's missing or broken.
- Tech lead — gets the day's Fiddle link up on arrival devices; first responder for "my Chromebook won't" before it becomes the teacher's problem.
- Lab steward — station reset after labs and stations days; owns the "clean up your station" line of the lab-safety rules.
- Submission checker — end of block, sweeps for classmates who haven't submitted their deck ("both answers present" is the whole check — it's completion, not judging anyone's answers).
- Print and prep — makes copies, cuts card sets, laminates; the "organizing or making materials" work from Katherine's own example, and the natural offer for a student working above the class.
- Greeter and catch-up — meets a returning absent student or a new student, points them to the slides and where the class is.
- Board and agenda — posts the day's agenda line from the Master Agenda; updates the cycle number.

Jobs instead of icebreakers. The first-week alternative to "two truths and a lie" is: on day two or three, hand out the job resume (next section); the following day, cluster students by the jobs they applied for and let them talk. This beats icebreakers on every axis that matters here. Icebreaker talk is content-free performance — exactly the kind of forced public sharing the rest of the curriculum is careful never to demand. Job-cluster talk is purposeful: students who chose the same kind of work are already, by selection, students with something in common, and the conversation has an object ("what does the tech lead actually do when the cart wifi dies?") instead of a script. Students meet each other the way adults meet — over shared work — and the room gets staffed in the process.

Job interviews in clusters. Run them as a 15–20 minute block late in week one:

1. Cluster students by job interest from the resumes (everyone who listed Tech lead in one corner, Materials in another; students who listed several pick the cluster they care most about).
2. Give each cluster the job's card — the one-line description and its two or three specifics — and one question: "What would make someone good at this, and which of you wants it when?"
3. Circulate cluster to cluster and interview briefly: who's done something like this, who wants it first, who'd rather be backup. This is also the best ten minutes of student-reading you will get all week — you learn who volunteers, who defers, who organizes the group without being asked.
4. Post the roster next day: job, holder, backup. Most jobs rotate; a student who loves a job and does it well can keep it — the point is ownership, not turn-taking fairness.

House rules, said to the class once: every job is real (nothing invented to keep hands busy); every job is refusable — you can say no, and you can stop when you want; no job is a reward or a punishment; and jobs are never graded. The agency stays theirs.

## The classroom job resume

REVIEW WITH KATHERINE — build-drafted from her design direction (a simple application gathering interests, skills, experiences, preferences, possible contributions; ninth-grade appropriate). The form below is drafted for her approval; no source form exists (GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES).

The resume is a one-page application, ten minutes, done in class in the first week. Its stated purpose is staffing the jobs. Its real yield is larger: it is the first piece of writing where the student tells you who they are, and it hands you a week-one read on every student that no icebreaker produces — who fixes phones, who has a job after school, who writes three words, who fills the page. Read every one. Use what you learn when you place jobs, and when you crouch beside them in week two already knowing something true about them.

Keep it exactly as low-stakes as the curriculum's first answers: completion credit only, never graded for writing, and say so on the form. Sentence fragments are fine. A student may apply for nothing — the form still asks what they're interested in, and that alone is worth having. This is the same rule the response slides run on: anything that shows real thinking counts.

Ready-to-copy form (fits one page; adjust the job list to your roster of jobs):

---

CLASSROOM JOB APPLICATION — Biology

Name: _______________________ What you like to be called: _______________________ Block: ______

This class runs partly on student jobs — real work the room needs. This form helps me match jobs to people. It is not graded for writing; short answers and lists are fine. You can apply for a job, or just tell me about yourself and skip the job part. Taking a job is always your choice, and you can step down from one at any time.

1. Jobs I'm interested in (circle up to 3, or none):

Materials manager · Tech lead · Lab steward · Submission checker · Print and prep · Greeter and catch-up · Board and agenda · Other idea: ______________

2. Things I'm good at (in school or out — fixing things, drawing, organizing, being on time, tech, talking to people, anything):

_________________________________________________________________________________

_________________________________________________________________________________

3. Experience (jobs, chores, teams, clubs, babysitting, helping at home or somewhere else — anything where people counted on you):

_________________________________________________________________________________

_________________________________________________________________________________

4. Preferences (circle any that are true for you):

I like working alone · I like working with others · I like being in charge of a thing · I'd rather help than lead · I like jobs at the start of class · I like jobs at the end of class · I'd rather have a behind-the-scenes job · I'm fine being the one who talks

5. Something I could contribute to this class that isn't on this form:

_________________________________________________________________________________

6. Anything you want me to know about you? (Optional. Really anything.)

_________________________________________________________________________________

---

Handling the stack: sort into job clusters for the interviews, note the students who circled nothing (they get an individual, pressure-free offer later — some students need to watch the system run for two weeks before wanting in, which is lurking, and it's fine), and keep the forms. In October, when a job opens or a student seems adrift, the resume tells you what they said they were good at when the year was new.

## How the week fits together

A suggested shape, not a script (the Master Agenda carries the actual daily lines; the schedule is flexible by design). It is counted in class meetings, not calendar days: on daily 90-minute blocks or 45-minute periods these five meetings are the first calendar week, while on an alternating A/B block schedule the first "week" spans two calendar weeks (see Schedule Options).

- Meeting 1 — roster at the door; sit with friends in clusters; Fiddle on arrival; Cycle 01 teaching begins.
- Meeting 2 — roster at the door; task index cards introduced inside the Cycle 01 cup sort; job resume handed out (ten minutes, end of block).
- Meeting 3 — names without the roster where you can; job interviews in clusters (15–20 minutes); Cycle 01 continues.
- Meeting 4 — job roster posted; jobs run for the first time; Cycle 01 Day 3 revision work.
- Meeting 5 — everything running at once: greeted by name, seated with friends, cards dealt, jobs live. Cycle 02 — Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships opens at the next meeting with the room's conditions already in place — which is the point: the first real first answer of the free demonstration unit lands in a room already built to receive it.

If your calendar gives you a short or interrupted first week (see Schedule Options), protect the order, not the days: door and seats first, cards with the first group task, jobs by the end of whatever the first full week turns out to be.
