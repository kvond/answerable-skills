## 1. The lesson's conceptual spine

### Critical aspects

Her core design principle, in her own words (Ch5, "Choosing the critical aspects"):

> "If the critical aspect is the thing the whole lesson turns on, then the most consequential decision I make is which aspects those are — and there are never many of them. A lesson, in my experience, can carry two or three critical aspects that actually shape understanding. Not eight facts. Two or three discernments, each of which, if a student fails to make it, leaves everything downstream incoherent."

How she finds them:

> "the way I find them is to ask where students reliably go wrong — because the critical aspects of a topic are usually the exact places where the discipline's compression hides a misconception." (Ch5)

Her worked example (natural selection, Ch5): the critical aspects are "not the vocabulary list" but the discernments that dissolve the giraffe-stretching misconception — "death-and-reproduction-not-individual-striving, population-not-individual, and variation-came-first." And the payoff standard: "A student who discerns those has understood natural selection, even if their vocabulary is still rough. A student who has memorized the vocabulary but not discerned them has understood nothing, and will reconstruct the giraffe-stretching story the moment the test is over."

The critical aspect is the shared object, not the slide (Ch5, "What the shared object actually is"):

> "The shared object is not the slide. It is not the sim, the lab bench, or the artifact. Those are vehicles. The shared object is the conceptual feature the vehicle is pointing at — in Variation Theory's terms, the critical aspect of the lesson, the one feature whose discernment is what the lesson is for."

> "a lesson can have one shared object running across many different vehicles" — her example: the light-reactions dependency made perceivable "through a slide that contrasts a green plant with an Indian pipe, through a sim that lets students turn the light on and off and watch the downstream chemistry stall, through an activity that has them sketch the dependency in their own arrows, through a what-if question spoken aloud in the last five minutes." (Ch5)

Each critical aspect gets its own purpose-built activity (Ch5): "each critical aspect gets tied to an activity built specifically for discerning that aspect, and built, where I can manage it, as something students play around with rather than something they are told." Test for the activity (Ch5, "Where to start"): "does it isolate the aspect, so that the thing the misconception gets wrong is the one thing the student cannot help noticing? If it does, it earns its place."

In-deck usage: every unit names its critical aspects on the teacher-reference slide and on "Critical aspect:" slides; Cycle 13 states them to students directly as "Critical aspects — What to watch for this week" (Deck: Cycle 13 — Genetics Continued _ Punnett, slide 3). The unit descriptions (UD) name each unit's two-to-four critical aspects in bold.

Her "two things per class" discipline is the operational form (Ch5):

> "I decided to do two things per class. Not four. Not six. Two. A lesson built around a critical aspect or two, where students had to actually think — not copy, not guess from context. And an activity or lab focused on the same critical aspect, not on completing a procedure for its own sake. The rest — the elaborate dialogue scaffolds, the supplementary worksheets — went."

> "This sounds like a reduction of ambition. It is the opposite. Doing two things well takes more pedagogical work, not less. The two have to be the right two. ... The cuts are pedagogical decisions, not laziness — and only the teacher can make them."

> "What I found, almost immediately, was that students found it doable. And doable turned out to be the condition for genuine thought."

### Question sequences

Her three named question types (Ch5, "Perceptual tripping points and other question types"):

1. **Perceptual tripping point** — "a place in the discipline where the conventional naming will mislead any student who is paying attention." Example: veins/arteries and the pulmonary exception. "A lesson that holds the pulmonary case beside the standard case and asks students to notice what has changed does something a definition cannot: it lets the student catch the trick of the language before the language traps them."
2. **Perceptual reframe** — "a question that holds a familiar concept against an unfamiliar case and asks whether the concept still fits. Is an Indian pipe a plant? Is a virus alive? Is fermentation a kind of respiration? These are not gotcha questions. ... The answer matters less than the articulation."
3. **Prediction-under-perturbation (the what-if)** — "These have no answer to copy. They require the student to commit to a position and defend it from inside their working model of the system."

Sequencing principle — don't ask precision questions too early (Ch5, Ito section): "A question asked too early, before the aspect has been located, asks the student to be precise about something they cannot yet see. They have no way to answer except by guessing what the teacher wants — which is pseudo-agency by another name."

The deck-level sequence she standardized (Deck: all VT decks, v2): bellringer guess → short sim/fiddle → contrast ("CASE A / CASE B") → "Build a rule from what you just saw / Finish this sentence as a rule:" → pattern-break slide that tests the rule ("Does your rule still hold?") → "Keep going" stretch → three-tier concept question → first answer on the response slide → Day 3 revision. Every thinking slide carries the same instruction line, verbatim across decks: "Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write."

Pattern-break slides put the break in her voice, e.g.: "Your rule said energy passes upward. But decomposers eat dead things at every level — dead grass, dead rabbits, dead hawks. Where does a decomposer fit — 10% from one level, or energy from all levels at once? Does your rule still hold?" (Deck: Cycle 02 — Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships, slide ~12). And the orchid-bee symbiosis break: "Who benefits? Who, if anyone, is harmed? A new type — or does your rule still work if you apply it carefully?" (same deck).

"Keep going" stretch questions are explicitly no-lookup: "If energy is lost at every step and never comes back, why hasn't life run out of energy? There's no answer to look up. Think it through and take your best guess." (Deck: Cycle 02, slide 13)

### Students formulating understanding in their own words

The first stage of her four-stage progression (Ch5): "Students put the content in their own words, in writing, without being given an answer to copy or a blank to fill. The slides are their reference. ... I am watching not for accuracy but for whether students have a working model at all — whether they can produce a sentence that shows genuine contact with the concept."

Her classroom protocol for the formulation pause (Ch5, "Two things per class"):

> "Inserted question slides ask students to put the previous content in their own words. I ask for a thumbs up when most seem done. I do not go over the answer — that is critical. If I answer it, students just wait for me to. Instead: discuss your answer with a partner, go back and check the slides, or come check with me if you are still uncertain. When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it."

Why notes are due before the bell — her rationale, written directly to students on a slide (Deck: Cycle 17 — Darwin & Evidence, slide 2; repeated in Cycle 12 — Reproduction & Meiosis, slide 3):

> "The purpose of the notes is to help your brain process today's lesson while it is still fresh. Students remember ideas better when they generate explanations in their own words immediately after instruction, rather than delaying the work until later. When you wait until later: you remember less, you're more likely to copy, you miss the learning that the notes are designed to create. For that reason, students who are present complete and submit their notes before leaving class."

Slide annotation as her low-stakes on-ramp (Ch5): "I do ask students to annotate the lesson slides as a means of accountability, but I do not evaluate their answers or make them speak aloud. The annotation is an approximation of lurking that serves institutional legibility ... They are touching the content with their pen before they are asked to touch it with their voice."

The revision-prompt ending in every deck reinforces it: "Now write your revised answer ... — in your own words." (Deck notes: all VT decks)

### Conceptual development

Her four-stage progression (Ch5, "The four-stage progression") — each stage "has to be genuinely inhabited before the next is worth attempting":

1. **Conceptualizing** — own-words working model (see above). "Testing students before they have conceptual footing does not measure understanding. It confirms its absence, which they already know."
2. **Accuracy and rephrasing** — "This is where vocabulary starts to become theirs rather than the slide's. ... This is not correction in the traditional sense; it is closer to translation work. ... The goal is that the biological term stops being a label to memorize and becomes a tool they reach for because it does something their everyday language cannot."
3. **Peer discussion and collaborative sense-making** — "Students who have reached this stage begin to behave like a study group. ... I have found it powerful to have students work through assessments collaboratively at this stage. ... you can hear who is beginning to geek out by listening for the student who is teaching."
4. **Formal assessment** — "assessed in a way that does not defeat students before they begin" (see IEP section, §10).

Her mapping of Ito's phases onto Variation Theory (Ch5): "Lurking corresponds to the whole. ... Messing around corresponds to contrast and separation. ... Geeking out corresponds to fusion." And the warning: "We demand fusion-level behavior from students who are still in the whole. ... We force lurkers to talk and we block messing around — and then we wonder why the room goes quiet. The student with their head down has often been mislabeled disengaged when they were lurking."

The whole book's inversion, which the curriculum is built on (Ch5): "the right teaching generates inquiry. ... The inquiry is not where the work starts. The inquiry is what the work produces." And: "Inquiry is not the fuel you need to start the engine. It is the exhaust of an engine that is running."

## 2. Instructional moves and vehicles

### Teacher explanation

Her explicit description of how she delivers direct instruction (Ch5, "Two things per class"):

> "My slides carry a fair amount of text — to support students who were absent or are reviewing — but I scan each one and then talk freely. Reading the slides as written would be deadening. I highlight important words with my marker. I draw where it helps."

Why direct instruction is not the enemy (Ch5): she "refuses the banking position" but "I bring the conceptual material. I curate the shared object. I have decided, before class begins, what critical aspect this lesson is going to make perceivable." The vehicle judgment: "This is why a lesson can fail with a beautifully designed slide and succeed with a clumsy sketch on the board. ... The judgment is not about producing the best vehicle. It is about producing whatever vehicle makes the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students in this room on this day."

On question difficulty vs. language difficulty: "I work to make the questions hard because the thinking is hard, not because the syntax is hard — obscure phrasing creates a pseudo-rigor that tests familiarity with school language rather than grasp of the concept." (Ch5)

### Demonstrations

GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES as a distinct category. She does not discuss teacher-performed demonstrations separately. Closest source material: her "vehicles" framework (Ch5, "Vehicles: the sim, the activity, the lab") treats every vehicle — slide contrast, sim, artifact pair, board sketch — by one test: "does this vehicle make the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students, and is it the best available vehicle for that purpose?" TRANSFER: any demonstration guidance in teacher docs should be derived from that single test and flagged, not attributed to her as demonstration-specific reasoning.

### Examples and contrasts

The Montessori lineage of her contrast design (Ch5):

> "The pink tower varies one thing — size — while holding color, shape, and material constant, so that size is the only difference the child's hand and eye can find. ... This is exactly the logic Variation Theory names as variation against invariance: move the one critical aspect, hold everything else still, and the aspect becomes perceptible because it is the only thing changing."

> "What I am trying to do is carry that same solution into content that cannot be held in the hand, and into a room of thirty students rather than one child on a rug. The contrast on the slide, the variable in the sim, the artifact pair on the counter — these are my attempt to do at scale, and for abstract ideas, what the pink tower does in the hand for size."

VT's grammar in her summary (Ch5): "contrast that varies only the critical aspect ... pattern breaks that interrupt the over-generalization the contrast will produce ... fusion that puts the parts back into the whole."

Her concrete contrast examples: green plant vs. Indian pipe; pulmonary vein/artery vs. the memorized rule; standard case vs. edge case ("Is a virus alive?"). In the decks: decomposers breaking the upward-energy rule, the orchid that deceives bees breaking the who-benefits rule (Deck: Cycle 02); "a scar, bigger muscle, or stretched neck may change an organism" as the acquired-trait pattern break (Deck: Cycle 17); "If you only had a photo, which group would you put each one in? Why does the 'looks like' rule fail here?" (Deck: Classifying_Organisms). The unit descriptions frame contrast as the load-bearing move per unit, e.g. "Setting that against the one-way flow of energy from the previous unit is what makes each idea finally hold still." (UD, Carbon Cycle)

### Matching/card activities

It is the Montessori logic again, made into furniture." (Ch5)
- "Christian ... got into the evolutionary-periods task cards on his own." (Ch5) — task cards as self-selected extension work.

### Open activities

Her design statement (Ch5, "Vehicles"):

> "Most of the activities I design are not for output. They are for noticing. I want students to handle something, notice something about it, and have a place to record what they noticed. The activity exists to put the critical aspect under their fingers and in front of their eyes. The output, if any, is a small note about what changed, what surprised them, what they have a question about. Drawing, when it serves the noticing, is a legitimate form."

> "A worksheet fits meaning to a form the student has not made. A noticing activity gives the student something to make meaning of."

Deck examples: the two-day food-web build students construct themselves (UD #1; Deck: Cycle 02); Energy Flow Stations, "Visit the stations in any order. Read the steps, do the task, write your answer, and check the box." (Deck: Cycle 03); the carbon-atom dice journey — "Did everyone's atom take the same path? Was there a station where you got stuck for a long time — and why?" (Deck: Cycle 04); four hands-on data-generation activities in natural selection (UD #17: "it holds students to the mechanism with data they generate themselves (a moth hunt, a bean-population test, a beak-and-seed round, an allele-frequency count)").

### Labs

Her lab philosophy, verbatim (Ch5, "Vehicles"):

> "Labs done soberly. Labs cost time, setup, and class periods. I do them when the lab is the right way to teach the critical aspect — not because the curriculum prescribed a lab this week, and not because the lab is the photogenic part of biology. The question I ask before scheduling one is whether the embodied nature of the lab actually matters for what I want students to discern. If yes, it is worth the time. If no, the lab is theater that takes time from the real lesson, and I have learned to refuse the theater."

On directions:

> "I used to treat labs like they were being prepared for FDA approval — serious steps, fine-print directions. Now I am messier. Directions are scant. If someone does it wrong, that is something to think about, not a failure to remediate. The lab is more honest when the student's noticing is what the lab is for, rather than adherence to a procedure."

Lab safety, taught once at the first real lab and in plain terms (Deck: Cycle 05 — Photosynthesis, slide 10): "This is our first real lab, so we start with safety. Today's lab uses only water, baking soda, and spinach - all safe. But we build good habits now for later labs that use real chemicals. The rules: wear goggles when told, never eat or drink anything in lab, handle equipment gently, clean up your station, and wash your hands when you finish. If something spills or breaks, tell the teacher - do not clean it up yourself. ... The habits carry into every lab this year."

### Models

Her sim reasoning transfers to every manipulable model (Ch5): "What makes the sim work is not that it is interactive or digital. It is that the variable they manipulate is the critical aspect itself. ... They are not being asked to memorize a dependency; they are given an object that lets them feel it."

Physical models in the products: pipe-cleaner chromosome build, gene map, class allele survey (Deck: Cycle 15 — Genes & Chromosomes, Teacher Navigation slide); Pop Bead Meiosis lab (UD #11); build-a-sugar-molecule (UD #4); DNA-replication role stations "HELICASE, DNA POLYMERASE, and LIGASE" (Deck: Cycle 14). Digital "tank" models recur across decks ("Explore — the Energy Tank Model ... drag the sliders — change the transfer efficiency and watch the tanks", Deck: Cycle 02; carbon/respiration/allele/biodiversity tank models in Cycles 04, 06, 18, 19).

Her caution about models and diagrams (Ch5, "What disciplines pose"): "Students can memorize a labeled diagram of a feedback loop and still miss how a system regulates itself. ... the diagram shows the parts but not the dynamic. Students see the representation and mistake it for the thing." TRANSFER: model-based docs should make the dynamic, not the labeled parts, the target.

### Purposeful exploratory messing around

Messing around is a legitimate learning phase, not off-task time (Ch5): "Messing around corresponds to contrast and separation. Students begin to poke at differences ... to feel the limits of their initial generalization." Her strongest activity ideal: "something students play around with rather than something they are told" (Ch5); the sim "lets them poke at something until they have a feel for it" (Ch5). The colored-prey natural-selection activity is her model case: "an activity students run rather than read ... so they watch the population's color shift across generations. The thing they cannot help noticing, because it is the only thing the activity lets vary, is that no individual prey ever changed." (Ch5)

Deck instantiation: "Play one minute in the LIGHT forest, then one minute in the DARK forest. Notice which color moth you catch fastest in each." (Deck: Cycle 18); "Play through one round. Notice what has to go IN for the plant to make food, and what comes OUT." (Deck: Cycle 05).

## 3. Attention, time, and the real classroom

### Attention

> "Students do not only need information or activities. They need help attending. They need help noticing. They need opportunities to articulate half-formed understandings, misconceptions, intuitions, and confusions while someone is actually there to respond. Learning is often less about delivering content than about helping students sustain meaningful contact with an object of study long enough for discernment to emerge." (Ch6)

The classroom's whole architecture is shared attention (Ch5): "There is something specific being held in the room, and the fact that we are all looking at the same thing is what makes the rest of what happens possible." And students can tell whether the teacher is attending: "Students feel when teaching becomes accelerated management rather than shared attention. They experience the difference between a teacher who is cognitively available and one who is merely trying to survive the period." (Ch6)

### Classroom breathing room

Her diagnosis of pace as the enemy of depth (Ch6):

> "This may be one of the hidden problems of modern schooling: the pace itself often destroys the possibility of depth. Students are asked to touch ideas briefly, perform understanding quickly, and move on before conceptual relationships stabilize. Under those conditions, school can become an exercise in procedural survival rather than intellectual involvement."

What slowing down looked like in her own room (Ch6): "some of the most important shifts occurred when I stopped trying to rush students through material and instead stayed among them longer while they worked. I found myself talking with students more, asking questions more slowly, noticing patterns in their thinking, and allowing concepts to remain alive in the room over longer stretches of time." Her evolution unit ran "several weeks circulating through related activities instead of rapidly moving from topic to topic. ... the slower structure appeared to create something that is often missing in school: repeated contact with ideas."

And the teacher-side mirror (Ch6): "Many of us operate in a constant state of urgency, trying to maximize output under impossible conditions. But that urgency changes the emotional and cognitive ecology of the classroom."

The specific list of real-classroom frictions (attendance, late arrivals, passes, drills, assemblies, tech problems) in design rule 10 is the build brief's operationalization; her sources give the principle, not that inventory. Mark derived text accordingly.

### Interruptions

Closest: the breathing-room and pacing material above, and Ch6's list of what consumes teachers ("pacing guides, grading, planning, data collection, parent communication, compliance documentation, and differentiated materials pushes teachers into a permanent state of cognitive triage").

### Unequal student work speed / Early finishers / Slower students

Her differentiation frame is respect-based, not easier/harder (Ch5, "The skull station"):

> "The standard differentiation language is calibrated by perceived ability and produces visibly different tasks for visibly different students — which telegraphs to everyone in the room which students have been judged less capable. Respect-based differentiation asks a different question: what does this student need in order to be respected as a knower right now?"

> "For a student who would be overwhelmed by too much at once, the simpler version of the timeline is a way in — not less, but the version that lets them be a knower in this moment. For the smart, turned-off student who has been bored and insulted by school for years, the harder version ... is a way of saying I see what you are capable of and I am not pretending otherwise."

Early finishers — the Jair/Christian passage (Ch5):

> "Jair ... is intelligent and earns good grades, but when the tasks are not complex he is done early and spends the period joking with friends, because there is nothing in the room that has yet met him. I told him, mildly but plainly, that he has learned he is smart and gets stuff done but does not challenge himself, that my classroom does make challenges available, and that I expect him to take them."

> "Much of teaching is making the harder thing available without forcing it, and reading which student is ready to reach for it on which day."

For students already ahead: "For students already geeking out, I sometimes offer what looks like a task that helps the classroom — organizing or making materials — but is really a way of meeting the level they are working at. I tell them: you can say no. You can stop when you want. The agency stays theirs." (Ch5)

Slower students — the entry-point principle from the Toma passage (Ch5): "The framework was the right one. The entry point was what had needed adjusting — to simplify without reducing, the whole first, one critical aspect at a time, one contrast, a conceptualization in their own words, and to meet students where they could be successful and build from there. Why is that not the assumption for every classroom?"

Product-level structure for pace differences — the CORE/EXTEND split, in her own product language (UD): "It is designed so resistant learners get solid footing on the core idea, with a second deck to extend the unit when a class moves faster." (UD #6); "Use the core deck alone for a resistant-learner pace, or add the second to extend." (UD #6); "built as four lesson decks so you can go as deep as a class can handle" (UD #15). In-deck stretch layers: "Keep going" slides and the Mastery tier serve fast thinkers without new logistics (Deck: all VT decks).

### Classroom ownership

The class builds its own knowledge base through the dialogic register (Ch5):

> "By the middle of the year the class has its own corpus — cases, questions, and small disagreements no other classroom will have, because no other classroom contains these students. The knowledge is theirs, partly, because they made it."

Student contributions "enter the room as contributions, not as deviations" — "to let what they notice enter the room as something the room now knows." (Ch5) Capstone-level ownership move written to students: "Pick ONE of the big ideas below. In a few sentences, write how it connects to your own life. ... There's no right answer. The connection is yours." (Deck: Cycle 20 — Human Impact Capstone)

## 4. Integrity, grading, and growth

### Copying

Her design position: remove the payoff rather than police the act. "A lesson built around a critical aspect or two, where students had to actually think — not copy, not guess from context." (Ch5) "The thought questions do not have answers you can copy; they require students to apply a distinction they have just made to a new case, or to notice where a pattern breaks down." (Ch5) What-ifs "have no answer to copy." (Ch5)

Her student-facing statement of why delay produces copying (Deck: Cycle 17, slide 2; Cycle 12, slide 3): "When you wait until later: you remember less, you're more likely to copy, you miss the learning that the notes are designed to create."

The Toma moment shows what she praises: "his own sentences, his own working-out, nothing copied — I said: Hey, all this you wrote in your own words? It's very good. Please continue." (Ch5)

(The v2 pipeline's integrity gate — first-answer copied→flag, AI-written→auto-fail — lives in the formative-pipeline spec, outside this corpus; note the consistency, source it there.)

### Completion grading

The standing student-facing rule, verbatim on the closing checklist of the decks (Deck: Cycle 02, 03, 04, 05, 06, 07, 08–20 closing slides):

> "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade."

The teacher-facing note in every response slide's speaker notes (Deck notes: all VT decks): "TEACHER: the finished deck (first + revised answers) is the single artifact that runs through the AI pipeline → each student's printable Growth Report + class summary. Completion = both boxes filled."

Why grades still exist at all (Ch5): "Grades heavy enough to matter — I am not going to pretend ninth-graders will engage purely for intrinsic reasons, and they need the grades to navigate the system. What I was not willing to simplify was the thinking itself."

Her credit standard for developing answers (Ch5): "I remind them constantly: I care about your thinking, not the answer. 'Because' is not an answer. 'I don't know' is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy."

### Proficiency

The completion/proficiency separation is structural: completion is participation in thinking (both boxes filled); proficiency is demonstrated understanding, read from the growth artifacts — "Students see how their thinking changed; you see what the class is ready for next. No stack of papers to grade." (UD, through-line). The class summary tells the teacher "who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed" (UD #1) — every unit description ends with a who-can/who-is-still proficiency read.

Proficiency is discernment, not vocabulary (Ch5): "A student who discerns those has understood natural selection, even if their vocabulary is still rough."

The three-tier concept questions operationalize levels without separate tracks: Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery, with the word bank "use any, modify any, or use none" (Deck: all VT decks). Students self-locate on the same scale: "Rate yourself ... Before this lesson: Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery. Right now: ..." (Deck: Cycle 20, "Then and Now" slide; also Cycles 02, 12).

Assessment that measures understanding rather than tolerance for confusion: see IEP section (§10).

### Revision

Revision is the payoff of the whole arc, and the point is visible change. Student-facing, verbatim (Deck: all VT decks, response slides):

> "Write your first answer now. On Day 3, open this slide's Notes, run the prompt, and write your revised answer below — leave both showing. Seeing your thinking change is the point."

Day 3 divider: "Day 3 of 3 — see your thinking change. Go back to your answers and revise them — right on the slides. ... Write your revised answer in the 'Your revised answer' area, right under your first. Leave both showing — the change is the point." (Deck: Cycle 07, 08, 20 and others)

The revision is the student's own: the AI "helps you see your own thinking so you improve it yourself" and must "stop so I can write my own revised answer" (Deck notes: standardized revision prompt, all decks). Book-level ground for revision as the marker of learning (Ch5): "I am watching differently now — listening ... for the moment a student starts to revise what they thought."

### Intellectual risk

Why silence at hard questions is not disengagement (Ch5):

> "My participation rates at the what-if stage are still low. Students are not yet confident enough to commit to a position in public, especially when there is no right answer to hide behind. But I have stopped reading that silence as disengagement. It is, more often, the silence of genuine thinking — of students working out what they believe before risking it. That working-out is exactly what I want. The stance comes first in private; with time, and a classroom culture that receives it safely, it begins to surface in public."

Preconditions for risk (Ch5): "before students have the confidence to dialogue in public, they need confidence in their own thinking." And the anti-defeat calibration: "I watch for the threshold where the task tips from challenging into defeating. Too hard and they stop; too easy and they are bored. The window is narrow and it moves."

### Student safety in exposing incomplete thinking

The skull-station effect (Ch5): "students surface partial knowledge they would not risk in front of the full class. A student offers an idea they are not sure about, half-finished, sometimes wrong in a way more interesting than being right."

Structural protections she names: annotation is required but not evaluated and not spoken aloud (Ch5); early testing "does not measure understanding. It confirms its absence, which they already know" (Ch5); partial understandings are received as "partial understandings of something, whose divergent answers are evidence of what they are noticing" (Ch5). The feedback register never uses "the language of deficit" (Ch6). The "got me" look — "the look of someone who has just found out that what they think actually matters to the person they are thinking with" — is what the safety is for (Ch5).

TRANSFER: this is the book-side ground for design rule 12 (grading every developing answer for correctness discourages intellectual risk) — the transfer to completion-based formative grading is direct.

## 5. Feedback and AI

### Formative feedback

The arithmetic problem AI solves (Ch6): "a single teacher cannot personally hear from every student in the room every day. Lezir got several minutes that Tuesday. Other students got none. Across a week, the arithmetic catches up. The shy student making real progress who never volunteers; the student who was absent ... the student who is half-right in a way that needs to be named for them to take the next step — these students sit at the edge of my attention."

Her feedback instructions — "the pedagogy of the last chapter, turned into instructions" (Ch6):

> "Do not grade. Do not produce a corrected version. Read the student as a knower. Name where their reasoning was already pointing at something true, even when the final answer was not. Name what their sentence reveals about how they are thinking. Name where their thinking has become more skilled than it was last time, so the growth is visible to them and not only to me. Never use the language of deficit. Speak to them the way I would crouch beside them in class — only on paper, and for every student, including the ones I missed that week."

The student-side revision prompt she standardized into every deck (Deck notes: all VT decks, verbatim): the AI "will NOT give you the answer — it helps you see your own thinking so you improve it yourself"; it must "Tell me one thing I got right, and quote my own words back to me. Ask me two questions that make me look again at one idea I might have wrong or left out. Name one distinction or example worth thinking about. Keep it short and in plain language, then stop so I can write my own revised answer."

In-person feedback habit (Ch5): "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it."

### AI

Her one-sentence position (Ch6): "AI helps make the work doable. It is not the doer."

What it does and must not do (Ch6): "What it produces is the production work of teaching. ... It drafts lesson outlines I then adjust. It generates slides I scan and talk over rather than read. It drafts feedback in a register I have specified. It writes IEP-accessible versions of an assessment I would otherwise have written by hand at midnight. None of that is teaching. Teaching is the shared object, the dialogic catch, the read on who is lurking and who is ready to be pushed, the moment a student's face changes when she finds out her thinking has weight. The AI does none of that, and it must not be allowed to, because that part is what students need from a person and from no one else."

Her three cautions, plainly (Ch6):
1. "the AI is confidently wrong in a particular and dangerous way: it can affirm reasoning that is actually mistaken, or invent a 'real part' of a student's answer that is not there, in fluent, encouraging prose. ... That is exactly why I read everything before it goes out."
2. "the defaults have to be overridden every single time. ... The work of telling it where its pedagogical lines are is itself a pedagogical act, and it belongs to the teacher."
3. "most teachers using AI right now are not overriding the defaults. ... Used that way, AI does not relieve the problem this book describes. It accelerates it, with a friendly interface and the word 'personalized' attached. The students who were already misread will be misread faster, more confidently, and at greater scale."

The pedagogy-underneath claim (Ch6): "AI built on a pedagogy of content-filling — answer-getting, output-scoring, coverage — will harm students, and it will harm hardest the students who were already being harmed. AI built on, or bent toward, a pedagogy that treats students as knowers will serve them. The tool is the same tool. The pedagogy underneath it is the whole difference."

Her limit prediction (Ch6): "I do not think students, without human interaction, will find AI sufficient for learning. ... The thing my students needed from me was never information; it was a person who shared an object of attention with them and received their thinking as real. A machine can extend that ... but it cannot originate it."

Portability caveat she insists on (Ch6): "I am not describing a product, and I am not recommending a particular tool. ... The principle is portable; the particular way I do it probably is not."

### Human gating

The architecture, in her words (Ch6):

> "I read every piece of it before it goes to a student, and I adjust it as needed. That is not a small caveat. It is the whole architecture. The AI's draft is exactly that: a draft, produced fast, for me to check against what I actually know about the student and the concept. ... The AI helps make it doable. I remain the doer. The feedback is mine; the AI just got me to a first draft I could stand at."

> "The reading is not quality control bolted on. It is the place the human judgment lives."

Why the machine's defaults require the gate (Ch6): "Left to its defaults, the AI does the opposite of what I want. Its native register is to judge: right or wrong, here is the corrected version, moving on. Pointed at a partial answer, it calls the answer deficient. Pointed at a divergent answer — the kind that is actually an epistemic move — it calls it wrong. A student still in the lurking phase reads, to it, as a student who has failed to participate. This is not a flaw I can toggle off. It is the shape of the thing."

### Teacher noticing

The shift she describes as the product of the whole redesign (Ch5, "Double booking"):

> "The work has moved from managing activities to noticing agency. My practices have become simpler — two things per class, real questions, thinking rewarded rather than correctness. What has become more complex is what I notice. I am watching differently now — listening for the beginning of interpretation, for the moment a student starts to revise what they thought, for the places where the room opens and the places where it closes down."

Proximity as stance, not technique (Ch5): "I move among students during activities and labs — talking over aspects together, noticing their ideas, crouching beside someone who is stuck. Students feel less like objects of my teaching and more like workmates in a shared process. That is not a technique. It is a stance."

The dialogic register only a person can run (Ch5): "The vehicle can hold the room. It cannot answer his specific question. ... Only a person can do that, in real time, in the seconds before the moment closes." Reading readiness by feel (Ch5): "I calibrate by feel: after a few slides of lurking, do I start to see enough confidence to risk an answer out loud? When answers become more thought-out and less random, we move. When they don't, I don't push."

Misreading as the deepest harm (Ch6): "A book whose whole argument is that the deepest harm is misreading a student as a knower..." — TRANSFER: technology-in-classroom docs should frame every tool by whether it extends this noticing (the Growth Report and class summary are noticing instruments: "you see what the class is ready for next," UD through-line).

### IEP-related classroom evidence

> "Many of my students have IEPs. Testing that is inaccessible in its language or overwhelming in its length does not measure biological understanding; it measures tolerance for confusion. I differentiate not by lowering the conceptual bar but by removing the linguistic obstacles — accessible phrasing, manageable length, questions calibrated to what I have actually seen students engage with."

### Curriculum pacing

Pacing is kept by depth, not despite it (Ch5, "Double booking"):

> "And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now — because I demand less compliant paper-pushing and focus directly on students' thinking and the activities that support it. Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly."

Slower is not behind (Ch6): the multi-week circulating evolution unit "felt inefficient compared to the traditional pacing of biology courses. Yet the slower structure appeared to create something that is often missing in school: repeated contact with ideas. ... The classroom began to feel less like a race through information and more like sustained engagement with a developing set of questions."

The whole is entered first, one aspect at a time (Ch5, Toma): "the whole first, one critical aspect at a time, one contrast, a conceptualization in their own words."

### Standards

Her double-booking stance — standards visible, thinking central (Ch5):

> "From the outside, this classroom still looks compliant. Objectives are visible. Vocabulary gets covered. The pacing guide would not immediately flag anything wrong. This is intentional."

> "Teachers who want to serve students honestly inside constrained systems often have no choice but to run two curricula at once: the one the institution can see, and the one that actually develops in students' minds. The first is the price of operating. The second is the reason to operate."

Where standards live in the products: NGSS Performance Expectations sit on the never-projected teacher-reference slide of every deck (Deck: all VT decks, slide 1), and "NGSS alignment for administrators lives in the separate one-sheet" (UD header) — standards documentation is deliberately separated from the operating documents. Critical aspects are "not the vocabulary, not the standards list, but the discernments the whole topic depends on." (Ch5)

### Teacher workload

The production-work diagnosis (Ch6): "For years, many teachers have survived by becoming managers of throughput. The pressure of pacing guides, grading, planning, data collection, parent communication, compliance documentation, and differentiated materials pushes teachers into a permanent state of cognitive triage."

Her numbers, with her own hedge (Ch6): "The production work — the slides, the differentiated versions, the feedback drafts, the IEP-accessible assessments — is roughly forty percent of what my job used to demand of my hours. When AI takes on that forty percent, the time comes back to me, and it goes into presence. ... My rough sense is that this shows up as somewhere between a twenty and sixty percent increase in the time I actually spend present with students, depending on the week. I want to be plain that this is an estimate from my own practice — a feel, not a measurement."

What the freed time is for (Ch6): "standing at the counter, crouching beside a stuck student, running the dialogic register for more of the room." The skull station "works [because] I am free to be at the counter — not preparing tomorrow's lesson, not formatting feedback, not writing IEP-accessible versions of an assessment in that moment." (Ch5)

The equity claim, stated narrowly (Ch6): "The barrier in a Title I classroom was never that the teacher did not know how to teach this way. The barrier was time: the production work consumed the attention that presence requires. Offload the production and the presence becomes affordable." With her guardrail: "Pedagogy mitigates structural problems; it does not cure them." And the teacher-facing promise ceiling (UD through-line): "No stack of papers to grade."
