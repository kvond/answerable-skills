# Activities, Models, and Labs Guide

Purpose: what each type of activity in Answerable Biology is actually for — matching/card activities, open activities, models, and labs have different instructional jobs — with real examples from the decks, and a catalog of every activity in the corpus with its type, cycle, and rights status.

How this guide fits the system: the "Year Arc" shows where each activity sits in its cycle; each cycle's "Unit Agenda" and the "Ecology Unit Guide" carry the run-of-day detail; supplies live in the "Master Supply System"; arrival-time and early-finisher use of these activities is covered in the Fiddles documentation; how completion is graded is in "Start Here" and the assessment documentation. Standards never attach to activities — they live on the teacher-reference slide and in "Standards and Alignment."

---

## 1. One test for every activity

Every activity, sim, card set, model, and lab in this curriculum was chosen or built against a single test, in Katherine's words:

> "does this vehicle make the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students, and is it the best available vehicle for that purpose?"

The activity is never the point. The critical aspect is: "The shared object is not the slide. It is not the sim, the lab bench, or the artifact. Those are vehicles. The shared object is the conceptual feature the vehicle is pointing at."

Each cycle carries two or three critical aspects, and "each critical aspect gets tied to an activity built specifically for discerning that aspect, and built, where I can manage it, as something students play around with rather than something they are told." Her test for whether an activity earns its place: "does it isolate the aspect, so that the thing the misconception gets wrong is the one thing the student cannot help noticing? If it does, it earns its place."

The design lineage is Montessori's pink tower — vary one thing, hold everything else still:

> "The pink tower varies one thing — size — while holding color, shape, and material constant, so that size is the only difference the child's hand and eye can find. ... The contrast on the slide, the variable in the sim, the artifact pair on the counter — these are my attempt to do at scale, and for abstract ideas, what the pink tower does in the hand for size."

The types below are not a hierarchy. A card sort is not a lesser activity than a lab; it has a different job. What ruins an activity is asking it to do a job it was not built for — treating a vocabulary sort as proof of deep understanding, or burying a lab's one noticing under twelve procedural steps.

## 2. Matching and card activities — the shared floor

The job: establish vocabulary, classifications, and structures so that every student in the room holds the same basic pieces before the thinking questions arrive. Matching and card work lowers the language load and the cognitive load — the terms are on the table, physically, instead of held in memory — which is exactly what the word banks do on the concept-question slides ("use any, modify any, or use none"). It creates a floor everyone can stand on.

What it is graded as: completion. A finished sort is participation, not demonstrated proficiency, and it is never presented to students, parents, or a gradebook as proof of deep understanding. The distinction is structural in this curriculum: "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." Proficiency is read from the revision artifacts, not from card work — a student can match every term to its definition and still reconstruct the misconception the moment the cards are put away. (Katherine states this test-proof point about vocabulary directly: "A student who has memorized the vocabulary but not discerned them has understood nothing.")

GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES: Katherine does not write out a matching-activity philosophy in her own words in this corpus; the framing above is derived from her completion/proficiency reasoning and word-bank practice, which it is consistent with. REVIEW WITH KATHERINE for a paragraph in her voice.

Real examples from the decks:

- Linnaean cup sort (Cycle 01, Classifying Organisms). Lab groups order cups labeled with the seven classification levels "from MOST general to MOST specific" — "When you think you have the correct order, call your teacher over." Vocabulary as objects in the hand, a check that is a conversation rather than a score, and a completion moment built into the directions.
- Energy Flow Stations (Cycle 03). A station rotation that is mostly card work, in the deck's own words: "Station 1 - Card Match: match each term or organism to its role. Station 2 - Build the Pyramid: sort the animal cards by feeding type and stack them producers on the bottom, top predator on top. Station 4 - Photosynthesis vs. Respiration: sort the cards into the process that STORES the sun's energy vs. the one that RELEASES it." Students visit in any order, do the task, write the answer, check the box. (The deck lists Stations 1, 2, and 4 — no Station 3 — REVIEW WITH KATHERINE.)
- Screen-Door Sort (Cycle 09). Sort six cards — oxygen, water, a large protein, a salt ion, a virus, CO2 — into CROSSES FREELY vs. BLOCKED / NEEDS HELP, then write a one-sentence generalization. Note the design: the sort builds the floor, and the closing sentence is the step up from the floor toward the critical aspect (selective permeability). That one sentence is the part worth reading.
- Lock & Key Match (Cycle 10). Paper enzyme-and-substrate cutouts matched by shape — including one deliberately warped active site that matches nothing. A card set with a pattern-break baked in: the miss is the point.
- Same Trait, Two Environments (Cycle 18). Trait and environment cards paired and reasoned about — the same trait card wins in one environment and loses in the other, which is Critical Aspect 2 of the cycle made physical.
- Karyotype sort (Cycle 16, Mutations & Genetic Disorders). Chromosome images sorted to spot the extra chromosome — classification work in service of gene-vs-chromosomal mutation.

Two working rules. First, card work is front-of-cycle work: it belongs on Day 1 or as the floor-setting step of a station day, before the contrast and pattern-break ask students to think with the terms. Second, finished card sets are reusable — the Screen-Door cards re-sort for early finishers, the Lock & Key cutouts work as transition puzzles, checkpoint cards re-deal as a quick game. See the Fiddles documentation.

## 3. Open activities — manipulate, compare, notice

The job: put the critical aspect under students' fingers and let them find it. Students manipulate something, change a variable, compare cases, build or model something of their own, and notice the critical relationship because it is the thing the activity lets vary. Katherine's design statement, verbatim:

> "Most of the activities I design are not for output. They are for noticing. I want students to handle something, notice something about it, and have a place to record what they noticed. The activity exists to put the critical aspect under their fingers and in front of their eyes. The output, if any, is a small note about what changed, what surprised them, what they have a question about. Drawing, when it serves the noticing, is a legitimate form."

> "A worksheet fits meaning to a form the student has not made. A noticing activity gives the student something to make meaning of."

Productive "messing around" is a legitimate learning phase here, not off-task time. In her mapping of Ito's phases onto Variation Theory: "Messing around corresponds to contrast and separation. Students begin to poke at differences ... to feel the limits of their initial generalization." The instruction language in the decks says so plainly — "Play one minute in the LIGHT forest, then one minute in the DARK forest. Notice which color moth you catch fastest in each." (Cycle 18); "Play through one round. Notice what has to go IN for the plant to make food, and what comes OUT." (Cycle 05). The verb is notice, and it appears on the slide.

Real examples from the decks:

- The two-day food-web build (Cycle 02). Students sort species cards into producers/consumers/decomposers, then draw the arrows themselves — who eats whom — and on Day 2 stack four organisms into a pyramid and run 10,000 units through the 10% rule. The web is something they made, which is why Break the Web works later: remove one species, trace every arrow that touched it, predict a year and a generation out. Arrows drawn "wrong" are something to talk over at the table, not a failure to remediate.
- Journey through the carbon cycle (Cycle 04). Each student plays one carbon atom, rolls dice at stations, and logs the moves. The debrief questions are the noticing: "Did everyone's atom take the same path? Was there a station where you got stuck for a long time — and why?"
- Build-a-Bilayer (Cycle 09). Draw eight phospholipids arranged against water, label the water-loving heads and water-fearing tails — drawing in service of noticing why the oily middle decides what crosses.
- Unzip and Rebuild, and the Replication Assembly Line (Cycle 14). Build a partner strand from a given strand, trade and compare; then process a paper strand through three stations — HELICASE, DNA POLYMERASE, LIGASE. Students are the enzymes; the pairing rule is the thing they cannot help using.
- The Cycle 18 quartet — the year's clearest case of data students generate themselves: the Peppered Moth One-Minute Hunt (catch data in a light forest, then a dark one); the Variation vs. No Variation bean-population lab ("Make two bean populations: Population A is all one color; Population B is a mix ... A 'predator' removes the easiest-to-see beans. Do this for 3 rounds ... Graph how each population changes"); Battle of the Beaks ("Pick a 'beak' tool: tweezers, clothespin, or spoon. You get 30 seconds to pick up 'seeds.' ... The environment changes: now only large, tough seeds remain"); and Gene-Pool Beads ("no single bead ever changed color, so what actually evolved?"). Four rounds of the same discernment — selection acts on variation, and no individual ever changes — each generating numbers the students made.
- Galapagos postcard task (Cycle 17) and OneZoom exploration (Cycles 01 and 19) — open exploration with a specific hunt: "Find a pair that look almost the same but are far apart on the tree. Find a pair that look very different but are close cousins."

What to collect: the small note. What changed, what surprised you, what do you have a question about. Do not convert an open activity into a worksheet by bolting a page of questions onto it — the output requirement should stay light enough that noticing remains the work.

## 4. Models — the variable they move is the aspect itself

Models are open activities with one special property: the manipulable variable is the critical aspect. Katherine's reasoning about sims transfers to every model, physical or digital:

> "What makes the sim work is not that it is interactive or digital. It is that the variable they manipulate is the critical aspect itself. ... They are not being asked to memorize a dependency; they are given an object that lets them feel it."

The interactive tank-and-flow models are the house-built version of this — the same visual metaphor accumulating meaning across the year: tanks of stored energy or carbon, flows between them, sliders on the one thing that matters. The family: the Energy Tank Model ("drag the sliders — change the transfer efficiency and watch the tanks," Cycles 02–03), the Carbon Tank Model (Cycles 04 and 20), Photosynthesis Rate Model (05), Respiration ATP Model (06), Osmosis Model (09), Enzyme Rate Model (10), Cell Cycle Model (11), Allele Tank Model (18), Biodiversity Tank Model (19). Operational flag before anything ships: only the Cycle 02 Netlify deployment of the Energy Tank Model is currently live; all nine answerable-teaching.github.io model URLs return 404 — REVIEW WITH KATHERINE (redeploy or repoint), LINK NEEDED for the eight dead models.

Physical models do the same job with hands instead of sliders:

- Pop Bead Meiosis lab (Cycle 12 CORE) — chromosomes as snap-together beads, halving and reshuffling performed rather than described. (The Process of Meiosis deck that carries it is SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED; the lab is named in the Unit Descriptions.)
- Build a sugar molecule (Cycle 05) — model kits: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O rearranged into C6H12O6 + 6 O2, with the constraint that carries the whole point: "Every atom has to be used - none get thrown away." Conservation of matter enforced by the kit.
- Pipe-cleaner chromosome build, gene map, and class allele survey (Cycle 15) — gene, locus, and allele as places and versions on a thing you made.
- Modeling Transcription with letter tiles (Cycle 15 Protein Synthesis) — the no-subscription alternative to the Gizmo, and the default path (see section 6).
- The Activation-Energy Hill (Cycle 10) — a marble over a cardboard hill; lower the hill. The enzyme's job in one move.
- THE DOG SAT (Cycle 16) — a sentence read in threes; delete one letter and re-read: HED OGS AT. The frameshift, felt in language before it is named in genetics. No materials at all.

One caution from the book applies to every model and labeled diagram: "Students can memorize a labeled diagram of a feedback loop and still miss how a system regulates itself. ... Students see the representation and mistake it for the thing." The target of a model activity is the dynamic — what happens when the slider moves, when the letter is deleted, when the beads are drawn — never the labeled parts. If the debrief question can be answered from the diagram without running the model, it is the wrong debrief question.

External sims and games (BioMan, PhET, Learn.Genetics, Ask A Biologist) are used the same way, usually in the fiddle or bellringer slot with a one-line noticing instruction. Their rights handling is in section 6.

## 5. Labs — hands-on is not automatically better

Katherine's lab philosophy, verbatim, is the whole section:

> "Labs done soberly. Labs cost time, setup, and class periods. I do them when the lab is the right way to teach the critical aspect — not because the curriculum prescribed a lab this week, and not because the lab is the photogenic part of biology. The question I ask before scheduling one is whether the embodied nature of the lab actually matters for what I want students to discern. If yes, it is worth the time. If no, the lab is theater that takes time from the real lesson, and I have learned to refuse the theater."

And on directions:

> "I used to treat labs like they were being prepared for FDA approval — serious steps, fine-print directions. Now I am messier. Directions are scant. If someone does it wrong, that is something to think about, not a failure to remediate. The lab is more honest when the student's noticing is what the lab is for, rather than adherence to a procedure."

The operating rules that follow:

1. A lab must earn its blocks by the same test as everything else: does the embodied part matter for the discernment? If a contrast slide or a sim teaches the aspect as well, use the cheaper vehicle.
2. Simplify the directions. Excessive procedure distracts from the science. When the direction count grows, the students' attention goes to compliance — did I do step 7? — instead of the phenomenon. Complexity belongs in the design of the lab, not in directions students must decipher.
3. Name what students are supposed to NOTICE, on the slide, in plain words. The decks model this: the Leaf-Disk Float Lab tells students "Floating disks are full of oxygen the plant just made - live proof photosynthesis is happening"; the Yeast Balloon lab names the noticing as "proof the yeast made energy with no air" — the balloon seals out oxygen and CO2 inflates it anyway. The scientific purpose is stated; the procedure serves it.
4. Build the control into the noticing. The no-CO2 cup that never floats gets its own debrief slide ("What does that tell you a plant NEEDS to make its food?"); the no-sugar bottle whose balloon stays flat; the no-fish BTB cup that stays blue. In every case the control is not a formality — it is one side of the contrast, CASE A / CASE B on the lab bench.

The corpus's labs, in this spirit:

- Leaf-Disk Float Lab (Cycle 05) — the year's first wet lab, and where lab safety is taught once, in plain terms: "Today's lab uses only water, baking soda, and spinach - all safe. But we build good habits now for later labs that use real chemicals. ... The habits carry into every lab this year." Counting floated disks each minute is deliberately built-in waiting-time work.
- Yeast Balloon Fermentation Lab (Cycle 06) — with the no-sugar control; watching the balloons inflate over 15–20 minutes is transition work by design.
- Temperature & pH Lab and the Liver-Catalase extension (Cycle 10) — catalase rate graphed against temperature and pH; the Day 2 callback (a boiled potato will not foam) turns the opening demo into the denaturing evidence.
- Variation vs. No Variation bean-population lab and Battle of the Beaks (Cycle 18) — hands-on data generation; see section 3.
- "What's in a Drop?" pond-dip (Cycle 01); PTC taste test and the named-but-absent Cheek Cell Lab (Cycle 13 — SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED); quadrat sampling (Cycle 07 — announced in the deck with no materials or directions, GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES); Oil, Water & the Bilayer mini-lab (Cycle 09, Day-3 challenge).

A note on teacher demonstrations. The corpus has two standing demos — the Fish & Bromothymol Blue demo (Cycle 06, running ambient across the whole block, with the teaching note "Hold 'respiration' and 'CO2' back — let them infer") and Potato & Peroxide (Cycle 10, with "Don't say 'enzyme' or 'catalase' yet. Let them wonder."). Katherine does not treat demonstrations as a separate category in her writing (GAP — NOT IN SOURCE NOTES); run them by the same vehicle test, and note what both existing demos share: the name of the phenomenon is deliberately withheld so the noticing comes before the vocabulary.

## 6. External activities: what ships, what links, what you write

Many activities point at external resources — BioMan games, PhET sims, HHMI interactives, Learn.Genetics, published lab methods. The full entry-by-entry audit is `ACTIVITY_SOURCE_AND_RIGHTS_AUDIT.md`; the governing rule from that audit:

> Independently written, simplified directions for using a linked resource are Katherine's own instructional design and can be sold — almost always yes. The one limit: never copy a protected resource's own directions, screenshots, worksheets, or answer keys closely enough to reproduce another creator's expression. Write directions from the classroom's point of view ("open the game, remove one species, record what happens"), not by paraphrasing the publisher's page.

So for every external activity, the product is three things: a lawful link to the source, a named attribution beside the link, and Katherine's own simplified directions — which are precisely the "name what to notice" one-liners this guide has been describing. The decks already do this correctly: "remove one species from a food web. What happens?" is her instructional design around BioMan's game, not BioMan's text. Her modifications (the control cup added to the leaf-disk method, the predict-first protocol around the Veritasium video, the data-recording round structure on the moth game) are hers and ship.
