# Start Here — How Answerable Biology Works

Purpose: the ten-minute introduction to Answerable Biology — what the curriculum is organized around, what each part of a lesson is for, and where to go next.

## What this curriculum is organized around

Answerable Biology is a full year of NGSS high-school biology in 20 flexible cycles, from Cycle 01 — Classifying Organisms to Cycle 20 — Human Impact Capstone. It is not a day-by-day pacing guide, and it is not organized around coverage. It is organized around questions and critical aspects — the small number of discernments each topic actually turns on.

A lesson can carry two or three critical aspects that actually shape understanding. Not eight facts. Two or three discernments, each of which, if a student fails to make it, leaves everything downstream incoherent. I find them by asking where students reliably go wrong, because the critical aspects of a topic are usually the exact places where the discipline's compression hides a misconception. In Cycle 02 — Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships, the two are: energy flow through trophic levels, and how two species affect each other. That's the whole cycle. Everything in the deck exists to make those two things perceivable.

The operating discipline is two things per class: a lesson built around a critical aspect or two, where students have to actually think — not copy, not guess from context — and an activity focused on the same critical aspect, not on completing a procedure for its own sake. That sounds like a reduction of ambition. It is the opposite: doing two things well takes more pedagogical work, not less, and what students find is that it's doable. Doable turns out to be the condition for genuine thought.

## The arc every core idea runs through

Students repeatedly stop and put their developing understanding in their own words — in writing, without an answer to copy or a blank to fill. Every core idea runs the same arc. Here it is as Cycle 02 actually runs it:

**First answer.** Before any teaching: "The energy in your dinner started as sunlight. Grass catches sunlight, a cow eats the grass, you eat the cow. At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy — or something else? Why?" Students write their best current thinking on a response slide. I am watching not for accuracy but for whether they have a working model at all. Every thinking slide carries the same line: Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write.

**Contrast.** Two cases that vary only the thing I want noticed. CASE A: grass — thousands of plants catching sunlight directly, roughly 10,000 calories stored across the field. CASE B: red-tailed hawks — 10 to 15 of them sharing the same meadow. "If the grass holds 10,000 calories, why can't the meadow feed 10,000 hawks?" This is the Montessori pink-tower logic carried into content that can't be held in the hand: move the one critical aspect, hold everything else still, and the aspect becomes perceptible because it is the only thing changing.

**Rule.** Students commit: "Finish this sentence as a rule: 'Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ______.'" The rule they build will be too simple. That's the point.

**Pattern-break.** A case that breaks the too-simple rule, put to them directly: "Your rule said energy passes upward. But decomposers eat dead things at every level — dead grass, dead rabbits, dead hawks. Where does a decomposer fit — 10% from one level, or energy from all levels at once? Does your rule still hold?" Pattern-breaks interrupt the over-generalization the contrast will produce. They are not gotchas; the articulation matters more than the answer.

**Revised answer.** On Day 3 students return to their first answer and revise it — right on the slide, underneath the original, with both left showing. Seeing your thinking change is the point.

Along the way, "Keep going" questions stretch whoever is ready ("If energy is lost at every step and never comes back, why hasn't life run out of energy? There's no answer to look up"), and three-tier concept questions — Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery, with a word bank students may "use any, modify any, or use none" of — let every student answer the same question at the depth they can currently reach.

## What each part of a lesson is for

Every element is a vehicle, and every vehicle answers one test: does it make the critical aspect of this lesson perceivable to these students, in this room, on this day?

**Teacher explanation.** I bring the conceptual material; I decide before class what aspect the lesson is going to make perceivable. The slides carry a fair amount of text — to support students who were absent or are reviewing — but I scan each one and talk freely. Reading slides as written would be deadening. I highlight with my marker. I draw where it helps. I make the questions hard because the thinking is hard, not because the syntax is hard.

**Examples and contrasts.** The load-bearing move. Grass beside hawks; a bee on a flower beside a mosquito on your arm ("Both are two species interacting. So what is actually different?"); a bat beside a bird in Cycle 01. A contrast varies only the critical aspect so students cannot help noticing it.

**Demonstrations.** Same test, teacher's hands: a demo earns its slot when watching the thing happen is the fastest way to make the aspect perceivable — the fish-and-BTB demo in Cycle 06, potato and peroxide in Cycle 10. If the demo is just the photogenic part of biology, it's theater.

**Simulations.** What makes a sim work is not that it's interactive or digital — it's that the variable students manipulate is the critical aspect itself. In the Energy Tank Model, students drag the transfer-efficiency slider and watch the upper tanks starve. They aren't asked to memorize the 10% rule; they're given an object that lets them feel it.

**Matching and card activities.** These establish a shared floor — vocabulary, classifications, structures everyone needs on the table. The Cycle 01 cup sort (order the seven Linnaean levels, then "call your teacher over") and the Pacific Northwest species cards in the food-web build are this kind of work. It is completion work, honestly graded as such. It is not proof of deep understanding, and nothing downstream pretends it is.

**Open activities.** Most of the activities are not for output. They are for noticing. I want students to handle something, notice something about it, and have a place to record what they noticed. A worksheet fits meaning to a form the student has not made; a noticing activity gives the student something to make meaning of. The two-day food-web build is the model case: students construct the web themselves, then take the energy-pyramid step and do the 10%-rule math on it. The bobtail squid investigation on Day 2 is another — twenty minutes with a Hawaiian squid that houses glowing bacteria in a light organ: What does the squid get? What do the bacteria get? Some activities deliberately allow productive messing around; poking at a thing until you have a feel for it is a legitimate phase of learning, not off-task time.

**Labs.** Labs done soberly. Labs cost time, setup, and class periods; I run one when the embodied nature of the lab actually matters for what students need to discern — not because the curriculum prescribed a lab this week. And the directions are deliberately scant. I used to write labs like they were being prepared for FDA approval; now, if someone does it wrong, that is something to think about, not a failure to remediate. When directions get complicated, students attend to the procedure instead of the aspect the lab exists to show them. Complexity belongs in the design, not in directions.

## Revision and feedback

The finished student deck — first answer beside revised answer, both showing — is the single artifact of the cycle. No exit tickets, no separate packet, no stack of papers to grade.

Each response slide's speaker Notes carry a standardized AI revision prompt. On Day 3 the student runs it against their own first answer. The AI will not give the answer — it tells the student one thing they got right, quoting their own words back, asks two questions that make them look again at an idea they might have wrong or left out, names one distinction worth thinking about, and stops so the student writes their own revised answer.

The teacher side: finished decks run through one batch step to produce a one-page Conceptual Growth Report per student and a class summary for you — who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed. Students see how their thinking changed; you see what the class is ready for next.

All of it is human-gated. I read every piece of feedback before it goes to a student, and I adjust it as needed. That is not a small caveat — it is the whole architecture. Left to its defaults, AI judges: right or wrong, here's the corrected version, moving on. Pointed at a partial answer, it calls the answer deficient. The reading is not quality control bolted on; it is the place the human judgment lives. AI helps make the work doable. It is not the doer.

Grading through all of this separates two things. Completion is participation in thinking — the students' standing rule, printed on every closing checklist, is "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." Proficiency is demonstrated understanding, and it is read from the growth artifacts, not from the completion column. The separation is what makes first answers safe. Students need to expose incomplete thinking for this to work at all; grade every developing answer for correctness and they stop risking one. I remind them constantly: "Because" is not an answer. "I don't know" is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy. And copying quietly loses its payoff, because a first answer, a pattern-break defense, and a revision can't be copied into existence — the thought questions don't have answers you can look up.

## Why lessons have breathing room

The pace itself often destroys the possibility of depth. Students asked to touch ideas briefly, perform understanding quickly, and move on before conceptual relationships stabilize are surviving procedurally, not thinking. So the three-block rhythm carries deliberate slack: Day 1 is the most teaching plus a shorter activity; Day 2 is shorter teaching plus the longer activity or lab; Day 3 is finishing, returning to prior answers, feedback, and revision. Real classes lose minutes to attendance, late arrivals, passes, office calls, tech problems, drills, and the individual help that is the actual job. These lessons are designed for that classroom, not an ideal one — and doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly. The pacing keeps.

## Why uneven pacing is designed for

Students do not finish at the same time, and the design assumes it rather than fighting it.

Within a lesson: the three tiers and "Keep going" questions let the fast thinker go deeper on the same question the whole room is answering — no separate track, no visibly different task telegraphing who's been judged less capable. The question I ask is not "who needs easier work" but what does this student need in order to be respected as a knower right now? For one student the Getting Started tier is the way in — not less, but the version that lets them be a knower in this moment. For the smart, turned-off student, the Mastery tier and the challenge slides say: I see what you are capable of and I am not pretending otherwise. Much of teaching is making the harder thing available without forcing it.

Across the year: cycles carry CORE, EXTEND, and EXPLORE/CHALLENGE options. CORE alone is a complete path for 14 of 20 cycles as shipped — the exceptions: Cycle 12's CORE deck is SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED (its EXTEND deck carries the cycle meanwhile), Cycles 08 and 16 CORE need an activity supplied, Cycles 07/13/15 CORE activities have SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED items, and NGSS HS-LS2-6 currently rests on the Cycle 07 EXTEND deck. Several cycles (Population Ecology, Cells, Meiosis, Genetics, Protein Synthesis, Mutations) add EXTEND decks when a class moves faster. Use one or several depending on your class's pace. Faster classes get relevant extension, so slower classes get time — without either one dragging the other.

## Why Fiddles and challenges exist

A Fiddle is a sim already open when students walk in — the Energy Tank Model sliders, the BioMan remove-a-species food-web game, OneZoom's tree of life ("find a pair that look almost the same but are far apart on the tree"). It covers attendance, transitions, waiting, and early-finisher time with something worth poking at — a real alternative to phones and off-task screens, and usually pointed at the very aspect the day will teach, so the fiddling is a head start, not a pacifier.

Challenges are the standing invitation upward: "Break the web" (remove one species, trace the arrows, predict a year out — is there any single species whose removal would barely change the web?), the "Keep going" stretches, the What-if slides ("What if decomposers suddenly disappeared from every ecosystem on Earth?"). They exist so there is always something in the room that has met your strongest student — available, never forced.
