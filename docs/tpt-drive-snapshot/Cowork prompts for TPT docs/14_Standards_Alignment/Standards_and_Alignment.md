# Standards and Alignment — Answerable Biology

Purpose: the complete NGSS documentation for Answerable Biology — Performance Expectations mapped to every unit and cycle, the DCIs/SEPs/CCCs behind them, where the assessment evidence lives, and a section written for administrators and observers. Use it for curriculum maps, required lesson planning, standards documentation, and observation pre-conferences.

## What this document is, and why it is separate

This document is deliberately separate from the operating documents. The Master Agenda and Unit Agendas carry no NGSS strings, no research citations, and no administrative formatting — they are meant to be readable during class. Standards, theory, and administrative justification live here. That separation is Katherine's design, in her own words:

> "From the outside, this classroom still looks compliant. Objectives are visible. Vocabulary gets covered. The pacing guide would not immediately flag anything wrong. This is intentional."

> "Teachers who want to serve students honestly inside constrained systems often have no choice but to run two curricula at once: the one the institution can see, and the one that actually develops in students' minds. The first is the price of operating. The second is the reason to operate."

In the product itself, NGSS Performance Expectations sit on the never-projected teacher-reference slide (slide 1) of each deck — visible to you, invisible to students. This document collects them all in one place.

How to read the tables:

- **On-slide** = the PE codes are printed verbatim on that deck's teacher-reference slide. These are the primary data.
- **Inferred** = the deck is a legacy-format deck with no NGSS field (13 of the 27 decks in the corpus). The PE listed is the standard NGSS HS-LS mapping for that topic, assigned in this document — every one carries the flag REVIEW WITH KATHERINE.
- The DCI/SEP/CCC columns are derived from the PEs using the standard NGSS foundation-box structure (the published architecture of the standards, not deck content). Where the PE itself is inferred, the derivation inherits the flag.

## 4. Assessment alignment — where the evidence lives

### The evidence system in one paragraph

Every cycle produces one primary artifact: the finished student deck, with the first answer and the revised answer side by side on each response slide. Grading distinguishes completion (both answers present — participation in thinking) from proficiency (demonstrated understanding, read from the Conceptual Growth Report and the teacher's class summary). The student-facing rule, verbatim from the decks: "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade." AI produces the feedback drafts and report drafts; the teacher reads everything before it reaches a student — human-gated by design. Details live in "AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth" and the assessment documentation; this section maps the artifacts to the standards.

### How the artifacts evidence the Science and Engineering Practices

**Constructing Explanations.** This is the practice the whole arc is built to produce, and the artifact captures it twice per critical aspect. Students commit to a first explanation in their own words ("The energy in your dinner started as sunlight... At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy — or something else? Why?" — Cycle 02), compress it into a rule ("Finish this sentence as a rule: 'Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ______________________.'"), test the rule against a pattern-break (decomposers eat dead things at every level — "Does your rule still hold?"), and then write a revised explanation directly under the first. The pair is a before-and-after record of explanation construction — not a claim that explanation happened, but the two versions themselves.

**Developing and Using Models.** Students build and manipulate models in nearly every cycle: the tank-and-flow models (drag the transfer-efficiency slider and watch the energy tanks), the two-day food-web build with species cards, the energy-pyramid math inside it, Build-a-Bilayer, the build-a-sugar-molecule kits (6 CO2 + 6 H2O with every atom accounted for), pipe-cleaner chromosomes, the Replication Assembly Line. The model-based products (food webs, pyramid math, bilayer drawings, molecule builds) are collectable evidence; the response slides show whether the model changed the student's explanation.

**Analyzing and Interpreting Data.** Cycles with data-generating activities produce it directly: Do the Energy Math (10,000 units through four levels), leaf-disk float counts against a no-CO2 control, catalase rate graphed against temperature and pH, the moth-hunt catch data, the bean-population lab across three predation rounds, the Gene-Pool Beads allele counts across four generations, Chargaff percentages. The three-tier questions then ask students to reason from that data — Cycle 02's Getting Started tier is itself a data question: "A meadow stores 10,000 calories in its grass. How many reach the hawks (secondary consumers)? Show your math."

**Using Mathematics and Computational Thinking.** The 10%-rule computations (Cycles 02–03), the four-level energy math, the 2²³ gamete-combinations challenge, Punnett-square probability, allele-frequency counts (Cycle 18).

**Engaging in Argument from Evidence.** The Day-3 challenges are argument tasks by design: "Write a short claim with reasoning - not just an answer, but WHY" (Cycle 03); Break the Web's prediction-and-defense (Cycle 02); the Cost vs. Benefit trade-off defense and the one-page evidence-based proposal (Cycle 20).

**The three-tier questions as differentiated evidence.** Every concept question offers Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery with a word bank ("use any, modify any, or use none"). The tier a student answers at — and the movement between tiers from first to revised answer, and across the "Then and Now" self-rating — is itself evidence of where they sit on the practice, on one scale, without separate tracks.

## 5. For administrators and observers

This section is written to be handed to an evaluator, or used in a pre-observation conference.

**The model in one paragraph.** Answerable Biology runs each core idea through a commit–develop–revise arc grounded in Variation Theory. Students commit to a first explanation in writing before instruction resolves the question; the lesson develops the idea through deliberately designed contrast cases and a pattern-break (a case that breaks the too-simple rule students have just built); students then revise their original answer with the first version left visible. Formative work is graded on completion so students can safely expose developing thinking; proficiency is judged separately by the teacher from a written growth record for every student, every cycle.

**What an observer will see in the room:**

- **Students writing developing explanations in their own words.** Question slides stop the lesson and require a written formulation before any answer is given. The teacher deliberately does not resolve the question from the front — students check the slides, discuss with a partner, or come ask. Every thinking slide carries the same instruction: "Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write." Silence during these stretches is thinking, not disengagement.
- **Contrast cases doing the teaching.** Concepts are taught by holding two cases side by side that differ in exactly one respect (CASE A / CASE B), then a case that breaks the rule students just built. Example from the free unit: a bee-and-flower beside a mosquito-and-human, then an orchid that tricks bees into pollinating it for nothing — "does your rule still work if you apply it carefully?"
- **A visible revision loop.** On Day 3 students return to their first answers and revise them on the same slide, both versions left showing. The deck is the record: an observer can pick up any student's deck and see the thinking change.
- **Tiered questions on one scale.** Every concept question offers three entry points (Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery) with an optional word bank. All students face the same concept; differentiation is by entry point, not by separate tracks or visibly different tasks.
- **The teacher among the students.** Direct instruction is short and front-loaded; the teacher then circulates through the activity or lab — questioning, noticing, crouching beside stuck students. Two things per class, done well: one conceptual lesson, one activity aimed at the same critical aspect.
- **Purposeful entry and transition work.** A sim or exploration task ("fiddle") is open when students arrive and fills transitions and early-finisher time, so arrival minutes and waiting time are on-task by design.

**How this maps to common evaluation language.** Objectives: on the board and on the teacher-reference slide of every v2 deck (legacy decks pending upgrade — see section 1), with NGSS PEs on the same slide and in this document. Engagement: 100% written response is structural — every student commits an answer on every question slide; participation is not sampled by hand-raising. Checks for understanding: the first answers are read every cycle, per student, and drive the Day-3 feedback. Differentiation: three-tier questions, word banks, CORE/EXTEND deck choice by class pace, and IEP-accessible phrasing — the conceptual bar stays fixed while linguistic obstacles are removed. Higher-order thinking: rule-building, pattern-breaks, and what-if questions have no answer to copy; they require applying a distinction to a new case. Feedback: every student receives written, targeted, non-evaluative feedback each cycle (AI-drafted, teacher-read and adjusted before release), followed by a required revision. Data: the class summary gives the teacher a per-cycle read of who has each idea and who does not, and the Growth Reports document individual growth across the year.

**One caution for observers.** Completion grades on formative work are deliberate, not lax. Grading developing answers for correctness teaches students to hide incomplete thinking; this system needs the incomplete thinking on paper, because that is what the feedback and revision work on. The rigor lives in the proficiency read — the Growth Report and the revised answers — not in red ink on first drafts. In Katherine's words to students: "I care about your thinking, not the answer. 'Because' is not an answer. 'I don't know' is not an answer. But anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy."
