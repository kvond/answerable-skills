# Schedule Options

Purpose: three worked examples of how the 20 cycles land on real school calendars — an alternating block schedule, daily 90-minute blocks, and 45-minute periods — so you can see the rhythm on your own bell schedule. These are implementation examples, not a pacing guide.

Read this after the Year Arc. What happens inside each block lives in the Master Agenda and each Unit Agenda; if you are new to the system, read Start Here first.

## Before you pick a schedule

Three things are true on every calendar:

- You may move faster or slower than any example here. The cycles are flexible by design. In Katherine's words: "Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly. And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now."
- CORE alone is a complete path for 14 of 20 cycles as shipped. The exceptions: Cycle 12's CORE deck is SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED (the EXTEND deck carries the cycle meanwhile); Cycles 08 and 16 CORE need an activity supplied; Cycles 07, 13, and 15 CORE activities have SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED items; and NGSS HS-LS2-6 currently rests on the Cycle 07 EXTEND deck. The EXTEND decks (Cycles 07, 08, 12, 13, 15, 16) and the EXPLORE layer inside every deck — "Keep going" stretches, What-ifs, Day-3 optional challenges, Mastery tiers, fiddles — are where pace differences go. A fast class absorbs them; a slower class skips them and loses nothing structural. "Use the core deck alone for a resistant-learner pace, or add the second to extend."
- The unit of planning is the 3-block cycle, not the week. Every cycle is Day 1 (the most teaching, plus a shorter activity), Day 2 (shorter lesson, longer activity or lab), Day 3 (finish the activity, return to first answers, feedback, revision). The day-divider slides in each deck mark these seams. Wherever your bell schedule forces a cut, cut at a seam.

One cutting rule worth keeping on any schedule: a pattern-break is written to test the rule the student just built ("Does your rule still hold?"). Keep rule-building and its pattern-break in the same sitting. The clean stopping points are after a response slide (first answer written — a complete state, and her standing policy is that notes are due before students leave) or at a day divider.

## A. Alternating (A/B) block schedule — the native rhythm

This is the schedule the decks were built on: roughly 90-minute blocks, class meeting every other school day — about five meetings across every two weeks.

Three blocks make a cycle, so five meetings carry one full cycle plus two-thirds of the next: about 1⅔ cycles per two-week span at full tilt. Cycles drift across the two-week boundary on purpose; you are pacing by cycle, not by week.

| Meeting (2-week span) | Content at full tilt |
|---|---|
| 1 | Cycle N — Day 1 |
| 2 | Cycle N — Day 2 |
| 3 | Cycle N — Day 3 |
| 4 | Cycle N+1 — Day 1 |
| 5 | Cycle N+1 — Day 2 |

The next span opens with Cycle N+1 Day 3.

Across a semester: an 18-week semester gives about 45 meetings, which is 15 cycles of capacity. Across the year, roughly 85–90 blocks against the 60 the cycles need. Nobody should run at full tilt — the gap is the design. Katherine's own Cycle 02 teacher slide reads "3 blocks · Sep 07–18": three blocks given a full two-week span, early in the year, with the other meetings absorbed by start-of-year routines, an activity that ran long, and the ordinary frictions of a real classroom. That is the system working, not the system behind.

A realistic year on A/B blocks looks something like:

- Semester 1: Cycles 01–10 (the ecology arc, then cells through enzymes). Ten cycles is 30 blocks of about 45 — roughly a third of your meetings free for the first-week setup, labs that spill over, reteaching, testing windows, and one or two EXTEND decks where the class is moving.
- Semester 2: Cycles 11–20. Ten cycles is 30 blocks — the same room again, which the cell cycle, the genetics stretch (Cycles 12–16, where five of the six deck-choice cycles sit), and the capstone will happily use.

The off day between meetings is a working asset: students get a night between committing a first answer and meeting the second critical aspect, and you get the gap to skim first answers — her habit, in her words: "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it."

## B. Daily 90-minute blocks

Same block length, meeting every day. Two common shapes:

4x4 semester block (the whole course in one semester of ~90 daily meetings): the arithmetic is the A/B year compressed — a cycle every three days, about a cycle and a half per week, 20 cycles in about 13–14 weeks of a roughly 18-week semester. The remaining weeks are your slack. At this speed the between-day thinking time disappears, so protect Day 3: do not fold revision into Day 2 to save a block. The revision against the first answer is the payoff of the whole arc — "Seeing your thinking change is the point."

Year-long daily blocks: enormous room — 20 cycles use about 60 of ~175 meetings. Run the EXTEND decks liberally, give labs their own full blocks, and let the EXPLORE layer breathe.

Ways to divide a 3-block cycle across daily blocks, using the deck's day dividers as the stopping points:

- Three consecutive days (Mon–Wed): the tightest run. Best when the Day 1–2 activity is short or digital (Cycle 09's Screen-Door Sort and Build-a-Bilayer; Cycle 14's Unzip and Rebuild).
- Three days across a week (Mon / Wed / Fri): closest to the native rhythm. The buffer days take the overflow — the food-web build that needs a third sitting, the students who were absent, the reteach the class summary told you it needed. Cycle 06 benefits here: the fish-and-BTB demo and the yeast balloons both reward time between looks.
- Days 1–2 back to back, Day 3 after a gap: gives the lab momentum (Cycle 05's Leaf-Disk Float debriefs on Day 2 while the result is fresh) and still gives students distance from their first answers before revising.

If a block gets cut short mid-day — assembly, drill, early dismissal — stop at a response slide. First answers in, both boxes rule intact, pick up at the next slide tomorrow.

## C. 45-minute periods, five days a week

One 90-minute block is about two periods, so a 3-block cycle spans roughly 5–6 periods — a bit over one week per cycle, which lands 20 cycles in about 24–27 weeks of a 36-week year. Where to split each day's content:

| Period | Content | Split point |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Day 1, first half: bellringer, short sim, first critical aspect — first answer, contrast (CASE A / CASE B), rule-building, pattern-break | Stop after the pattern-break lands |
| 2 | Day 1, second half: three-tier concept question, first answer on the response slide, the shorter Day-1 activity | Day 1 divider seam |
| 3 | Day 2 lesson: second critical aspect, full arc through its response slide | Stop with first answer written |
| 4 | Day 2 activity or lab, the whole period | Day 2 divider seam |
| 5 | Day 3: finish the activity, run the revision prompt, write revised answers, submit the deck | Cycle complete |
| 6 | Overflow and EXPLORE: the optional challenge, the What-if, Then and Now — or the next cycle's Day 1 | Flexible |

Notes for short periods:

- Give the lab a clean period of its own (period 4) and keep setup light — the labs are deliberately not procedurally complicated, and most key activities fit: leaf disks start floating within minutes, the yeast balloons inflate in 15–20 minutes, the bobtail squid investigation is about 20 minutes, the Peppered Moth Hunt runs in one-minute rounds.
- The all-block ambient demos convert well: Cycle 06's fish-in-BTB becomes a look-at-start, look-at-end observation across periods 3 and 4 — the color change needs the time anyway.
- Fiddles earn more of their keep here. Arrival and transition time is a larger fraction of a 45-minute period; a sim already open when students walk in (OneZoom, PhET Natural Selection, Tour of the Basics) protects the minutes the lesson needs.
- Her notes-due rule applies per period, not per cycle-day: whatever thinking a period asked for is written before students leave. "Students who are present complete and submit their notes before leaving class."
- Resist letting a cycle sprawl past six periods by default. If a class needs more time, that is what the slack (below) is for — spend it deliberately, on the cycles that need it.

## The slack is structural

On any of these calendars, 20 cycles do not fill a ~36-week year — and that is deliberate. Sixty blocks of cycle time against 85–90 blocks of school leaves roughly a third of your meetings unassigned. That slack absorbs, in rough order of predictability: the first week of routines, holidays and testing windows, drills and assemblies, labs and builds that deserve a second sitting, reteaching that the class summaries surface, EXTEND decks where a class is moving fast, and the days a class simply needs to sit with an idea longer. Katherine's diagnosis is the reason the count is 20 and not 28: "the pace itself often destroys the possibility of depth. Students are asked to touch ideas briefly, perform understanding quickly, and move on before conceptual relationships stabilize." The schedule options above are ways of spending the room this structure buys — not quotas to hit.
