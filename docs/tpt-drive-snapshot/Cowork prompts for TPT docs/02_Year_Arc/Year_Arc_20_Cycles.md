# Year Arc — Answerable Biology in 20 Cycles

Purpose: the full year, cycle by cycle — what each cycle asks, what students do, what they revise, and what you need — so you can see the whole arc on a few pages and plan from it.

This is the planning view. Day-to-day operation lives in the Master Agenda and each Unit Agenda; calendar placement lives in Schedule Options; if you are new to the system, read Start Here first. The seven ecology cycles (01–07) are documented in full teaching depth in the Ecology Unit Guide.

## What a cycle is

A cycle is one core idea run through the full Variation Theory arc across three blocks:

- Day 1 — the most teaching. Bellringer and a short sim, then the first critical aspect: students commit to a first answer, work a deliberate contrast (CASE A / CASE B), build a rule in their own words, and meet a pattern-break — a case that breaks the too-simple rule. A shorter activity closes the block.
- Day 2 — shorter lesson, longer activity or lab. The second critical aspect runs the same arc; the lab or main activity gets the bulk of the time.
- Day 3 — see your thinking change. Students finish the activity, return to their first answers, run the AI revision prompt (in the response slide's speaker notes — it quotes one thing right back, asks two questions, names one distinction, and never gives the answer), and write a revised answer directly under the first. Both stay visible: the change is the point. The finished deck — first answer beside revised answer — is the single artifact that feeds each student's Conceptual Growth Report and your class summary. Completion = both answers present; growth shows in the report, not the grade.

Every cycle carries two or three critical aspects, never more. In Katherine's words: "A lesson, in my experience, can carry two or three critical aspects that actually shape understanding. Not eight facts. Two or three discernments, each of which, if a student fails to make it, leaves everything downstream incoherent."

## Why 20 flexible cycles, not a day-by-day pacing guide

Katherine's diagnosis, from the book: "This may be one of the hidden problems of modern schooling: the pace itself often destroys the possibility of depth. Students are asked to touch ideas briefly, perform understanding quickly, and move on before conceptual relationships stabilize." Her answer was not to fall behind — it was to stop over-programming: "Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly. And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now."

A cycle is three blocks of teaching, but no real school year delivers three clean blocks in a row. Holidays, testing windows, weather days, field trips, assemblies, fire drills, and the days a class simply needs to sit with an idea longer all land somewhere. The 20-cycle structure absorbs them: a typical block schedule gives roughly 85–90 blocks a year, and 20 cycles at 3 blocks each uses about 60 — the rest is your breathing room, spent on reteaching, labs that run long, review, and the interruptions you cannot schedule. (The friction inventory is the build's operationalization of her breathing-room principle, not her wording.)

Pace differences are handled inside the structure, not by rushing: six cycles offer a CORE deck plus one or more EXTEND decks, "choose by pace" — "Use the core deck alone for a resistant-learner pace, or add the second to extend." Within every deck, "Keep going" stretches, Mastery tiers, and Day-3 optional challenges serve fast thinkers without new logistics, and Fiddles (a sim open on arrival) cover attendance, transitions, and early finishers.

One design feature to protect when you compress or reorder: the cycles are deliberately threaded. Most decks end with a "Keep going" question whose answer is the next cycle — Cycle 03's leftover atoms become Cycle 04's carbon, Cycle 05's stored sugar becomes Cycle 06's respiration, Cycle 11's half-and-different division becomes Cycle 12's meiosis, Cycle 18's two separated forests become Cycle 19's speciation. The chain is noted per cycle below.

## Cycle 06 — Cellular Respiration & Fermentation

Unit: 5. Cellular Respiration & Fermentation. Deck: Cycle 06 (CORE).

Central question: How do your cells cash in the energy in food — and what happens when the oxygen runs out? Essential claim (verbatim): "Cells release the energy stored in glucose. When oxygen runs out, fermentation is the backup pathway."

Critical aspects (verbatim): "Critical aspect: Releasing the energy in glucose" · "Critical aspect: Oxygen and how much ATP you get"

Core lesson: Arc 1 contrasts a marshmallow burning in one flash with a cell releasing the same energy in tiny steps trapped as ATP, broken by fever — respiration "leaking" straight to heat, "like a tiny flame." Arc 2 contrasts ~34 ATP with oxygen vs. ~2 without, broken by Raquel's mile-20 sprint: fermentation adds no ATP, so what is it FOR? A block-long teacher demo runs throughout: a fish in bromothymol blue turns the water blue → green → yellow, control cup stays blue — "Hold 'respiration' and 'CO2' back — let them infer."

Activity / lab: "Releasing the Energy in Food" trace worksheet (Day 1); Yeast Balloon Fermentation Lab with a no-sugar control — the balloon seals out oxygen, and CO2 inflates it anyway, "proof the yeast made energy with no air" (Day 2).

## Cycle 13 — Mendelian Genetics & Punnett Squares
