## 1. The message map — twelve complaints, twelve concrete answers

Each entry: the complaint in the buyer's words, then the answer drawn from the system's actual machinery, with the Cycle 02 (Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships — the free unit) example that proves it. These paragraphs are the copy bank: listings, the storefront, preview pages, and any future promo video pull sentences from here.

### 1.1 "I bought curriculum and still had to figure out how to teach it."

Most purchased curriculum is a pile of materials; the teaching — the sequence of moves, what to say when, what each piece is for — is left to you. Answerable Biology ships the teaching. Every deck is organized around two or three critical aspects — the discernments the topic actually turns on, chosen at the exact places students reliably go wrong — and the deck itself carries the instructional sequence: first answer, a CASE A / CASE B contrast, rule-building in the student's own words, a pattern-break that tests the rule, a three-tier concept question, revision. The day-divider slides are the block plans ("Day 1 of 3 — the most teaching"). Teacher advice is written where you need it, on the slides themselves: Cycle 02's Day-1 activity slide says, in the deck, "(We keep it simple this first cycle — the energy-pyramid math comes next class.)"

Around the decks sits an operating documentation system: Start Here (the ten-minute version of how the curriculum thinks), the Master Agenda (the year's one operating page — keep it open during class), a Unit Agenda per unit (one readable page: central question, critical aspects verbatim, the three days, supplies — produced from the included template; filled agendas ship in the bundles), and the Ecology Unit Guide (the free unit taught slide by slide). You are never handed a folder and left to reconstruct the pedagogy.

Copy line: *This is not a folder of materials with the teaching left as an exercise. The deck carries the question sequence; the agendas carry the year.*

### 1.2 "There is never enough time to finish the pacing guide."

Answerable Biology is not a pacing guide. It is 20 flexible cycles of three blocks each — about 60 blocks against the roughly 85–90 a typical block-schedule year provides. The gap is the design: the slack absorbs assemblies, drills, testing windows, labs that run long, and the days a class needs to sit with an idea. Katherine's own teacher slide for Cycle 02 reads "3 blocks · Sep 07–18" — three blocks given a full two-week span. That is the system working, not the system behind.

Her words carry the argument better than any feature list: "Doing two things well turns out to take less time than doing six things poorly. And the pacing? I can more or less keep it now." Each cycle does two things per class — a lesson on a critical aspect where students actually think, and an activity aimed at the same aspect — and cuts the rest. Schedule Options shows the 20 cycles worked onto three real bell schedules: alternating A/B blocks (the native rhythm), daily 90-minute blocks, and 45-minute periods.

Copy line: *Twenty cycles use about 60 of your ~85–90 blocks. The rest is breathing room — on purpose.*

### 1.3 "The activities don't connect to the lesson."

In this system an activity exists to make one critical aspect perceivable — the same aspect the lesson is teaching that day — or it doesn't ship. Cycle 02, Day 1: the lesson's aspect is energy flow through trophic levels; the activity is a food-web build students construct themselves from species cards, finished on Day 2 with the energy-pyramid step — 10,000 units of energy through the 10% rule, in their own arithmetic: 10,000 → 1,000 → 100 → 10. The contrast on the slide (a meadow holding 10,000 calories of grass, and the 10–15 hawks it can feed) becomes math in the students' own hands two days later. Nothing in the cycle is a time-filler with the topic's name on it.

The cycles connect to each other the same way: most decks end with a "Keep going" question whose answer is the next cycle. Cycle 02 asks "If energy is lost at every step and never comes back, why hasn't life run out of energy?" — tagged in the deck "There's no answer to look up." Cycles 03–05 are the answer. Students commit to a guess at the end of one cycle and meet the payoff at the start of the next.

Copy line: *Every activity is built for the exact idea the lesson teaches that day — and each cycle's last question is the next cycle's opening problem.*

### 1.4 "The labs are so procedural students miss the science."

Katherine's lab philosophy, in her own words: "I used to treat labs like they were being prepared for FDA approval — serious steps, fine-print directions. Now I am messier. Directions are scant. If someone does it wrong, that is something to think about, not a failure to remediate. The lab is more honest when the student's noticing is what the lab is for, rather than adherence to a procedure." Labs run only when the embodied experience matters for the aspect — "Labs done soberly" — and directions stay short so students attend to the phenomenon, not the checklist. The Cycle 05 leaf-disk lab is the model: punch disks, sink them, watch which cups float them — the floating disk full of just-made oxygen is the observation the whole lesson turns on, and counting floats each minute is the whole procedure. Complexity lives in the design, not in directions students must decipher.

Copy line: *Directions stay scant on purpose. When directions get complicated, students attend to the procedure instead of the thing the lab exists to show them.*

### 1.5 "My students work at completely different speeds."

The design assumes it rather than fighting it, at three scales:

- Within a question: every concept question comes in three tiers — Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery — with a word bank students may "use any, modify any, or use none" of. Every student answers the same question at the depth they can currently reach; no separate track telegraphs who has been judged less capable.
- Within a lesson: "Keep going" stretches, What-if slides, and Day-3 optional challenges (Cycle 02's "Break the Web": remove one species, trace every arrow, predict a year out) keep something in the room that has met your strongest student — available, never forced.
- Across the year: cycles carry CORE, EXTEND, and EXPLORE/CHALLENGE options. CORE alone is a complete path for 14 of 20 cycles as shipped — Cycle 12's CORE deck is SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED (the EXTEND deck carries the cycle meanwhile), Cycles 08 and 16 CORE need an activity supplied, the Cycle 07/13/15 CORE activities have SOURCE NOT YET LOCATED items, and NGSS HS-LS2-6 currently rests on the Cycle 07 EXTEND deck. In her product language: "Use the core deck alone for a resistant-learner pace, or add the second to extend." Faster classes absorb the extensions, so slower classes get time — without either dragging the other.

Copy line: *Fast students get real extension on the same question; slow students get time. Nobody gets a visibly easier worksheet.*

### 1.6 "My students default to their phones or unrelated Chromebook activity."

The system's answer is the Fiddle: a sim already open when students walk in, covering attendance, transitions, waiting, and early-finisher time with something genuinely worth poking at — and usually pointed at the very aspect the day will teach, so the fiddling is a head start, not a pacifier. Cycle 02's fiddles: the BioMan food-web game ("remove one species from a food web. What happens?") and the Energy Tank Model sliders ("what's the highest transfer efficiency that still starves the top tank?"). Cycle 01's is OneZoom's tree of life: "Find a pair that look almost the same but are far apart on the tree." Every cycle names its fiddle; the Master Agenda repeats the fiddle URL per cycle so it is the tab you open before the bell.

Copy line: *Every idle minute in the room has something real in it — a sim aimed at today's idea, open before the bell rings.*

### 1.7 "They just copy."

The system removes the payoff instead of policing the act. First answers, rule-building, pattern-break defenses, and What-ifs have no answers to look up — "the thought questions do not have answers you can copy; they require students to apply a distinction they have just made to a new case." Notes are due before the bell, with the rationale written to students on a slide: "When you wait until later: you remember less, you're more likely to copy, you miss the learning that the notes are designed to create." Completion grading makes honesty cheap — "anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy," so there is nothing to gain by copying a first answer that is graded only for being present.

And the pipeline backstops it: the teacher batch checks the first answer — and only the first answer — before drafting anything. A copying or AI flag holds that completion entry pending your review; nothing posts automatically. An AI-written first answer is auto-flagged for failure, confirmed by you. The revision is exempt by design, because students are told to use their AI there. Flags stay visible to you and you decide.

Copy line: *Copying loses its payoff when the work is formulate, defend, and revise — and the batch still checks first answers, with you as the judge.*

### 1.8 "I cannot give this much formative feedback."

The arithmetic is the real problem, and Katherine names it: "a single teacher cannot personally hear from every student in the room every day... Across a week, the arithmetic catches up." The system's answer is one loop, run every cycle. On Day 3, each student opens their response slide's speaker Notes, copies the standard revision prompt into their AI with their own first answer, gets back one thing they got right (quoted in their own words), two questions, one distinction — never the answer — and writes a revised answer under the first. Both stay visible: "Seeing your thinking change is the point."

Then the finished decks — the single artifact, no exit tickets, no separate packet — run through one batch step that drafts a one-page printable Conceptual Growth Report per student (with progression bars per critical aspect, evidence quoted from the student's own revised answer, and a next step written as an invitation) and a whole-class view for you: student by student, concept by concept, where the class is stuck, and what needs reteaching stated as the next contrast to teach. In the Unit Descriptions' own through-line: "who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed. No stack of papers to grade."

The gate is non-negotiable and sold as such: "I read every piece of it before it goes to a student, and I adjust it as needed. That is not a small caveat. It is the whole architecture."

Copy line: *Every student gets targeted feedback every cycle, and you read every word before it lands. The AI drafts; you remain the doer.*

### 1.9 "I spend hours locating and ordering lab supplies."

The Master Supply System is one workbook: a MASTER sheet with one row per supply per cycle — quantity per group, prep, storage, safety, approximate cost, a cheap substitute on the expensive lines, and a store-link column (links are still being added) — plus formula-driven views that never drift: per-quarter gather lists, a full-year shopping view for one summer pass, and a COSTS sheet splitting one-time reusables from per-September consumables. Change your class count once and every total updates. The Master Agenda and each Unit Agenda carry a one-line supply summary per cycle; the checklists live in the workbook and only there. The supply load itself is deliberately light: the first wet lab arrives in Cycle 05, the expensive lines are few, and every one carries a substitute (window light for lamps, paper circles for model kits).

Copy line: *One editable sheet produces every quarter checklist, the shopping view, and the cost totals — quantity, prep, cost, and a cheap substitute on the expensive lines, with store links being added.*

### 1.10 "I don't want to rebuild an entire curriculum inside my LMS."

You don't. The setup guide's explicit instruction is the opposite of the rebuild: teach from the linked canonical files, and copy only what students must type into — their own editable copy of the current cycle's deck, which is the one non-negotiable duplication in the system. Sims, activity guides, videos, and agendas stay linked; the deck you project already carries its own links; the Master Agenda is the index either way. The Set Up guide runs the whole onboarding as a numbered sequence, each step tagged MUST DO BEFORE TEACHING or CAN BE BUILT AS YOU GO — and it works identically if all you have is the free unit.

For teachers who use Claude, the documentation includes Katherine's own working process for wiring each cycle's assignments into the LMS and gradebook — Claude drafting assignment text, running the teacher batch, filling the completion column — with her verification rules attached ("Every word that reaches a student or the gradebook passes through her first"). Copy line: *Setup is a checklist, not a construction project. One deck copy per student is the only thing you duplicate.*

### 1.11 "The curriculum assumes technology or printing I don't have."

Every fiddle has a written no-tech alternative, and the free unit demonstrates a no-tech path for every digital element — including the AI step. From the Ecology Unit Guide, for Cycle 02 alone: the food-web sim becomes six species cards on the board with one physically removed; the Energy Tank Model becomes water poured up a line of successively tiny cups, spilling ~90% into a "heat" basin at each step — or just the Day-2 pyramid arithmetic, which is the same model on paper; and the Day-3 AI step becomes a partner running the same three moves by hand (one thing right, quoted; two questions; one distinction), with the revision still the student's own. Print load is low — decks are projected and submitted digitally where possible, handouts run on the school copier, and Schedule Options covers 45-minute periods for schools without blocks.

Copy line: *Every fiddle has a written no-tech fallback, and the free unit demonstrates a no-tech path for every digital element — the AI step included. The pedagogy survives the wifi.*

### 1.12 "The AI feature is just a generic prompt."

Two answers, both specific. First, the student prompt is not "get feedback from AI" — it is a fixed, tested shape sitting verbatim in every response slide's speaker Notes: the AI must tell the student one thing they got right and quote their own words back, ask two questions that make them look again, name one distinction, "keep it short and in plain language, then stop so I can write my own revised answer." It is forbidden to give the answer or rewrite the student's work. Every constraint is doing pedagogical work: quoting the student's words back reads them as a knower; "then stop" keeps the AI from becoming the doer.

Second, the teacher side runs on a specified feedback register — Katherine's pedagogy "turned into instructions": "Do not grade. Do not produce a corrected version. Read the student as a knower... Never use the language of deficit." — plus report standards (deeply positive and true, readable by a striving 9th grader, no research jargon reaches a student, one printable page) and a human gate at the end. And it is honest about tools: "I am not describing a product, and I am not recommending a particular tool... The principle is portable." The prompts work in any capable general-purpose model; nothing is subscription-locked to a vendor.

Copy line: *The prompt has a fixed shape with a reason for every line — and a teacher reading every word before anything reaches a student.*

## 6. Claims discipline — what the sales copy may and may not say

The copy above holds to these lines; keep them when editing:

- **Workload numbers stay hedged as she hedges them.** Her estimate — production work "roughly forty percent" of former hours, presence up "somewhere between twenty and sixty percent" — carries her own caveat: "an estimate from my own practice — a feel, not a measurement." If a listing uses the numbers, it uses the hedge. Safer: "No stack of papers to grade," which is hers and concrete.
- **Never promise the AI grades or teaches.** The sellable claim is the opposite: human-gated, teacher reads everything, "AI helps make the work doable. It is not the doer."
- **No efficacy claims** ("raises test scores," "proven") — nothing in the corpus supports them.
- **NGSS claims point at the Standards and Alignment one-sheet**, not at listing prose: "NGSS alignment for administrators lives in the separate one-sheet."
- **Do not sell the missing pieces.** The Cycle 12 CORE deck (missing), the Cycle 13 cheek-cell lab (name only), and the succession deck's absent activity are all flagged in the source audit; listings must match what ships on the day they post.
- **Interactive models are "hosted interactive model (linked)" — never "included."** The tank models ship as hosted links, not files, and any listing whose included-materials list names an interactive model does not post until the tank models are redeployed (all nine github.io models are currently 404; only Cycle 02's Netlify energy model is live) and the wording is changed to "hosted interactive model (linked)." Listings 13 and 16 point at Learn.Genetics and PhET — LINK ONLY sources that can never say "included" at all.
