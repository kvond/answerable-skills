Purpose: Show the teacher what to do before, during, and after one Answerable Biology Variation Theory cycle.

# How to Teach a VT Cycle

## Typical cycle length

Plan roughly a week to a week and a half for one cycle. The extra half-week is not a recovery buffer for failed pacing; it is part of the design for classes that need time to formulate, test, and revise an idea. A faster class can complete the same cycle in about a week.

When a cycle has a / b / c / d deck set, treat the additional decks as pace-and-depth options rather than an automatic required sequence. Start with the core deck. Add additional decks when this class has time and is ready for more depth.

## Before the cycle

Read Slide 1 first. It is the teacher reference for cycle number, unit, block expectation, essential claim, standards, and objectives.

Identify the conceptual spine: What must students be able to distinguish by the end that they are likely to fuse together at the beginning?

Scan the deck for the first-answer slide, deliberate contrasts, rule-building question, pattern-break, tiered concept questions, the final response slide, and the response slide's Notes.

Choose CORE before choosing anything else. Add EXTEND or EXPLORE/CHALLENGE only when it serves this class rather than merely filling time.

## Day 1 — establish the whole and create a first answer

Start with the whole idea or phenomenon. Students need somewhere for details to land.

Ask for a first answer early enough that they have not simply copied the finished explanation. A first answer can be wrong. Its job is to reveal the student's starting model.

Teach only the key terms needed to see the relationship under study. Vocabulary should support noticing, not replace it.

Use the first contrast to make one critical aspect stand out. In a feeding-relationship cycle, for example, two cases might keep the ecosystem context stable while varying who eats whom or where matter and energy enter the relationship. The point is not that students memorize CASE A and CASE B. The point is that the difference between the cases becomes visible enough to reason with.

Ask students to finish a sentence as a rule. A provisional rule gives the class something to test.

Begin the shorter activity or simulation if the class is ready. If not, stop. Breathing room is part of the design.

## Day 2 — manipulate the idea

Use a shorter teaching segment. Move students into the activity, lab, model, data set, comparison, or simulation that lets them vary something and observe what changes.

Open activities do a different job from matching activities. Matching can establish names, categories, and shared reference points. Open work lets students manipulate a relationship and notice consequences.

When the class has a simple rule, give them the pattern-break. The pattern-break should not feel like a trick. It should be the case that makes the limits of the current rule impossible to ignore.

Ask: What changed? What stayed the same? Which part of our rule survives? What needs revision?

Use Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery questions to keep the destination shared while allowing different entry points.

Keep a Fiddle or relevant challenge available during dead space. Do not make faster students wait for slower students by giving everyone more of the same work.

## Day 3 — return, feedback, revise

Finish the activity first if it still needs finishing. Do not rush the conceptual return simply because the calendar says Day 3.

Return students to their first answers. Ask them to read what they originally wrote before changing it.

Run the response deck through the AI feedback step. The AI should identify one thing that is right in the student's own words, ask two questions, and offer one distinction. It must not write the revised answer.

Give students time to revise beside the original answer.

The first answer and revised answer together are the evidence of conceptual movement. Preserve both.

## Teacher moves when students are stuck

If a student has almost nothing, lower the entry threshold: ask them to identify a difference, choose between two cases, or complete the Getting Started version.

If a student has a partially right rule, do not replace it. Give a contrasting case that tests the part that is too broad.

If a student has the right words but weak reasoning, ask for mechanism: What causes that? What changes next? What would you predict if one part changed?

If a student is already strong, give a transfer or pattern-break question rather than extra repetition.

## What not to do

Do not turn the cycle into a minute-by-minute script.

Do not grade every developing answer for correctness. Students need to expose incomplete thinking for the cycle to work.

Do not mistake completion tasks for proficiency evidence.

Do not add directions simply because a teacher guide can hold them. Complexity belongs in the design, not in instructions the teacher must decipher while teaching.
