# AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth

Purpose: the AI feedback system — how one finished student deck becomes targeted feedback during learning, a printable Conceptual Growth Report per student, and a whole-class view for you — and where the human judgment sits in that pipeline, on purpose.

This is the feedback reference. The grading side (completion, copying, the integrity gate, gradebook setup) lives in Assessment: Completion, Copying, and Proficiency. Day-to-day operation lives in the Master Agenda and each Unit Agenda; the whole system is introduced in Start Here; the cycle structure is in the Year Arc. Examples below come from Cycle 02 (Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships), the free demonstration unit — see the Ecology Unit Guide.

---

## 1. The loop

Every cycle ends the same way, and the whole feedback system runs on it:

STUDENT → AI FEEDBACK → STUDENT REVISION → TEACHER (the human gate)

1. **Student.** In class, the student commits to a first answer on the response slide — before the idea has fully been taught, in their own words. The deck is their thinking made visible.
2. **AI feedback.** On Day 3, the student opens that slide's speaker notes, copies the standard revision prompt into their AI, and pastes in their own first answer. The prompt is built so the AI cannot give them the answer — it can only help them see their own thinking.
3. **Student revision.** The student writes a revised answer directly under the first, in their own words. Both stay visible. As every deck tells them: "Seeing your thinking change is the point."
4. **Teacher — the human gate.** The finished deck (first answer beside revised answer) is the single artifact submitted. You run the batch over the folder of decks; it drafts a Growth Report per student and a class summary for you. You read every word before anything reaches a student, and you adjust it. That reading is not a formality. In Katherine's words: "The reading is not quality control bolted on. It is the place the human judgment lives."

Nothing else is collected. No exit tickets, no separate reflection doc, no email. One artifact, one loop, every cycle.

The loop is deliberately human-gated at both ends: the student's own words go in (the integrity gate protects that — see section 7), and your judgment signs off on what comes out. Do not collapse either checkpoint to save time. The checkpoints are the system.

## 2. The standard student revision prompt

This prompt sits, verbatim, in the speaker notes of every response slide in every deck. Students copy it into their own AI:

> Copy everything between the lines into your AI, and paste your first answer where it says [YOUR FIRST ANSWER]. The AI will NOT give you the answer — it will help you see your own thinking so you can improve it yourself.
>
> -----
> I am a high school student. Below is a science question and my first answer. Do NOT rewrite my answer and do NOT give me the answer. Instead:
> 1. Tell me one thing I got right, and quote my own words back to me.
> 2. Ask me two questions that make me look again at one idea I might have wrong or left out.
> 3. Name one distinction or example worth thinking about.
> Keep it short and in plain language, then stop so I can write my own revised answer.
>
> QUESTION: [the slide's question]
> [YOUR FIRST ANSWER]:
> -----
>
> Now write your revised answer in "Your revised answer" on the slide — in your own words.

The shape is fixed: one thing right, quoted back in the student's own words; two questions; one distinction; never the answer; then stop. Every constraint is doing work. Quoting the student's words back reads them as a knower — their sentence was worth quoting. The two questions send them back to their own idea, not to a correct version. The one distinction gives them something to think with, not something to transcribe. And "then stop" is the line that keeps the AI from becoming the doer: the revised answer has to be the student's.

**What this looks like in Cycle 02.** The response slide asks (first-answer question, slide 7): "At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy — or something else? Why?" A student's first answer: "you get all the energy because you ate the whole cow." A well-behaved run of the prompt returns something like:

> You're right that the energy in your food came up the chain from the grass — "because you ate the whole cow" shows you tracking where it came from. Two questions: What was the cow doing with its energy during all the years it was alive? If you ate the whole cow, did you also eat the heat it gave off? One thing worth thinking about: there is a difference between eating an animal's body and getting all the energy that ever passed through it. Now write your revised answer.

That is the target: short, plain, warm, and it leaves the thinking to the student. ## 3. The feedback register — the instructions behind everything the AI writes

The revision prompt governs what students run for themselves. The register below governs everything the AI drafts on your side — Growth Reports, any written feedback. It is Katherine's pedagogy "turned into instructions," in her words:

> "Do not grade. Do not produce a corrected version. Read the student as a knower. Name where their reasoning was already pointing at something true, even when the final answer was not. Name what their sentence reveals about how they are thinking. Name where their thinking has become more skilled than it was last time, so the growth is visible to them and not only to me. Never use the language of deficit. Speak to them the way I would crouch beside them in class — only on paper, and for every student, including the ones I missed that week."

That last clause is why the system exists at all. The problem AI solves here is arithmetic, not pedagogy: "a single teacher cannot personally hear from every student in the room every day. Lezir got several minutes that Tuesday. Other students got none. Across a week, the arithmetic catches up. The shy student making real progress who never volunteers; the student who was absent and came back without anyone noticing what she wrote; the student who is half-right in a way that needs to be named for them to take the next step — these students sit at the edge of my attention."

None of this register is the machine's default. The defaults, and what to do about them, are in section 6.

## 4. The three feedback forms

### A. Inline feedback — short, targeted, during learning

This is the in-deck revision loop itself (sections 1–2). It is the only feedback form students drive themselves, and it happens while the idea is still live — between the first answer and the revised answer, inside the cycle, not after it.

What makes it work as feedback:

- It is targeted at exactly one thing: the answer the student just wrote, on the critical aspect the lesson turns on. Not their writing, not their effort, not the unit.
- It is short by instruction ("Keep it short and in plain language, then stop").
- It arrives at the moment of maximum use — right before revision, when there is still something to do with it.
- It cannot substitute for the student's thinking, because it is forbidden to contain the answer.

Your part during inline feedback is presence, not processing. Day 3 is a circulation day: students run the prompt and revise while you move among them — the student whose AI response confused them, the student whose first answer was blank, the student whose revision is a copy of the AI's question. Katherine's habit for work that needs a human: "When I grade their work and find a problem, I hunt them down to talk about it."

### B. The individual Conceptual Growth Report

One per student, per cycle, generated from the finished deck by your batch run, read and adjusted by you, then delivered — printed and handed back, and digital where your workflow supports it (the print copy is the designed artifact: something a ninth grader holds, keeps, and shows). It is where all proficiency lives; the gradebook holds completion only.

The report reads the first answer and the revised answer as a pair and tells the story of the movement between them. Its sections, in the student's own frame (first-person, plain headers, from Katherine's v2 pipeline spec):

- **Where my thinking started** — the student's initial thinking, stated plainly and without judgment. The first answer was doing its job.
- **How my thinking changed** — the important revision: the distinction or correction that happened between the two answers, named so the student can see it.
- **Evidence from my final answer** — a verbatim quote of the student's own words carrying the change. For the Cycle 02 student above: "the cow used most of its energy being alive — it went off as heat." Their sentence, quoted back as the proof.
- **Current understanding** — progression bars, one per critical aspect of the cycle (Cycle 02: energy flow through trophic levels; symbiosis and species relationships), with plain labels — Proficient / Almost there / Developing (final label wording is Katherine's call — REVIEW WITH KATHERINE). Always a position on a path, never a deficit.
- **My next step** — the remaining conceptual target, framed as one forward, doable move and written as an invitation, not a remediation.
- **What I understand now that I didn't before** — left blank, for the student to complete. The report ends in the student's handwriting, not the machine's.
- **An idea worth chasing** (when present) — names a generative idea in the student's work, even at low proficiency, and invites them to bring it to class. Wrong-but-original thinking gets named as an asset here, because it is one.

Evidence from later work: as the year accumulates, the cross-unit rollup produces a student year-arc — "how your thinking has grown across the year" — drawing on later cycles' reports as evidence that an earlier revision held. A student who broke the energy-passes-upward rule in Cycle 02 meets the same reasoning again in Cycle 03's 10%-math and Cycle 04's carbon cycling; the rollup can point at that.

The standards every report meets, no exceptions (Katherine's v2 spec):

- **Deeply positive AND true.** Every affirming line is anchored to something the student actually did — a verbatim quote or a named move. No generic or unearned praise: teenagers read that as fake, and it buries the next step.
- **Readable by a striving 9th grader.** Plain language, without dumbing down the science.
- **No coding vocabulary reaches the student.** No NGSS codes, no rubric or research jargon, no "epistemic," "warrant," "agency," no proficiency-scale terminology. That vocabulary lives only on your side (Katherine's pipeline keeps a separate research rail for it; students never see it).
- **Print-friendly.** One page, formatted to hand back.

And the register of section 3 governs every sentence. The AI drafts; you read every word; the report that reaches the student is yours.

### C. The whole-class teacher view

The same batch run that drafts the reports produces the class summary — the room-scale read, for you only. It answers four questions per cycle:

- **Student by student:** each student's position per critical aspect, with integrity flags visible. This is your intervention list — who needs you to hunt them down this week, before the next cycle builds on this one.
- **Concept by concept:** the proficiency spread per critical aspect. In the Unit Descriptions' own through-line: "who can trace energy through a web, and who is still grouping organisms by what they are rather than by how they feed."
- **Where the class is stuck:** the recurring misconceptions, verbatim where possible — if a third of the room still writes "the hawk gets the grass's energy" after revision, that is not thirty individual problems, it is one class problem.
- **What needs whole-class reteaching:** stated as the next instructional contrast to teach, not a topic to repeat. The system's answer to a stuck class is never "go over it again" — it is a sharper CASE A / CASE B that isolates exactly what the room has not discerned.

Across cycles, the course-level rollup shows recurring misconceptions and growth trends over the 20 cycles and your four marking periods — the read that tells you in November what to build differently in February. This is what the report system buys you beyond the students' benefit: "You see what the class is ready for next. No stack of papers to grade."

## 5. Culminating versions — conferences and meetings

The same evidence base — first answers, revised answers, per-aspect progressions, the year-arc rollup — can be compiled into versions for occasions where you need to speak about a student's development with someone else in the room:

- **Parent-teacher conferences** — the cycle reports plus the year-arc: here is where your student's thinking started in September, here is what it looks like now, in their own quoted words.
- **Student conferences** — the year-arc as the agenda for a one-on-one: which revisions held, which next steps got taken, what is worth chasing next.
- **Parent meetings** outside the conference calendar — the same artifacts, current to the most recent cycle.
- **IEP-related classroom meetings** — the reports as classroom evidence of conceptual development: what this student can do, per aspect, shown in their own written work over time.
- **Individual-progress discussions** with counselors, co-teachers, or administrators.

Two hard rules for every one of these versions:

- **Grounded only in observable classroom work and conceptual development.** Everything in these documents traces to something the student actually wrote or did — a quoted revised answer, a progression across named cycles, a completed revision. Nothing else.
- **No diagnoses. Ever.** These documents describe what a student's work shows about their developing understanding of biology. They never characterize ability, attention, processing, disability, or anything clinical. In an IEP context especially, the report is classroom evidence brought to the meeting — it is not an evaluation instrument, and it never speaks in evaluative or diagnostic language.

## 6. What to be careful about

Katherine's three cautions, and they are load-bearing — this section is why the human gate exists.

**First: the AI is confidently wrong in a particular and dangerous way.** "It can affirm reasoning that is actually mistaken, or invent a 'real part' of a student's answer that is not there, in fluent, encouraging prose. A book whose whole argument is that the deepest harm is misreading a student as a knower cannot pretend that an instrument which sometimes misreads — beautifully, persuasively — is harmless. That is exactly why I read everything before it goes out." The failure mode is not clumsy feedback you would catch at a glance; it is persuasive feedback that quotes a sentence the student never wrote, or praises a misconception warmly. Only someone who knows the student and the concept catches it. That is you, and that is the gate: "The reading is not quality control bolted on. It is the place the human judgment lives."

**Second: the defaults have to be overridden every single time.** Left alone, "the AI does the opposite of what I want. Its native register is to judge: right or wrong, here is the corrected version, moving on. Pointed at a partial answer, it calls the answer deficient. Pointed at a divergent answer... it calls it wrong." The register instructions in section 3 "are not set once; they have to be reasserted, and checked, against the grain of what the tool wants to do. The work of telling it where its pedagogical lines are is itself a pedagogical act, and it belongs to the teacher." Practically: the prompts in this document ride along in every run — in the deck's speaker notes for students, in your batch instructions for reports. Never assume a tool remembers them, and spot-check outputs against the register every batch, not just the first one.

**Third: the default configuration harms the students already being harmed.** "Most teachers using AI right now are not overriding the defaults. They are using it the way it ships: as a generator of correct outputs and a scorer of student performance against them. Used that way, AI does not relieve the problem... It accelerates it, with a friendly interface and the word 'personalized' attached. The students who were already misread will be misread faster, more confidently, and at greater scale." The tool in this system and the tool in the answer-scoring configuration are the same tool. "The pedagogy underneath it is the whole difference."

And the boundary that holds it all: "AI helps make the work doable. It is not the doer." What it does is production — drafts, reports, differentiated versions, IEP-accessible assessments. "None of that is teaching. Teaching is the shared object, the dialogic catch, the read on who is lurking and who is ready to be pushed, the moment a student's face changes when she finds out her thinking has weight. The AI does none of that, and it must not be allowed to, because that part is what students need from a person and from no one else."

One more of her caveats belongs in a document you bought: "I am not describing a product, and I am not recommending a particular tool... The principle is portable; the particular way I do it probably is not." This document gives you the prompts, the register, the report standards, and the gate. Which AI you run them through is your call, and every part of the system works with any capable general-purpose model.

## 7. The integrity gate, briefly

Authentic feedback requires authentic work, so the batch checks the first answer — and only the first answer — before drafting anything: a copied or AI-written first answer is flagged, and the flag holds that student's completion entry pending your review — nothing posts automatically. An AI-written first answer is auto-flagged for failure, confirmed by you: it fails the entry only after you confirm the flag. The revision is exempt by design (students are told to use their AI there). Flags stay visible to you and you decide — detection misfires, on ESL students and atypical writers especially, and because the gradebook is completion-only, a wrong flag costs a cycle's feedback, not a grade. The full policy, including what not to police, is in Assessment: Completion, Copying, and Proficiency.

## 8. The honest workload framing

What this system costs and buys, in Katherine's numbers, with her own hedge attached:

> "The production work — the slides, the differentiated versions, the feedback drafts, the IEP-accessible assessments — is roughly forty percent of what my job used to demand of my hours. When AI takes on that forty percent, the time comes back to me, and it goes into presence: standing at the counter, crouching beside a stuck student, running the dialogic register for more of the room. My rough sense is that this shows up as somewhere between a twenty and sixty percent increase in the time I actually spend present with students, depending on the week. I want to be plain that this is an estimate from my own practice — a feel, not a measurement. I offer it because the rough proportion is the point, not because the number is precise."

Note what the freed time is for. Not more production, and not a shorter workday: presence. The reading time the human gate costs you is real — a class set of reports is a read-every-word job — but it replaces a heavier job (composing thirty pieces of feedback from scratch) with a lighter one (checking thirty drafts against what you actually know about each student), and the unit descriptions' promise holds: no stack of papers to grade. If you find yourself tempted to skip the reading to save that time, reread section 6. The system without the gate is the default configuration, and the default configuration is the thing this system exists to refuse.
