## 1. The ecology arc: how Cycles 01–07 build

Every cycle in this arc runs two or three critical aspects through the same Variation Theory rhythm: first answer → contrast (CASE A / CASE B) → build a rule → pattern-break → three-tier concept question → Day 3 revision. What makes the seven cycles a single arc is that each cycle's critical aspects pick up exactly where the previous cycle left students hanging — several decks end with a "Keep going" question that is, deliberately, the next cycle's opening problem.

| Cycle | Deck | Critical aspects | What it hands the next cycle |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | Classifying Organisms | What makes a grouping useful; why DNA beats appearance | A way of grouping organisms — which Cycle 02 immediately replaces with a better one for ecology: group by how they feed, not what they are |
| 02 | Ecosystems & Feeding Relationships | Energy flow through trophic levels; symbiosis | The 10% loss, noticed but not yet explained — and the question "why hasn't life run out of energy?" |
| 03 | Energy Flow & Trophic Pyramids | Producers are the only entry point for energy; only ~10% passes up | Energy flows one way — but its Keep going asks where the atoms go, which is Cycle 04 |
| 04 | Carbon Cycle | Carbon moves from air into living things; matter cycles, energy does not | Its Keep going asks HOW a leaf grabs an invisible gas and locks it into wood — which is Cycle 05 |
| 05 | Photosynthesis | Light energy becomes chemical energy in glucose; a plant's material comes from air and water, not soil | Its Keep going asks how a living thing gets the energy back OUT of the sugar — which is Cycle 06 |
| 06 | Cellular Respiration & Fermentation | Releasing the energy in glucose slowly; oxygen and the ATP payoff | Its Keep going asks which living things would colonize a bare patch first — which is Cycle 07's succession question. Note: that payoff lives in the Populations & Succession EXTEND deck; CORE-only classes close the loop verbally on Day 1 of Cycle 07 |
| 07 | Population Ecology (core) · Populations & Succession (extend) | Exponential vs. logistic growth; carrying capacity and limiting factors; (extend) succession, pioneers, primary vs. secondary | Closes the arc: what limits populations is the energy and matter budget built in Cycles 02–06 |

The bridges are written into the decks verbatim. Cycle 03's Keep going: "the actual atoms in your food (like carbon) do not disappear when the energy is used up. Where do you think those atoms go next, and could the SAME atom end up in your food again someday?" Cycle 04's: "We keep saying a plant turns invisible carbon dioxide from the air into solid wood 'using photosynthesis.' But HOW?" Cycle 05's: "how do you think a living thing gets the energy back OUT of the sugar?" Cycle 06's: "If a bare patch of land suddenly opened up, which living things do you think would move in and start using that energy FIRST, and why?" Each is tagged in the deck "There's no answer to look up. Think it through and take your best guess" — students commit to a position at the end of one cycle and meet the answer at the start of the next. When you open a new cycle, it is worth thirty seconds to say so: "Last week you guessed at this. Now we find out."

Two structural notes for this arc:

- Cycles 02–06 are full v2 decks (teacher reference slide, day dividers, KEY TERMS boxes, word banks, Then and Now, Think → Write → Submit checklist). Cycles 01 and 07 are older-format decks with VT arcs inserted — they are teachable but missing v2 furniture, flagged in their sections below. REVIEW WITH KATHERINE (bring Cycles 01 and 07 to v2 standard).
- The conceptual through-line is two quantities, energy and matter, traced at ecosystem scale (02–04), then at cell scale (05–06), then back out to populations (07). The tank-and-flow models are the same visual metaphor cycle after cycle — tanks of stored energy or carbon, flows between them — so the models accumulate meaning as the arc proceeds.

### 2.3 Day 1 — the most teaching (Critical Aspect 1: energy flow)

Bellringer (slide 3), on the screen as students arrive:

> "Think about the last meal you ate. Where did the energy in that food originally come from? Write your best guess."

with the short sim beside it: "BioMan Ecology Games — remove one species from a food web. What happens?" The sim is the fiddle for this cycle — open on arrival, played during attendance, no output collected. The KEY TERMS box sits on the same slide: Autotroph — makes its own food · Heterotroph — must eat food · Producer / consumer / decomposer · Trophic level — a step in the chain · Energy pyramid.

Big idea (slide 4): "living things are connected by how energy and matter move between them," with the two questions the day is figuring out: "How do organisms get their energy — what separates the makers from the eaters?" and "Why does energy run out so fast as it moves up a food chain?"

Direct teach (slides 5–6): makers and eaters (autotroph/heterotroph, the consumer types), then "Zoom out from your plate" — organism → population → community → ecosystem → biome → biosphere, built from one cow. On delivering direct-instruction slides, Katherine's own method: "My slides carry a fair amount of text — to support students who were absent or are reviewing — but I scan each one and then talk freely. Reading the slides as written would be deadening. I highlight important words with my marker. I draw where it helps." An example of what that sounds like on slide 6, built from the slide's own ladder: start at the plate — one cow is an organism; every cow that breeds with it, a population; add everything those cows interact with — grass, flies, hawks — a community; now add what isn't alive, the soil and water and sun, and you have an ecosystem. Each rung adds one new thing. Circle the one new thing at each level and move on — the slide holds the definitions; your talk holds the logic.

First-answer question (slide 7) — students commit before any teaching on the aspect:

> "The energy in your dinner started as sunlight. Grass catches sunlight, a cow eats the grass, you eat the cow. At each step, do you get all of the grass's energy — or something else? Why?"

Every thinking slide in the deck carries the same instruction line: "Think first. Discuss with a partner. Then write." Do not go over the answer. Her protocol: "I ask for a thumbs up when most seem done. I do not go over the answer — that is critical. If I answer it, students just wait for me to."

Explore (slide 8): the Energy Tank Model — "Tap to open and drag the sliders — change the transfer efficiency and watch the tanks." Give it two or three minutes; the point is to feel the tanks shrink as energy climbs levels, not to produce anything.

The contrast (slide 9) — CASE A / CASE B, verbatim:

> Both live in the same meadow. So why thousands of grass plants but only a dozen hawks?
>
> CASE A — Grass (producers): Thousands of plants. Catch sunlight directly. ~10,000 calories stored across the field. First trophic level.
>
> CASE B — Red-tailed hawk: 10–15 hawks share the meadow. Can't make their own food. Eat rabbits, which eat grass. Third trophic level.
>
> If the grass holds 10,000 calories, why can't the meadow feed 10,000 hawks?

This is the pink-tower move at ecosystem scale: same meadow, same sunlight, same calories — the only thing varying is position in the chain, so position in the chain becomes the thing students can't help noticing.

Direct teach (slide 10): "Where does the other 90% go?" — only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level reaches the next; the rest doesn't vanish: most is lost as heat when organisms use energy to live (respiration), some leaves as waste that isn't eaten, some organisms die without being eaten. Teach it as three exits from the tank, not a list to copy.

Rule-building (slide 11):

> Finish this sentence as a rule: "Each time energy moves from one trophic level to the next, ______________________."

Pattern-break (slide 12), verbatim — the too-simple rule meets decomposers:

> "Your rule said energy passes upward. But decomposers eat dead things at every level — dead grass, dead rabbits, dead hawks. Where does a decomposer fit — 10% from one level, or energy from all levels at once? Does your rule still hold?"

Keep going (slide 13), the stretch for students ready for it:

> "If energy is lost at every step and never comes back, why hasn't life run out of energy? There's no answer to look up. Think it through and take your best guess."

Three-tier concept question (slide 14). Stem: "What has to be true about how organisms use energy for there to always be more producers than consumers?"

- Getting Started: "A meadow stores 10,000 calories in its grass. How many reach the hawks (secondary consumers)? Show your math."
- Working On It: "Why can there never be more foxes than rabbits in a stable ecosystem — even if foxes are faster, better hunters?"
- Mastery: "The 10% rule limits most ecosystems to 4–5 trophic levels. What would have to change for a 7-level ecosystem to be stable?"
- Word bank (use any, modify any, or use none): [ trophic level ] [ biomass ] [ producer ] [ consumer ] [ energy pyramid ] [ most energy is lost as heat during respiration ] [ only 10% passes to the next level ]

Students answer at the tier they are ready for, then write their first answer on the response slide (slide 15): "Write your first answer now. On Day 3, open this slide's Notes, run the prompt, and write your revised answer below — leave both showing. Seeing your thinking change is the point." First answers are graded for completion, not correctness — the standing rule is "anything that shows me your thinking is credit-worthy."

Day 1 activity (slide 16) — start the food web build, verbatim directions:

> 1. Take your set of species cards. Sort them into three piles: producers, consumers, decomposers.
> 2. Using each card's diet, draw arrows for who eats whom. Build a food web on your sheet.
> 3. Show your teacher your web before you leave.

The slide adds: "Attempt the Mastery response, then SUBMIT YOUR NOTES. Present today = due today." and — teacher advice written into the deck itself — "(We keep it simple this first cycle — the energy-pyramid math comes next class.)" This is a two-day build by design: students only sort and draw arrows on Day 1. The build is a noticing activity, not an output activity — circulate, talk over webs, crouch beside stuck groups. Arrows drawn "wrong" (pointing from eater to eaten) are something to think about with the group, not a failure to remediate.

### 2.4 Day 2 — finish, then the second idea (Critical Aspect 2: symbiosis)

Day divider (slide 17): "First: finish your food web from last class (the energy-pyramid step). Critical Aspect 2: how two species affect each other. Guided notes, then attempt the Mastery response. A short symbiosis activity, then submit your notes."

Finish the food web (slide 18) — the energy-pyramid step, verbatim:

> 1. Choose 4 organisms from your web and stack them into a pyramid — producers on the bottom.
> 2. Start with 10,000 units of energy in the producers. Using the 10% rule, how much reaches each level above? Fill it in.
> 3. In one sentence: why does the energy get smaller as you go up?

This is where Day 1's contrast becomes arithmetic in students' own hands: 10,000 → 1,000 → 100 → 10.

First-answer question for CA2 (slide 19):

> "A bee lands on a flower, drinks nectar, and carries pollen to the next flower. A mosquito lands on your arm, drinks your blood, and you itch for days. Both are two species interacting. So what is actually different?"

Direct teach (slide 20): mutualism — both species benefit; parasitism — one is helped, the other is harmed; commensalism — one benefits, the other is neither helped nor harmed. "The name for these close relationships is symbiosis."

The contrast (slide 21), verbatim:

> Both get what they need. What makes them different — and why does it matter?
>
> CASE A — Bee ↔ Flower: Bee gets nectar. Flower gets its pollen carried to other flowers. Both walk away with something. Neither is harmed.
>
> CASE B — Mosquito ↔ Human: Mosquito gets blood. Human gets an itchy welt and maybe disease. One gains; the other loses. The cost is real.

Rule-building (slide 22):

> Finish this sentence as a rule: "To identify what type of symbiosis two species have, you need to figure out ________________."

Pattern-break (slide 23), verbatim — the bee-mimic orchid:

> "Your rule said: figure out who benefits and who is harmed. But some orchids look and smell like female bees. A bee tries to mate with the flower, pollinates it — and gets no nectar at all. Who benefits? Who, if anyone, is harmed? A new type — or does your rule still work if you apply it carefully?"

This break is worth protecting time for. It rewards students who apply the rule carefully (the orchid benefits; the bee wastes time and energy — is that harm?) rather than reaching for a fourth category, which is exactly the discernment CA2 exists for.

Three-tier concept question (slide 24). Stem: "How does the outcome — who gains, who loses, or neither — decide the type of symbiosis?"

- Getting Started: "A pilot fish eats scraps near a shark; the shark doesn't notice. Name the type and each organism's outcome."
- Working On It: "Two species evolve traits that only make sense if the other exists. What type are they in, and what's your evidence?"
- Mastery: "A bee pollinates a flower (mutualism). If it evolved to steal nectar without touching pollen, what would the relationship become — and what happens to the flower over time?"
- Word bank (use any, modify any, or use none): [ mutualism ] [ parasitism ] [ commensalism ] [ symbiosis ] [ niche ] [ coevolution ] [ both gain ] [ one benefits, one is harmed ] [ one benefits, the other is unaffected ]

First answer goes on response slide 25.

Day 2 activity (slide 26) — the bobtail squid symbiosis investigation, observational, about 20 minutes, verbatim:

> 1. Open the Living Together resource. Look at the Hawaiian bobtail squid. Write: what do you notice? what do you wonder?
> 2. The squid holds glowing bacteria in a light organ. What does the squid get? What do the bacteria get?
> 3. Name the type of symbiosis, and write one sentence of evidence for why.

Notice/wonder first, classification last — students look before they label. The evidence sentence is the point: naming "mutualism" is floor-level; saying why (camouflage for the squid, home and food for the bacteria) is the discernment.

### 2.5 Day 3 — see your thinking change (revision and AI feedback)

Day divider (slide 27), verbatim: "Reopen this deck to your Concept Question response slides — your first answers are already there. On each, open the slide's Notes and copy the revision prompt you find in the Notes. Run it in the class AI. Read what it pushes you to reconsider. Write your revised answer in the 'Your revised answer' area, right under your first. Leave both showing — the change is the point."

Student steps (slide 28), verbatim:

> 1. Go to a Concept Question response slide. Your first answer is already there.
> 2. Open that slide's Notes (View ▸ Notes) and copy the AI prompt.
> 3. Run it in the class AI. Read what it asks you to reconsider.
> 4. Write your revised answer in the "Your revised answer" box, under your first one.
> 5. Do the same for your other Concept Question. Done = both answers filled, both visible.

The exact revision prompt, from the Notes of response slides 15 and 25 (identical on both):

> REVISION PROMPT — copy everything between the lines into your AI, and paste your first answer where it says [YOUR FIRST ANSWER]. The AI will NOT give you the answer — it helps you see your own thinking so you improve it yourself.
> -----
> I am a high school student. Below is a science question and my first answer. Do NOT rewrite my answer and do NOT give me the answer. Instead:
> 1. Tell me one thing I got right, and quote my own words back to me.
> 2. Ask me two questions that make me look again at one idea I might have wrong or left out.
> 3. Name one distinction or example worth thinking about.
> Keep it short and in plain language, then stop so I can write my own revised answer.
>
> QUESTION: [the slide's question]
> [YOUR FIRST ANSWER]:
> -----
> Now write your revised answer in "Your revised answer" on the slide — in your own words.

Canonical text lives in the deck's response-slide Notes and in AI Feedback and Conceptual Growth; if the wording ever differs, the deck Notes rule.

The same Notes carry the teacher line: "TEACHER: the finished deck (first + revised answers) is the single artifact that runs through the AI pipeline → each student's printable Growth Report + class summary. Completion = both boxes filled."

How Day 3 actually runs. Students who moved fast are revising within the first fifteen minutes; most of the block is students at different points in the same five steps, which is what frees you to circulate — reading first answers over shoulders, catching the student whose AI exchange has drifted, sending strong revisions to the What-if. The AI is human-gated at two points: the prompt itself forbids the AI to answer or rewrite (the automated integrity gate runs on FIRST answers only — an AI-written first answer is auto-flagged for failure, confirmed by you; a revised answer that reads like the AI's voice is a coaching moment, not a flag, because using the AI in the revision is the assignment — that is the conversation to have on the spot), and after decks are turned in, you run the finished decks through the pipeline and read every Conceptual Growth Report before it goes to a student. In Katherine's words: "I read every piece of it before it goes to a student, and I adjust it as needed. That is not a small caveat. It is the whole architecture." The batch workflow lives in the AI Feedback documentation, not here.

For students who finish revising, Day 3 has three layers, in order:

What if (slide 29), verbatim: "What if decomposers suddenly disappeared from every ecosystem on Earth — not just some, all of them? What would happen to energy flow within a year? Within a generation? Watch first: decomposers video." Prediction-under-perturbation — no answer to copy; students commit and defend from inside their model of the system.

Optional challenge (slide 30) — Break the Web, "for students who finished their draft and want to push further," verbatim:

> 1. Go back to the food web you built. Choose one species and remove it completely.
> 2. Trace every arrow that touched it. Which populations grow? Which shrink? Which disappear?
> 3. Predict the web one year later, then one generation later. Defend each prediction with the 10% rule or a symbiosis type.
> 4. Hardest version: is there any single species whose removal would barely change the web? Explain what makes a species replaceable.

Then and Now (slide 31): "Rate yourself: 'I can trace how energy and matter move through an ecosystem, and why energy runs out.' Before this lesson: Getting Started / Working On It / Mastery. Right now: …"

Think → Write → Submit checklist (slide 32): both response slides have my first answer · I ran the Notes prompt and wrote a revised answer under each · both answers are visible · I turned the deck in to the folder. And the closing line students see in every cycle: "Completion = both answers present. Your growth shows in your printed Growth Report, not the grade."

## 3. Cycle 01 — Classifying Organisms (compact guide)

The opening deck of the arc. No TPT unit description exists for it and its deck lacks the v2 teacher reference — placement as Cycle 01 and its absence from the TPT listings are flagged REVIEW WITH KATHERINE. It is a pre-v2 deck with VT inserts: no essential claim, no day dividers for Days 1–2, no KEY TERMS boxes, no Keep going.

Focus questions (deck slide 3): "What makes one way of grouping organisms better than another?" and "Why does DNA give us more accurate groupings than what an organism looks like?"

Question arc:
- Bellringer: "You meet a creature you've never seen before. What 3 things would you most want to know about it to figure out what it is and how it lives?" plus the OneZoom fiddle: "Open & explore onezoom.org — Find a pair that look almost the same but are far apart on the tree. Find a pair that look very different but are close cousins."
- Arc 1 (what makes a grouping useful): first answer — "Why bother grouping organisms at all?…" → contrast: Way A, group by where they live (ocean: shark, whale, dolphin, tuna) vs. Way B, group by how they're related (mammals vs. fish) — "If you knew 'mammal,' what could you predict about a new one? What about 'ocean group'?" → rule: "A grouping is useful when ____." → pattern-break: bats and birds both fly, but DNA puts bats with mice and humans — "Why is the 'flying animals' group not actually a useful one?" → three-tier with word bank.
- Arc 2 (DNA beats appearance): first answer — "What's the problem with grouping by what animals LOOK like?" → contrast: streamlined sea creatures (shark, tuna, dolphin) vs. DNA groups (dolphin with cows) → rule: "DNA gives us better groupings than appearance because ____." → pattern-break: shark / dolphin / ichthyosaur, three DNA groups, near-identical bodies — "Why does the 'looks like' rule fail here?" → three-tier with word bank.
- What-if (closing): an organism whose DNA matches none of the three domains — fourth domain, or is the tree wrong?

Activities and links:
